## 1. Physical security as part of the security engineer's job (Ch. 13)
- `[from source]` Reasons for the infosec specialist to know physical security: risk management, physical-logical interaction, giving an opinion on facilities, infosec mechanisms are defeated with physical access, mechanical locks compromised by bumping and electronic ones weak (Mifare classic) (SE3 p.511). **Alarms teach availability**: defeating an alarm = making it stop or look unreliable; historically ~90% of security research was about confidentiality, 9% about authenticity and 1% about availability, but real spending and attacks are the opposite (SE3 p.512).
- **Policy: Deter – detect – alarm – delay – respond**; embed operational security in the culture; unify physical, business and information security (US$10m in the vault and a false delivery order in the system) (SE3 p.512-513).
- **Threat model:** "security is not scalar"; classification of attackers (Derek, Charlie, Bruno, Abdurrahman); defenses of banks and computer rooms are designed for Bruno; "it is not prudent to design a nuclear plant assuming Charlie" (SE3 p.513-514).
- **Deterrence:** anonymity/inconspicuousness; invisible alarms reduce crime in the neighborhood, visible ones redistribute it (Lojack: US$100/year to the driver, social benefit US$1,500); CPTED (strong lighting creates glare; chain-link fences communicate absence of control; CCTV deters only where there is a single exit); broken windows; situational prevention (SE3 p.514-516).
- **Alarm communications:** cable cuts (Hackensack: US$2.1m in watches; Tulsa: 50,000 homes) — "bulk DoS saturates response capacity"; make alarm communications **anonymous** to prevent targeted DoS; two independent paths (London insurance, >£20m); datacenter: two fibers and two providers, but "the simplest way to take down a datacenter is to cut its communications" (SE3 p.532-533).
- **Lessons** (SE3 p.533-534): most locks can be defeated, so alarms matter; **dealing with DoS is the hardest part of many designs**; look at the whole system; "the outermost perimeter is the one you most want to trust and can trust least"; the missed alarm × false alarm trade-off (ROC) is ubiquitous; guards become inattentive when almost everything is a false alarm — TSA: ~20% of threats get through if tested several times per shift, 60-75% if tested once; failure to understand the threat model (designing for Charlie hoping to stop Bruno); do not leave the technical side to subcontractors alone.

## 2. Metering and monitoring (Ch. 14)
- `[from source]` Metering = trust in devices in the hands of the opponent; subjects cause false readings or failure; evidence matters (SE3 p.537). Prepayment tokens: prevent forgery at scale; "most people don't cheat unless someone industrializes it" (SE3 p.538).
- **STS (prepayment meters):** 68 million meters in 98 countries; token encrypted with the meter's unique key; "ghost vendors"; credit counter in the vending machine's chip (SE3 p.538-541). **What went wrong:** lightning; brown-out gave maximum credit; minimum tariff; refund without invalidation; only remembered the last serial → ABAB... (SE3 p.541-542). **Lessons:** control the sales channel; security infrastructure is expensive to change; recycle technology; use several specialists; prolonged field testing; designing to eliminate scalable fraud = centralization; the date counter rolls over in 2024: "think 100 years, not 30" (SE3 p.542-543) `[DATED]`.
- **Smart meters:** privacy (consumption per second), remote switch as a cyberwar threat, divergent incentives; Italy/Spain vs UK (£20bn projected, 12.5 million installed and 39 million still to install at the end of 2018) and Ontario (SE3 p.543-546) `[DATED]`.
- **Postal meters:** from signature to MAC (verification is expensive and unnecessary; two-digit MACs detect systematic abuse); "preventing printing more than once" (Stampit) vs "preventing *using* more than once" (UK, grey-list) (SE3 p.564-565).
- **Summary:** monitoring protection is tied to dependability and DoS; keys in cheap, distributed devices; mutual suspicion (SE3 p.565-566).

## 3. Nuclear command (Ch. 15)
`[from source]` Spin-offs: optical fiber around warheads, passwords of >12 digits unusable in the field, iris (SE3 p.568); treaty verification with digital signatures and *cut-and-choose* protocols (SE3 p.579); Petrov 1983 and the risks of cyberattacks on ISR; 15 of 44 countries with a civil program have no cybersecurity laws (SE3 p.580-581); **subliminal channels** (in DSA the signer chooses k to hide bits in r; remedy: deterministic signature) (SE3 p.581-582); **modern Kerckhoffs:** public review is worth more than design secrecy, keeping implementation details such as the location of the response force secret (SE3 p.582-583).

## 4. Seals and security printing (Ch. 16)
- `[from source]` Seals have limits: the owner may be the villain; inspection is harder than it looks (grey market; samples by agents) (SE3 p.599). **Gundecking** (inspectors who don't inspect): seals that display a new code every minute and erase the key if violated ("anti-evidence") (SE3 p.600). Seals break for innocent reasons (SE3 p.601); control of materials (SE3 p.601-602); protect the right thing and define the threat (counterfeiting? alteration? dilution?) (SE3 p.602-603).
- **Trujillo experiment (2006):** 71 tamper-detection experts were no better than chance at identifying 3 of 10 tampered products (SE3 p.603).
- **Seal evaluation methodology (the whole life cycle):** who must notice the forgery and with what false acceptance/rejection rates; has anyone competent tried to defeat it? what is "defeat"? who can buy/forge the materials? can the applicator be careless or corrupt? does the seal protect the right party? quality (dirt, oil, cleanliness)? in court, are there experts beyond yours? disposal (SE3 p.604-605). "Defeating seals is deceiving people, not beating hardware" (SE3 p.605) — the book notes that this process is "a microcosm of the assurance process for systems in general" (SE3 p.605).
