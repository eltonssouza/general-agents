## 1. Why move away from least squares
- `[from source]` Least squares has low bias if the relationship is approximately linear, and low variance only if n ≫ p; with n not much larger than p there is overfitting and, if p > n, there is no unique solution (infinite variance) (ISLR p.218-219). Shrinking or constraining coefficients can greatly reduce the variance at the cost of a negligible bias (ISLR p.219). There is also a gain in interpretability by zeroing coefficients (ISLR p.219). Three families: subset selection, shrinkage (regularization) and dimension reduction (ISLR p.219). ESL: the same two reasons (accuracy and interpretation) (ESL p.76).

## 2. Subset selection
- `[from source]` **Best subset** (Algorithm 6.1): fit the C(p, k) models of each size, choose the one with the lowest RSS (or highest R²) for each k and then choose among M₀…M_p by CV, Cp (AIC), BIC or adjusted R²; training RSS and R² **never** serve to choose among sizes; for logistic regression the *deviance* = −2 × maximum log-likelihood is used (ISLR p.220-221, 225). There are 2^p models: with p = 20, more than a million; infeasible above ~40; *branch-and-bound* only works for least squares (ISLR p.221-222). ESL: the *leaps and bounds* algorithm makes p of 30 to 40 feasible (ESL p.76).
- `[from source]` Huge search spaces favor overfitting and high variance of the coefficients (ISLR p.222). **Forward stepwise** (Alg. 6.2): fits 1 + p(p + 1)/2 models (p = 20: 211 against 1,048,576), does not guarantee the best (Table 6.1 shows *cards* entering the 4-variable best subset but not the forward one); works with n < p only up to M_{n−1}; **backward** (Alg. 6.3) requires n > p; hybrids add and remove (ISLR p.222-225). ESL: forward is greedy, may be preferred for computational (p ≫ N) and statistical (lower variance) reasons; backward removes the variable with the smallest Z-score; in the simulation with N = 300 and p = 31, best subset, forward and backward perform very similarly (ESL p.77-78).
- `[from source]` ESL, cautions: selection by F statistics is "out of fashion" for ignoring multiple testing; **the standard errors of the model chosen after the search are not valid** because they do not account for the search process (the bootstrap can help); grouped variables (dummies) must enter and leave together (ESL p.79). *Forward stagewise* is even more restricted, slow (more than 1000 steps in the example), but useful in high dimension (ESL p.79).

## 3. Cp, AIC, BIC and adjusted R²: **the definitions in ISLR and ESL differ in scale**
- `[from source]` ISLR (least-squares model with d predictors, σ̂² estimated): **Cp = (1/n)(RSS + 2dσ̂²)** (ISLR p.226); **AIC = (1/(nσ̂²))(RSS + 2dσ̂²)** omitting an additive constant, so Cp and AIC are proportional (ISLR p.227); **BIC = (1/(nσ̂²))(RSS + log(n)·d·σ̂²)** (ISLR p.227, eq. 6.3); adjusted R² = 1 − [RSS/(n − d − 1)]/[TSS/(n − 1)], which is **maximized** (the others are minimized) (ISLR p.227). Checked visually on the rendered page (in this 2017 printing AIC and BIC carry the factor 1/(nσ̂²); `[extrapolation]` other printings or editions may write the factor differently: check yours).
- `[from source]` log n > 2 for n > 7, so BIC penalizes more and chooses smaller models than Cp (in Credit: BIC chooses 4 predictors, Cp 6, adjusted R² 7) (ISLR p.226-228). Cp, AIC and BIC have asymptotic justification; adjusted R² is less well founded (ISLR p.228). If σ̂² is an unbiased estimator of σ², Cp is unbiased for the test MSE (ISLR p.226).
- `[from source]` ESL: optimism ω = (2/N) Σ Cov(ŷᵢ, yᵢ), which for a linear fit with d parameters equals 2(d/N)σ²ε; hence **Cp = err + 2(d/N)σ̂²ε** (eq. 7.26) and **AIC(α) = err(α) + 2(d(α)/N)σ̂²ε** (eq. 7.30), equivalent in the Gaussian case; for log-likelihood, AIC = −(2/N)·loglik + 2d/N (7.29) (ESL p.248-250). **BIC = −2·loglik + (log N)·d** (7.35), and in the Gaussian case BIC = (N/σ²ε)[err + (log N)(d/N)σ²ε] (7.36); BIC is proportional to AIC with 2 → log N, and applies only to log-likelihood (not to the classification error rate) (ESL p.252-253). Checked visually.
- `[inference]` The scales differ (ISLR divides by nσ̂²; ESL expresses it in terms of err = RSS/N), but the same models are ordered the same way as long as σ̂² and n are fixed; when comparing numbers across sources, do not mix the scales (`slr_calc.py criteria` shows both).
- `[from source]` ESL: if the basis is chosen adaptively (best model of d among p variables), the optimism exceeds 2dσ²ε/N (ESL p.250). Effective df: ŷ = Sy, df(S) = trace(S) (ESL p.251-252), df = Σ Cov(ŷᵢ, yᵢ)/σ²ε (ESL p.252); for networks with *weight decay*, df(α) = Σ θ_m/(θ_m + α) (ESL p.252).
- `[from source]` **AIC × BIC**: BIC comes from the Bayesian approach (Laplace approximation; posterior probability of the model ∝ e^{−BIC/2}); it is consistent (the probability of choosing the true model tends to 1), whereas AIC tends to choose models that are too complex as N → ∞; in finite samples BIC can choose models that are too simple; "there is no clear choice" (ESL p.253-254). MDL is formally identical to BIC (ESL p.254-256). VC/SRM: loose but useful bounds for relative selection; hard to compute (ESL p.256-258).
- `[from source]` Alternative: validation/CV estimates the test error directly, with fewer assumptions, and holds when it is hard to count the df or estimate σ²; today the computational cost of CV is almost never a problem (ISLR p.228). One-standard-error rule (ISLR p.229). Cp/AIC/BIC and adjusted R² **are not appropriate with p > n** (the estimated σ̂² becomes 0) (ISLR p.256).

## 4. Ridge and lasso
- `[from source]` **Ridge**: minimizes RSS + λΣβ²ⱼ (λ ≥ 0, tuning parameter); λ = 0 recovers least squares and λ → ∞ drives the coefficients to zero; the intercept is not penalized; centering the variables gives β̂₀ = ȳ (ISLR p.230). Least squares is scale-equivariant, ridge is **not**: standardize first, x̃ = x/√((1/n)Σ(x − x̄)²) (ISLR p.232). Ridge reduces variance at the expense of bias: in the example (p = 45, n = 50) the minimum MSE occurs near λ = 30 and ridge works better when least squares has high variance; with p > n ridge still works (ISLR p.232-233). The path for all λ costs almost the same as one least-squares fit (ISLR p.234).
- `[from source]` ESL ridge: β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy; makes the problem nonsingular even if XᵀX does not have full rank; coefficients of correlated variables are unstable and ridge alleviates this (ESL p.82-83). Via SVD, ridge shrinks the coordinates of y in the basis U by the factor d²ⱼ/(d²ⱼ + λ): it **shrinks the low-variance directions more** (ESL p.85-86), df(λ) = Σ d²ⱼ/(d²ⱼ + λ) (ESL p.87), and implicitly assumes that the response varies more in the directions of highest predictor variance (ESL p.86). Ridge as a posterior: with a prior N(0, τ²) on the β and λ = σ²/τ², it is the posterior mode and mean (ESL p.83).
- `[from source]` **Lasso**: minimizes RSS + λΣ|βⱼ| (ℓ₁ penalty); forces some coefficients to exactly zero (sparse models, variable selection), whereas ridge never zeroes (ISLR p.234). Constrained form: Σ|βⱼ| ≤ s (lasso) or Σβ²ⱼ ≤ s (ridge); best subset corresponds to Σ I(βⱼ ≠ 0) ≤ s, intractable; lasso zeroes coefficients because the region is a diamond with corners (ISLR p.235-238). ESL: lasso path at the cost of ridge; small t zeroes; s = t/Σ|β̂ⱼ|; on prostate s ≈ 0.36 zeroed 4 coefficients (ESL p.87-88).
- `[from source]` **Comparison**: neither dominates; lasso tends to be better if few predictors have large coefficients and the rest ~0; ridge, if the response depends on many predictors of similar size; use CV to decide (ISLR p.238-239). In the orthonormal case (X = I): ridge β̂ = y/(1 + λ), proportional shrinkage; lasso by *soft-thresholding*, y ∓ λ/2 and 0 if |y| ≤ λ/2 (ISLR p.240); ESL Table 3.4: ridge β̂/(1 + λ), lasso sign(β̂)(|β̂| − λ)₊, best subset by hard thresholding (ESL p.89-90). **Beware of λ scales**: the ISLR lasso (6.13) has threshold λ/2, the ESL one (3.52) uses ½RSS and threshold λ.
- `[from source]` Bayesian view: Gaussian prior → ridge (mode and mean); Laplace (double-exponential) prior → lasso (mode; the posterior mean is **not** sparse) (ISLR p.241-242; ESL p.91). ESL generalizes with Σ|βⱼ|^q: q = 0 is subset, q = 1 lasso, q = 2 ridge; lasso is the smallest q with a convex region; estimating q "is not worth the effort" (ESL p.91); **elastic net** (Zou and Hastie) mixes the penalties, selects like lasso and groups correlated predictors like ridge (ESL p.92).
- `[from source]` **Choosing λ**: grid of values, CV for each one, choose the one with the lowest error, refit with all the data (ISLR p.242). In Credit (LOOCV) the chosen λ is small and the minimum is not sharp; with lasso and 10-fold, the n = 50, p = 45 example recovers the two signal variables (ISLR p.242-243). ESL: LAR (*least angle regression*) is a "democratic" version of forward stepwise, closely tied to the lasso and efficient for computing the lasso path; Algorithm 3.2 moves the coefficients of the active variables while keeping their correlations with the residual tied and decreasing (ESL p.92-93); the details of the modification for the lasso are on pages not read.

## 5. Dimension reduction: PCR and PLS
- `[from source]` Z_m = Σ φ_jm X_j, M < p; fit by least squares on Z; β_j = Σ θ_m φ_jm (ISLR p.244-245); M = p is equivalent to least squares (ISLR p.245). **PCR** uses the first M principal components; assumes that the directions of greatest variation of X are those associated with Y; standardize the predictors; M by CV; it is not variable selection and approaches ridge ("ridge is a continuous version of PCR") (ISLR p.248-251). **PLS** is supervised: φ_j1 is the coefficient of the simple regression of Y on X_j (∝ correlation); following directions via residuals; M by CV; in practice it rarely beats ridge or PCR (the gain in bias is offset in variance) (ISLR p.252-253).
- `[from source]` ESL: PCR discards the p − M smallest components whereas ridge shrinks them (ESL p.98-99); PLS maximizes Corr²(y, Xα)·Var(Xα), whereas PCR only Var(Xα), and the variance aspect tends to dominate (ESL p.99-100). **Comparison** (Frank and Friedman, 1993): to minimize prediction error ridge is usually preferable to subset, PCR and PLS, although the improvement over the last two is small; PLS can inflate high-variance directions (unstable); lasso falls between ridge and best subset (ESL p.101).

## Pitfalls
- Not standardizing before ridge, lasso, PCR or PLS (ISLR p.232, 251).
- Choosing the subset size by RSS or R² (ISLR p.220).
- Presenting standard errors and p-values of the final model after a search (ESL p.79).
- Comparing numerical values of AIC/BIC across sources with different scales (see §3).
