# On-call, troubleshooting, emergency response and incident management (Ch. 11-14, App. C)

Pages = PDF (printed = N−12).

## On-call (Ch. 11)
- At most 50% on purely operational work; of the rest, at most 25% on-call. [from source] (SRE p.99-100)
- Agreed response times: typically 5 min for user-facing or very sensitive services, 30 min for less sensitive ones. [from source] (SRE p.99)
- 99.99% in a quarter allows about 13 min of downtime, hence the on-call reaction in minutes. [from source] (SRE p.99-100)
- Minimum of 8 engineers at a single site (2 people on-call, one-week shifts); in dual-site, 6 per site. Preference for multi-site (follow the sun). [from source] (SRE p.100)
- Quality: on average 6 hours to handle an incident (analysis, remediation, follow-ups), hence a maximum of 2 incidents per 12 h shift; incident = events/alerts with the same root cause that would go into the same postmortem. [from source] (SRE p.100-101)
- Compensation: time-off-in-lieu or money with a cap. [from source] (SRE p.101) [DATED]
- Feeling safe: stress impairs cognition (e.g., confirmation bias, cited as an illustrative example of heuristics under stress); three resources: clear escalation paths, well-defined incident management procedures, a blameless postmortem culture. [from source] (SRE p.101) The book does not turn this bias into a rule for investigating an external cause; using the example that way is [inference].
- **App. B (p.331), limit per shift** [from source]: expect at most two events per on-call shift (for example, per 12 hours), because responding, fixing the outage, starting the postmortem and filing the bugs takes time; more events can degrade the quality of the response and suggest a problem in system design, monitoring sensitivity or the response to postmortem bugs (the same argument appears in the 6 h per incident arithmetic on p.101; more detail in gestao-treinamento-interrupts-e-engajamento.md). (SRE p.331; p.101)
- Operational overload: measurable goals (e.g., daily tickets < 5; paging events per shift < 2); a common cause is badly configured monitoring; "give back the pager" in extreme cases. [from source] (SRE p.102-103)
- Underload: system too quiet; size so that each engineer is on-call once or twice a quarter; "Wheel of Misfortune" exercises and the annual disaster recovery event DiRT (Disaster Recovery Training: theoretical and practical drills, multi-day tests) help keep skills practiced. P.331 (App. B) only brings the irony that good practices leave the team out of practice and asks for practicing hypothetical outages. [from source] (SRE p.103)

## Troubleshooting (Ch. 12)
- Hypothetico-deductive method: report → telemetry/logs → hypotheses → test (compare or "treat" the system) → cause → fix and postmortem. [from source] (SRE p.104)
- Steps: Problem Report, Triage, Examine, Diagnose, Test and Treat, Cure. [from source] (SRE p.106-111)
- Pitfalls: irrelevant symptoms, not knowing how to change the system safely, improbable or past-bound theories, spurious correlations ("horses, not zebras"). Correlation is not causation: the book's example is **packet loss inside a cluster and hard drives failing in the cluster**, correlated events that have a **common cause, a power outage**, even though a network failure obviously does not cause disk failure or vice versa; and, the larger the system and the more metrics, it is inevitable that some events correlate by pure coincidence. [from source] (SRE p.106)
- Triage: respond proportionally to impact; in a severe outage the priority is to stop the bleeding (divert traffic, drop load, shut down subsystems) while preserving evidence; "fly the airplane" analogy. [from source] (SRE p.106-107)
- Examine: metrics, logging (adjustable verbosity levels, sampling), tracing (Dapper), current-state endpoints. [from source] (SRE p.107)
- Diagnose: simplify and reduce; divide and conquer/bisection; ask "what/where/why"; "what touched it last" (recent changes). [from source] (SRE p.108-109)
- Test and Treat: tests with mutually exclusive alternatives, obvious first, attention to confounding factors and side effects; record everything. [from source] (SRE p.110)
- Negative results are magic: negative results are conclusive and should be published. [from source] (SRE p.110-111)
- App Engine case study: mitigated with resources before finding the cause (whitelist created on each access to a path, triggered by a security scanner); notes 75 and 76 are on p.115. [from source] (SRE p.112-115)
- Making troubleshooting easier: white-box observability and structured logs from the start, request ID, changes recorded. [from source] (SRE p.114)

## Emergency response (Ch. 13)
- Three cases: Test-Induced (a test that blocked access to a DB and exposed dependencies), Change-Induced (a config push that caused a global crash loop and was rolled back in ~5 min), Process-Induced (turndown automation of small installations that sent to Diskerase the machines of all installations in turndown globally; the large installations were not affected, p.119). [from source] (SRE p.116-120)
- Lessons, **one per case**: rollback must be tested before large-scale tests (Test-Induced, p.117); full canary "regardless of perceived risk" and the **luck** that the engineer who pushed was following real-time channels (Change-Induced, p.118); automation without sanity checks ("empty filter = all") is dangerous (Process-Induced, p.119-120). Details in the next section. [from source] (SRE p.117-120)
- General: every problem has a solution; keep a history of outages; ask improbable questions ("what if"); encourage proactive testing; do not panic. [from source] (SRE p.116, 120-121)

## The three Ch. 13 cases in detail (each lesson belongs to one case) [from source]
**Test-Induced (SRE p.116-117): the lesson is the tested rollback.**
- The test aimed to expose hidden dependencies of a test database inside a large distributed MySQL, by **blocking access to only 1 of 100 databases**. Within minutes, dependent services reported that internal and external users could not access key systems (some intermittently). SRE aborted the test, but **the rollback of the permissions change failed**; they restored permissions on the replicas and failovers through an already-tested path, and in parallel sought out the developers to fix a defect in the application-layer library. **Within 1 hour** access was fully restored.
- What went well: affected services escalated immediately; the test was aborted without hesitation; some teams reconfigured their systems to avoid the test database. What was learned: the test was reviewed and "well bounded", but the interaction between dependent systems was not understood; **they did not follow the incident response process, which had existed for only a few weeks and had not been well publicized**; **since the rollback procedures had not been tested in a test environment, they were defective and lengthened the outage**; they began to require testing of rollback procedures before large-scale tests and periodic retests.

**Change-Induced (SRE p.117-119): the lesson is the full canary and what was luck.**
- A configuration change to the infrastructure that protects services against abuse was pushed **globally on a Friday**; it triggered a crash-loop bug in essentially all externally facing systems, and the internal systems that depend on them also went down.
- Response: within seconds alerts pointed to sites being down; some on-calls thought the corporate network had failed and went to secure rooms ("panic rooms") with backup access to production; **within 5 minutes** the engineer who made the push, aware of the corporate outage but not of the general one, pushed a new change that **reverted the first** and services began to recover; **within 10 minutes** the on-calls declared the incident; some services suffered unrelated bugs and incorrect configurations triggered by the event and **took up to 1 hour** to recover fully.
- What went well: monitoring detected it almost immediately (but it was bad: alerts firing nonstop, overloading the on-calls and flooding the regular and emergency channels); incident management was good, with clear updates; out-of-band communication systems and command-line tools and alternate access worked (they need more familiarity and testing); and **the affected system rate-limited the delivery of full updates to new clients, which may have slowed the crash loop and left jobs up long enough to serve some requests between crashes**.
- **The luck** (the book's text: "we should not ignore the element of luck"): **the engineer who pushed happened to be following real-time communication channels**, a diligence that is not part of the normal release process, saw the flood of corporate-access complaints right after the push and reverted almost immediately; without that quick reversion, the outage could have lasted "considerably longer" and been much harder to debug, because the troubleshooting and communication tools were behind jobs in crash loop.
- What was learned: an earlier push of the new functionality had gone through a **rigorous canary that did not exercise a very rare configuration keyword in combination with the functionality**; the change that caused the problem **was not considered risky and followed a less rigorous canary**; when pushing globally the untested combination was used. Canary and automation improvements were planned for the following quarter and **moved up in priority**: full canary "regardless of perceived risk".
- Be careful when answering "what was luck": the book **names** the luck (the engineer following real-time channels) and does **not** attribute the success to "rollback tested beforehand", the lesson of the Test-Induced case.

**Process-Induced (SRE p.119-120): the lesson is validating the filter output (empty set).**
- In a routine test of the automation, **two consecutive turndown requests** were submitted for the same installation to be decommissioned; on the second, a subtle bug sent **all machines of all small installations, worldwide, to the Diskerase queue**. The on-call was paged when the first small installation went offline, **drained the traffic** (the machines had already been wiped and did not respond), but soon pagers from all installations were firing; they shut down all of the team's automation and froze the other automations and maintenance; **within 1 hour all traffic was at other locations** (users perhaps saw higher latency). Recovery was the hard part: congested network links required workarounds; **the first installation was rebuilt within 3 hours**; they moved to a manual three-part reinstallation plan (one step each) and **within 3 days most of the capacity was back**, with the stragglers recovered in a month or two.
- Well: the reverse proxies of the large installations are managed differently and **the large ones were not affected** (they handle full load); the turndown of many installations took **less than 1 hour**; they reverted the monitoring cleanup; the incident protocols had matured greatly since the first outage of the chapter.
- Cause: the turndown server **had no sanity checks**; when run again and receiving an empty response for the rack, it **passed the empty filter** to the machine database, which ordered all of them wiped ("sometimes zero means everything"). Slow and unreliable reinstallation by **TFTP at the lowest QoS** from distant locations (BIOS hung or went into continuous reboot, depending on the network card), a regression that limited to **at most two setup tasks per worker**, poorly tuned QoS and timeouts, and forced kernel reinstallation; the solution was to reclassify installation traffic with slightly higher priority and restart machines stuck by automation. (SRE p.119-120)
- General points of the chapter: "every problem has a solution"; if overloaded, call in more people (sometimes the whole company); "the one with the most state is often the one who triggered the event"; keep a history of outages and chase the follow-up of postmortems; ask the "what if" (building without power, racks in water, primary datacenter dark, compromised web server); test proactively with the best people nearby (not at 2 a.m. on Saturday). (SRE p.116, 120-121)

## Troubleshooting and on-call: numbers that were missing (Ch. 11-12) [from source]
- On-call: with 99.99% in the quarter the allowed downtime is ~13 minutes [derived: 0.0001 × 90 days × 1,440 min ≈ 13 min, the book says "around 13 minutes"], hence the reaction is "on the order of minutes"; incident = related events that fit in the same postmortem; **an incident consumes on average 6 hours**, which gives at most 2 per 12 h shift; tune toward a **1:1 alert/incident ratio**; example goals for overload: daily tickets < 5 and page events per shift < 2. (SRE p.99-102)
- Troubleshooting: logging with adjustable verbosity levels and sampling (e.g., record 1 in every 1,000 operations), request tracing through the stack with Dapper; Shakespeare probe example that runs every minute and, in a 10-minute window, about half of the probes work (indicates partial failure and not total); in the App Engine case, the extra latency came from a gap of ~250 ms between the start of work on a request and the first RPCs. (SRE p.107-113)

## Managing incidents (Ch. 14)
- Three risks: narrow technical focus, poor communication, freelancing. [from source] (SRE p.122-123)
- Based on the Incident Command System; four roles: Incident Command, Operational Work (Ops), Communication, Planning. Only the operations team modifies the system during the incident. [from source] (SRE p.123)
- Elements: recursive separation of responsibilities, recognized command post, **live incident state document**, **clear, live handoff** ("You're now the incident commander, okay?" with confirmation). [from source] (SRE p.123-124)
- When to declare an incident (any one true): needs a second team; customer-visible outage; no solution after an hour of focused analysis. [from source] (SRE p.125)
- Best practices: Prioritize, Prepare, Trust, Introspect, Consider alternatives, Practice, Change it around. [from source] (SRE p.125)

## Round 4 sweep: Ch. 11, 12 and 14 in more detail and notes [from source]
**On-call (Ch. 11).**
- The distribution of roles between primary and secondary varies: one team uses the secondary as a **fall-through** for pages the primary misses; another leaves the primary with only pages and the secondary with the rest of the non-urgent activities. When they do not need an exclusive secondary rotation, **two related teams can serve as each other's secondary**. Pages take priority over almost everything, including projects; non-page events (lower-priority alerts, releases) can be handled during business hours. Google delivers pages by multiple means (email, SMS, robot call, app) on several devices. (SRE p.99-100)
- On-call quality: the distribution of page events should be **very flat, with a likely median of 0**; if a component pages every day (median incidents/day > 1) it is likely that something else will break and blow the limit; if the limit is temporarily exceeded (for example in a quarter), corrective measures should return the load to a sustainable state. Multi-site brings communication and coordination cost: the single/multi-site decision weighs trade-offs, the importance of the system and the load. Night shifts harm health; limiting the size of the rotation avoids losing touch with production. (SRE p.100-101)
- Feeling safe: two ways of thinking (intuitive and fast versus rational and deliberate); under stress (cortisol, CRH) the second tends to be swallowed by the first and the on-call abuses heuristics, e.g., **confirmation bias** when the same alert pages for the fourth time in the week and the three previous ones came from an external infrastructure system (this is the book's **illustrative example** of how stress leads to automatically associating the fourth occurrence with the previous cause; it is **not** the book's recommendation on how to handle an external cause: the book gives no specific guidance for that case); intuition may be wrong and habitual quick reactions can be disastrous. The developers of the teams SRE supports also participate in a 24/7 rotation, and one can always escalate to them. When the incident is complex enough to involve several teams, or an upper bound on its duration cannot be estimated, it is advisable to adopt the formal incident management protocol, supported at Google by a **web tool** that automates handover of roles and status recording/communication (so as not to spend effort formatting emails). (SRE p.101-102)
- Overload: page alerts should be aligned with symptoms that threaten the SLO and be **actionable**; low-priority alerts that bother every hour create fatigue and make serious alerts get less attention; the **alert fan-out per incident** should be regulated by grouping related alerts and silencing duplicate or uninformative ones; noisy alerts should be tuned toward a 1:1 ratio. If the change causing the overload comes from the developers, set common goals with them; as a last resort, "give back the pager" (the development team is exclusively on call until the system reaches the standard), which is rare; temporary alternative: renegotiate to route some or all pages to the developers' on-call until the service can be taken back. (SRE p.102-103)

**Troubleshooting (Ch. 12).**
- Effective problem report: **expected behavior, actual behavior and, if possible, how to reproduce**; consistent form and searchable storage (bug system); teams have forms or small web apps that generate and route the bug automatically and may offer self-diagnosis. It is practice to open a **bug for each problem**, even one received by email or chat; many teams **discourage reporting directly to a person** (requires transcribing, produces worse reports invisible to the team, concentrates the load on whoever the reporter knows rather than on the on-call). (SRE p.106)
- Shakespeare example: the black-box prober looks for "the forms of things unknown" and the alert already opens a bug with links to the results and the playbook; the prober does a GET on `/api/search` and expects HTTP 200 with an exact JSON; it probes every minute and, in 10 minutes, about half succeeded with no pattern; the prober does not show what came back in the failures (note it to fix); with `curl` one gets a 502 with no payload, but with the `X-Request-Trace` header listing the backends involved, which rules out the frontend and the load balancers as the source. (SRE p.106-109)
- Diagnose: injecting known test data at each step (black-box) and having a **reproducible test case** speeds everything up and allows using a non-production environment with more invasive techniques; divide and conquer from one end to the other (good for pipelines) or **bisection** in huge systems. "What, where, why" example (Spanner with high latency: CPU in the tasks; profiling shows log sorting; the cost is in a regex; solution: rewrite to avoid backtracking, look for similar patterns, consider **RE2**, which does not backtrack and has linear growth, while PCRE can take exponential time, note 68). "What touched it last": systems have inertia; mark the start and end of deploys on the error graphs. (SRE p.108-109)
- Test and Treat: five cautions when designing tests: **mutually exclusive** alternatives; **obvious first**, in decreasing order of likelihood and considering the risk; **confounding factors** (a firewall rule that only allows a certain IP); **side effects** of active tests (more CPUs can increase data races; verbose logging can worsen a latency problem); some tests **only suggest** (race conditions and deadlocks that are hard to reproduce). Write down ideas, tests and results; in a shared document or chat, the timestamp helps the postmortem and informs others (note 70); record changes systematically so they can be undone. (SRE p.110, 115)
- "Negative results are magic": negative results are **conclusive** (one example: a web server handled only ~800 of the 8,000 connections needed due to lock contention, and that record saves the next team the experiment); microbenchmarks, documented antipatterns and project postmortems qualify; benchmark tools and load generators can be born from experiments that did not confirm (Apache Bench); publishing improves the data-based culture; be suspicious of a document that mentions no failure. (SRE p.110-111)
- Cure: proving the cause in production is hard because systems are complex (**several factors that together cause it**, path/state dependence) and reproducing may be infeasible; a non-production environment mitigates, at the cost of maintaining another copy of the system; afterward, write the postmortem. (SRE p.111)
- App Engine case study (simplified version): latency rose almost an **order of magnitude**, CPU and processes nearly **quadrupled**, with no traffic increase and no code or infrastructure change (the change was on a Saturday, with no pushes underway); other apps were not affected; the correlation with `merge_join` (suspected index) was spurious because even static content, served without the datastore, was slow; there were ~**250 ms** between the start of work on the request and the first RPCs, where Dapper does not help because it only traces RPCs; since the customer had a public launch the following week, they **mitigated before understanding**, recommending the instance type with more CPU, which brought latency to an acceptable level; the root cause: an old access-control bug created a whitelist object every time a specific path was accessed, and an automatic security scanner generated **thousands of objects in half an hour**, which came to be checked on every request (common pattern: configuration objects read from the datastore and kept in memory, with time proportional to the quantity, note 76). Note 75 (printed on p.115 of the PDF) admits that launching with an unidentified bug is not ideal, but eliminating all known bugs is usually impractical. (SRE p.112-115)
- Troubleshooting notes (p.114-115): "horses, not zebras" holds in some domains, but in a system with a well-designed cluster filesystem entire classes of failure can be eliminated (note 61); prefer simple explanations, but remember **Hickam's dictum** (several small problems can explain all the symptoms, note 62); the **5 Whys** technique (Ohno) resembles "what, where, why" (note 67); note 72 refers to works on the limits of looking for a single root cause. To make troubleshooting easier: white-box observability and structured logs from the start, well-understood interfaces between components and a **unique request ID** across RPCs. (SRE p.114-115)

**Incident management (Ch. 14), details.**
- The book's unmanaged incident (Mary, 2 p.m. on a Thursday): three of five datacenters go down in sequence and the rest overload; Mary looks at logs, reverts the release with no effect, calls whoever wrote the code (3:30 a.m. in their time zone), other colleagues "just look", executives and vice presidents demand an ETA and make irrelevant suggestions, and Malcolm, uncoordinated, deploys a CPU affinity change that **takes down** the servers. Hazards: **narrow technical focus**, **poor communication** and **freelancing**. (SRE p.122-123)
- Roles (p.123): the *incident commander* holds the high-level state, structures the task force and in fact **fills all the roles not delegated**; the *Ops lead* applies the operational tools and **only the operations team modifies the system**; *Communication* is the public face, issues periodic updates to the team and stakeholders (usually by email) and may keep the incident document up to date; *Planning* handles the longer term: filing bugs, **ordering dinner**, organizing handoffs and tracking how far the system has diverged from normal so it can be reverted later. If someone's load is excessive, ask the planning lead for more people and delegate, including creating sub-incidents. The clear separation of responsibilities gives **more autonomy**, since no one needs to doubt a colleague. (SRE p.123)
- Command post (p.123-124): war room or working at desks with email and IRC; Google considers **IRC** a great help (reliable, serves as a log of communications, bots record incident traffic and events such as alerts, useful for distributed teams). The **live incident state document** is the IC's most important responsibility: editable by several people (most use Google Docs; **Google Docs SRE uses Google Sites**, since depending on the software you are trying to fix is a bad idea); it can be messy, but must be functional; use a template and the most important information at the top; keep it for the postmortem. **Handoff**: at the end of the day, pass command explicitly (by phone or video, saying "you're now the incident commander, okay?") and only leave the call with a firm acceptance; tell everyone who is leading. (SRE p.124)
- Managed incident: Mary hands command to Sabrina, who summarizes by email to a pre-agreed list, records the impact assessment, keeps VPs informed without minutiae, asks an external communications representative to draft the message to users, and calls in the on-call developer only with Mary's approval; those helping prioritize the delegated tasks and report what they do; at **5 p.m.** she starts finding replacements, at **5:45 p.m.** there is a conference, at **6 p.m.** the handoff to the sister office, which mitigates, closes and starts the postmortem. (SRE p.124)
- Declare early (p.125): it is better to declare the incident early and close it with a simple fix than to trigger the framework hours later. Skills atrophy without use: use the framework in ordinary operational changes that cross time zones/teams, include incident management in disaster recovery tests (DiRT) and simulate responses to problems already solved. Stated result: lower **mean time to recovery** and less stressful work. Best practices: **Prioritize** (stop the bleeding, restore service, preserve evidence), **Prepare**, **Trust** (autonomy within the role), **Introspect** (attention to emotional state; if you panic, ask for support), **Consider alternatives**, **Practice**, **Change it around** (switch roles). (SRE p.125)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 6): on-call compensation (Ch. 11, p.101) and the 25%/50% (p.100) [from source]
- **What the book says about compensation (p.101).** One must consider **adequate compensation** for out-of-hours support; organizations handle this in different ways, and Google offers **time off in lieu (time-off-in-lieu) or cash payment, with a cap defined as some proportion of total salary**. (SRE p.101)
- **What the cap is for (p.101).** The compensation cap represents, in practice, a **limit on the amount of on-call work any person will take on**. The structure ensures the **incentive** to take part in on-call as the team needs, but also promotes **balanced distribution** of on-call and limits the possible negative effects of too much on-call, such as burnout or lack of time for projects. (SRE p.101)
- **How it fits with the rest of the chapter (p.100).** Google's ideal is to invest **at least 50% of SRE time in engineering**; of the rest, **at most 25% on-call**, leaving up to another 25% for other operational non-project work; the 25% rule gives the minimum of **8 SREs** for a 24/7 rotation at a single site (primary and secondary, weekly shifts, each person on-call one week per month) and **6 per team** at two sites. There are at most **2 incidents per 12-hour shift** (p.101). That is, the compensation cap is a second brake on the same sustainability problem. (SRE p.100-101)
- The book does not state the percentage of salary used as the cap nor time-off amounts per on-call hour; nor does it address the labor law regime on standby duty of any country. Any number, article of law or standby rule (for example in Brazil) is outside the book and, from memory, is [extrapolation]: the correct conduct is to refer to legal or HR instead of quoting values. (SRE p.101)

## What the book does NOT say
Severity levels (SEV); "scribe" or "liaison" roles; standard status cadence beyond the 4 h of the example; MTTD; runbooks as a separate document (it uses "playbook").
