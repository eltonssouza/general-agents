# Data integrity, backup and recovery (Ch. 26)

## Definition and requirements
- Integrity = a measure of the accessibility and accuracy of datastores for an adequate level of service; to the user, loss, corruption and prolonged unavailability are indistinguishable. [from source] (SRE p.242)
- Uptime and integrity are independent requirements: a 99.99% SLO of good bytes on 2 GB would allow up to 200 KB corrupted. [from source] (SRE p.242)
- Secret: proactive detection plus fast repair and recovery. [from source] (SRE p.243)
- Five goals of cloud apps: uptime, latency, scale, velocity, privacy. [from source] (SRE p.243)

## Backups vs archives
- The focus is recovery; backups can be reloaded into the application, archives cannot (audit, discovery, compliance); recovering archives generally does not need to fit the service's uptime requirements. Book example: retaining financial transaction data for seven years by moving the accumulated audit logs, once a month, to long-term offsite archival storage = archive, not backup. [from source] (SRE p.244)
- "A recovery system, not a backup system"; data availability SLOs per failure mode and proof that they are met. [from source] (SRE p.246)

## 24 failure types
A combination of three factors: root cause (6 values), scope (wide/narrow) and rate (big bang/creeping): 6 x 2 x 2 = 24 (the book states 24; the multiplication is from the book and its notes). [from source] (SRE p.246)

## Replication is not recoverability
- Replication propagates corruption and deletions; diversity of layers (media isolation) protects against layer failures. [from source] (SRE p.247)
- Point-in-time recovery (PITR; "time-travel" inside Google) for an application with ACID and BASE datastores, meeting strict uptime, latency, scale, velocity and cost targets, is "a chimera" in the text. [from source] (SRE p.247) [DATED] (2016; do not extrapolate to current products). Solving it with in-house engineering sacrifices velocity; that is why many projects adopt a **layered backup strategy without PITR**.
- **Example of layers without PITR (p.247)** [from source]: the APIs under the application may offer several recovery mechanisms; expensive local "snapshots" give limited protection against application bugs and fast restore, so a few days of them are retained, taken a few hours apart; cheaper full and incremental copies every two days can be retained longer. PITR is great if one of these strategies offers it. Consider the recovery options of the cloud APIs you will use, trade PITR for layers if needed, but **do not end up with neither** (the book accepts a layered strategy without PITR and accepts trading one for the other; the caveat is not to end up with neither; it is not a requirement to always have PITR **and** layers); **if you can have both capabilities, use both**, since each will be valuable at some point. (SRE p.247)
- **Harder variants (p.246-247)** [from source]: the study of 19 recovery efforts shows that the most common loss involves data deletion or loss of referential integrity due to software bugs; the harder variants are low-grade corruption or deletion discovered weeks or months after the bug reached production, which may require recovering data of millions of users spread over days, weeks or months, each artifact at a distinct point in time. (SRE p.246-247)
- **Retention (p.248)** [from source]: gradual data loss can take days to catch the right person's attention, which calls for older snapshots; when going back that far, it is usually necessary to merge the restored data with the current state, which greatly complicates the restore. Freshness x completeness (p.247): exporting a database snapshot to the filesystem can take about 40 minutes (restoring the last snapshot loses up to 40 recent minutes), a full filesystem backup can take hours, and the restore usually takes as long as the backup. (SRE p.247) **Snapshots and site failure (p.250):** to recover fast, Google keeps expensive snapshots for very short periods inside the storage instance itself and less recent backups in random-access distributed storage in the same (or a nearby) datacenter for a little longer; **a strategy with only this does not protect against site-level failures**, so these backups are usually moved to nearline or offline locations for longer before expiring in favor of newer backups. Snapshots on the same storage also compete for the same capacity as the live datastore (note 129, p.260). (SRE p.250, 260)

## Backup versus archive and the cloud environment (p.244-245) [from source]
- **Backups x archives (p.244)**: the real focus of a backup is recovery; the decisive difference is that backups can be reloaded into the application and archives cannot. Archives keep data for long periods for audit, discovery and compliance, and recovery does not need to fit the uptime requirements (example: retaining financial data for seven years, with a week to recover logs during a one-month audit). Real backups need to be recovered quickly, well within uptime needs; since the most recent data is at risk until it is copied, it is advisable to schedule them daily, hourly or more often, as full and incremental or continuous (streaming). When formulating the strategy, consider how fast recovery must be and how much recent data can be lost. (SRE p.244-245)
- **Cloud environment challenges (p.245)**: mixing transactional and non-transactional backup/restore can return incorrect data; services that evolve nonstop have different versions of the logic acting in parallel on the data; independently versioned services may momentarily interact at incompatible versions, increasing the risk of corruption; the provider offers few APIs, simple but robust (locality and cache, distribution, strong/eventual consistency, durability and recovery). SRE goal: integrity is the means and **availability is the objective**; moral of the e-mail with 10 days of unavailability (data "lost" and later recovered): integrity without expected and regular availability amounts, to the user, to having no data. (SRE p.245)

## Defense in depth
- Layers: 1st soft deletion, 2nd backups and recovery, 3rd regular data validation (early detection); replication as an overarching layer. [from source] (SRE p.248)
- Soft deletion: typical destruction delay of 15, 30, 45 or 60 days; most problems appear within 60 days. Lazy deletion is controlled by the storage system. [from source] (SRE p.249)
- Backups: decide per recovery scenario (how much recent data can be lost, how fast to recover, how far back in the past); Google drew the line between 30 and 90 days for many services; tier 1 layers (fast, days), tier 2, nearline/offline. [from source] (SRE p.250-251)
- 1T vs 1E: validating 700 PB at 300 MB/s by one task would take 8 decades; full copies take at least that and the restore even more (almost a century to restore a backup that was up to 80 years old). Most common and effective technique: **trust points**, portions of the data verified after they become immutable (usually through the passage of time); what is fixed is verified and copied and incrementals are made only of what changed since the last backup, aligning backup time with that of the main processing. But the restore matters: a full backup from 3 years ago + daily incrementals = a chain of more than 1,000 interdependent backups, each with extra risk of failure and logistical cost. Parallelism: with good sharding, N tasks verify/copy 1/N of the data, requiring planning of the schema and physical deployment to (1) balance the data, (2) ensure each shard's independence, (3) avoid contention between sibling tasks; combined with time slices, it reduces the 8 decades by orders of magnitude (detail in 'Numbers and narratives'). [from source] (SRE p.251-252)
- Out-of-band validation: validate only invariants that cause devastation; Gmail detects within 24 h; Drive turned an emergency into routine; requires job management, alerts, rate limiting, tools, playbooks and APIs. [from source] (SRE p.252-253)
- Who builds and how to scale the rigor of validators: small high-velocity teams rarely manage to design and maintain all these systems alone (the result would be brittle improvisations); so a **central infrastructure team** maintains the out-of-band validation framework for several product teams, and each **product team** maintains the validator's customized business logic. As the service scales, a layered strategy is worthwhile: **sacrifice rigor in the daily validators**, ensuring they still catch the most disastrous scenarios within 24 h, and do the more rigorous validation at a **lower frequency**, to contain cost and latency (Compute Storage needed a more efficient way than brute force when its validators stopped finishing in a day). Investigating intermittent failed validation is hard work: the cause may vanish in minutes, hours or days, so it is essential to drill down quickly into the validation audit logs; the Gmail on-call gets alert response playbooks, a BigQuery-like investigation tool and a validation dashboard. [from source] (SRE p.253)

## Knowing that recovery works
Test recovery continuously, end to end; alert if the process stops emitting a success heartbeat; verification checklist (valid backups, resources, time, monitoring, dependencies). [from source] (SRE p.254)

## Cases

## Numbers and narratives from Ch. 26 that were missing [from source]
- "How long is too long?": the 2011 Gmail incident showed that **four days** is too long; Google considers **24 hours** a good starting point for the "too long" limit of data unavailability in Google Apps. A 99.99% uptime SLO leaves about an hour per year (the book text; by the App. A table it is 52.6 min), but a 99.99% SLO of good bytes in a 2 GB artifact would accept up to 200 KB corrupted, catastrophic. An object unavailable for 30 minutes in the year would still be 99.99% available and, from the user's point of view, with 100% integrity (or close). (SRE p.242-243)
- **24 failure types** = 6 root causes (user action, operator error, application bug, infrastructure defect, failed hardware, site catastrophe) × 2 scopes (wide/narrow) × 2 rates (big bang, such as 1 million rows becoming 10 in a minute; or creeping, such as 10 rows deleted per minute for weeks). A study of **19 data recovery efforts** at Google found that the most common loss scenarios involved **data deletion or loss of referential integrity caused by software bugs**. (SRE p.246)
- A database snapshot to the filesystem can take **40 minutes**: restoring the last snapshot loses up to 40 minutes of recent data. (SRE p.247) Soft deletion: delays of 15, 30, 45 or 60 days; most account hijackings and integrity problems appear within 60 days; Gmail's trash keeps messages deleted less than 30 days ago. Backups: Google drew the line between **30 and 90 days** for many services. (SRE p.249-250)
- **1T vs 1E**: validating **700 PB** with one task at 300 MB/s (ideal SATA 2.0) would take **8 decades** [checked: 700×10^15 B ÷ 300×10^6 B/s ≈ 2.3×10^9 s ≈ 74 years]; making a few full backups takes at least as long, and the restore, with post-processing, even more: almost a century to restore a backup that was up to 80 years old when the restore began (scenario 1: monolithic, serial). **Scenario 2, distinct**: with trust points and incrementals (full from 3 years ago + daily incrementals), a full restore serially processes a chain of more than 1,000 highly interdependent backups, each with additional risk of failure, scheduling logistical load and execution cost ("we care about restores, not backups"). Solutions: trust points (data verified after becoming immutable, usually through time; incrementals only of what was modified since the last backup) and N tasks in parallel (1/N of the data each), which requires balancing the data, ensuring each shard's independence and avoiding contention between sibling tasks. (SRE p.251-252)
- Out-of-band validation: production errors detected within **24 hours**; the largest validator is split into **10 to 14 shards**, validating one per day; refactoring cut spindle contention by 60%; at Drive a 2013 emergency became routine. At scale, the book proposes less rigorous daily validators (still covering the most disastrous scenarios within 24 h) and more rigorous validation at lower frequency, rather than abandoning coverage; the central infrastructure team maintains the framework and the product teams maintain the validator logic; intermittent failure requires quick drill-down into the audit logs (the cause may vanish in minutes, hours or days), with playbooks, a BigQuery-like tool and a dashboard. (SRE p.253)
- **Gmail, 02/27/2011 (GTape)**: user data vanished from Gmail despite the safeguards; it was the first large-scale use of GTape, a global tape backup, to restore live data (simulated many times beforehand); Google gave a time estimate, restored all accounts a few hours after the estimate and recovered **more than 99% of the data before the estimated deadline**; the public disclosure of the tape caused surprise and amusement. (SRE p.254-255)
- **Google Music, March 2012**: the deletion pipeline, rebuilt in 2012 for efficiency, had its first run on **February 6** (metadata) and a second stage removed the associated audio; an engineer noticed the problem on **March 6**; an improvised MapReduce showed on **March 8** that about **600,000** audio references had been removed, affecting **21,000** users (the real damage could be larger). Root cause was identified in parallel with the recovery, because reactivating the pipeline would hurt innocent users (the "Catch-22"); Music's tapes went to external locations (unlike Gmail), so tape recovery was slow. Only **436,223** of the ~600,000 tracks were on tape; **~161,000** were removed before being copied. The restore of **1.5 PB** was split into **5,475 restore jobs** (typing one per minute would take more than 3 days, so SRE built a programmatic solution); it finished requesting at midnight on **March 9**; 4 hours later the list of **5,337 tapes** to retrieve from offsite came out; 8 hours later they arrived by truck; technicians loaded the tapes by hand (in DiRT this proved hundreds of times faster than the vendor's robots). It took longer than the 2 days estimated: on the morning of March 10 only **74%** of the 436,223 files (from **3,475** tapes) had been transferred, because a vendor omitted **1,862** tapes and there were **17 bad tapes** (covered by redundant encoding); **more than 99.95%** on the morning of March 11; an unrelated critical failure occupied the team for 2 days; all 436,223 tracks became accessible again on **March 13**, in just under **7 days** (5 of effective recovery). Second wave: the ~161,000 remaining tracks were mostly purchased from the store or promotional (original copies intact, reinstated quickly); the small part uploaded by the users themselves was recovered by asking customers to **re-upload** the files from March 14 onward, which took over a week. Root cause: a race condition between stages designed to run in strict sequence with a 3-hour gap, an assumption that stopped holding as volume grew. (SRE p.255-258)

## Round 4 sweep: notes and details from Ch. 26 [from source]
- Note 133 (p.260): cloud engineers hesitate to alert on the **data deletion rate** because of natural per-user variation; since the goal is to detect **global** anomalies, it is more useful to alert when the global deletion rate, aggregated across all users, exceeds an extreme threshold (the book's example is **10x the observed 95th percentile**) than to alert per user. After the incident, Music redesigned the pipeline to eliminate the race condition and strengthened monitoring to catch runaway deletions before users noticed. (SRE p.258, 260)
- Music mechanics (p.256-258): the engineer escalated the case to top priority and the pipeline was shut down to stop the damage; manually verifying metadata of millions to billions of files across several datacenters was infeasible; the team split into two fronts (more experienced SREs on recovery and developers on code analysis); the first root cause hypothesis (a dependent storage service supplying faulty data) was a **red herring** and was discarded; the tape team was notified at **4:34 pm (Pacific time) on March 8**; the recovery took place a few weeks after the annual DiRT, and the tape team already knew the new tool tested in DiRT; Music's tapes went by truck to external locations (unlike Gmail) because they needed more space. (SRE p.256-257) Encryption (p.256): Google Music's backup tapes were **encrypted** and trucked to offsite locations, unlike the Gmail case, which made the restore slow; in the extracted text the term appears on p.30 (Colossus, replication and encryption, Ch. 2) and on p.256, that is, **not only in Ch. 2**; the book does not treat encryption as a topic of its own [inference]. [from source] (SRE p.256)
- Chapter notes: 128 (p.259-260) **Blobstore** example, whose APIs do not allow deleting data and metadata directly: they offer backup policies by default (offline replicas), end-to-end checksums and a default tombstone lifetime (soft deletion), which saved customers several times; for those with mandatory deletion deadlines, soft deletion was the most pertinent protection. 129 (p.260): a *snapshot* is a static read-only view (usually copy-on-write) and can be expensive because it **competes for the same storage capacity** and because, the faster the data changes, the less is gained from copy-on-write. 130: GFS-style replication and Reed-Solomon codes. 132: creating a programmatic solution to request the restores was not an obstacle because most SREs are experienced software engineers, and that is why SRE is hard to hire for. 122-124: ACID (SQL, MySQL/PostgreSQL) versus BASE (Bigtable, Megastore, "NoSQL"). (SRE p.259-260)
- Conclusion (p.259): data availability must be the main concern; as in test-driven development, prove that the system maintains data availability with a predicted maximum unavailability time; avoid "the operation was a success, but the system died"; have a disaster matrix with updated and exercised plans; reduce the recovery time N with faster and more granular loss detection, in the direction of N = 0, and then move from recovery planning to prevention planning. In the "Revisit and Reexamine" example, the Shakespeare index never had a backup because it could be recreated by MapReduce; when the user annotations feature appears, the data stops being recreatable and starts to require backup and tested restore (including in DiRT) within the SLO. (SRE p.259)
