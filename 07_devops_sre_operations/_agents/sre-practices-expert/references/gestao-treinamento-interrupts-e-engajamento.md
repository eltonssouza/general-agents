## Training up to on-call (Ch. 28)
- Trust in the team: colleagues know the system, ask for help, react under pressure. [from source] (SRE p.274)
- Eight recommended patterns (Table 28-1; the anti-patterns column is cut off in the PDF): concrete, sequential experiences; reverse engineering and statistical thinking; celebrating failure analysis by reading postmortems; realistic, contained breakages; disaster role-play; early on-call shadowing; mentoring; a non-trivial project with partial ownership. [from source] (SRE p.274)
- "Trial by fire" (throwing the newcomer into the ticket queue) is typical of reactive ops. [from source] (SRE p.276)
- On-call learning checklist (Mixer as an example): contacts, backends, what to know before moving on, docs, comprehension questions; layered access ("powerups"). [from source] (SRE p.277)
- Initial projects: a user-visible change, monitoring where there is a blind spot, automating one pain. [from source] (SRE p.278)
- Five practices: hunger for failure (postmortem reading clubs, "tales of fail"), disaster role playing (Wheel of Misfortune: game master, 30-60 min), breaking and fixing real things, documentation as apprenticeship, shadow on-call (and reverse shadow). [from source] (SRE p.279-282)
- Going on-call is a rite of passage; "scale your humans faster than your machines". [from source] (SRE p.283)

## Interrupts (Ch. 29)
- Three categories of operational load: pages, tickets, ongoing operational activities. [from source] (SRE p.284)
- Google on-call: a single engineer per shift (avoids the bystander effect); secondary as backup. [from source] (SRE p.284)
- Flow state; polarize time (ideally a week; a day may be practical) to minimize context switches. [from source] (SRE p.285-287)
- Do not expect anyone to do on-call and project work at the same time; stop assigning tickets randomly "to victims"; push manager for ongoing responsibilities. [from source] (SRE p.287-288)
- Reduce interrupts: analyze tickets by root cause, "policy can be as powerful as code", give effort back to customers, "give the pager back". [from source] (SRE p.288-289)

## Embedding an SRE (Ch. 30)
- One embedded SRE, focused on practices, not on emptying the queue; two do not necessarily improve things. [from source] (SRE p.290)
- Phase 1 learn the service and identify "kindling" (emergencies waiting to happen); Phase 2 share context (write a blameless postmortem for the team; refute the Bad Apple Theory); Phase 3 drive change (start with an SLO; do not fix it yourself; explain the reasoning; guiding questions). [from source] (SRE p.290-293)
- End: after-action report (or "postvitam"). [from source] (SRE p.294)

## Communication and collaboration (Ch. 31)
- Weekly production meeting of 30-60 min; rotating chair; standard agenda (upcoming changes, metrics, outages, paging events, nonpaging events, previous action items). [from source] (SRE p.296-297)
- Formal roles: tech lead (TL), manager (SRM) and project manager (PM, TPM or PgM). **Well-defined roles** allow deciding within scope quickly and safely; a **fluid** team (responsibilities negotiated dynamically) tends to be more mature and more able to adapt, at the cost of communicating more and more often, because less context can be assumed. The TL is responsible for technical direction (from commenting on everyone's code to quarterly direction presentations or building consensus); at Google the TL can do almost all of the manager's work, but the manager has two responsibilities the TL does not: **performance management** and being the **catchall** for whatever no one else covers. [from source] (SRE p.298)
- Specialization: a team that takes care of only one product gains technical mastery and reduces cognitive load, but this also creates **silos** and ignorance of the bigger picture; the book seeks a clear **charter** of what the team supports and, more importantly, what it does not support, and admits it does not always succeed. [from source] (SRE p.298)
- Diversity: the chance of collaborating well increases with more diverse teams (the book cites evidence that diverse teams are better); this requires attention to communication and cognitive biases, which the book says it does not cover in detail. [from source] (SRE p.298) The link between defined or fluid roles and team maturity is from the book; using the choice between the two for a concrete case is [inference].
- Viceroy case (dashboards, 2012-2014): duplicated efforts that came together; cross-site collaboration recommendations (summary below). [from source] (SRE p.298-301)
- **Recommendations for cross-site projects (p.301).** Develop cross-site **only when needed** (cost: higher latency of actions and more communication; benefit, if the mechanics are good: much greater throughput; a single-site project also has a cost, since no one outside it knows what is being done). Motivated contributors are worth it, but not every contribution is worth the same: confirm they are **committed**, and not joining for self-fulfillment (earning a résumé mark by putting their name on a trendy project, coding on an exciting new project without committing to maintaining it); those with a specific goal tend to maintain what they deliver better. Project structure: leaders provide long-term vision and align/prioritize the work; have an **agreed way to decide**, optimizing for local decisions when there is a lot of agreement and trust; "divide and conquer": as many reasonably sized components as possible, each for a small group, preferably within one site, with **clear deliverables and deadlines** (without letting Conway's law distort the software too much); team goal oriented to functionality or a problem, with the component only complete when integrated and used; design docs and reviews per component; writing is the main technique against distance. Standards: style guides are only a start; in every debate, discuss thoroughly **with a strict time limit**, choose, document and follow; without agreement, an **arbiter** that everyone respects. Face-to-face interaction is irreplaceable: leaders meet the rest of the team and, if there is a budget, a **summit** (in a **neutral location** when neutrality matters, so no site has "home advantage"). Management style appropriate to the project stage (low overhead at the start). [from source] (SRE p.301)
- Early collaboration with development (DFP migration from MySQL to F1). [from source] (SRE p.301-303)

## Engagement model (Ch. 32)
- PRR (Production Readiness Review) is the typical initial step for SRE to take over a service. [from source] (SRE p.305)
- Simple PRR in 6 phases: Engagement, Analysis, Improvements and Refactoring, Training, Onboarding, Continuous Improvement. [from source] (SRE p.308-309)
- PRR goals: (1) verify whether the service meets accepted production setup and operational readiness standards and whether the owners are prepared to work with SRE and take advantage of its experience; (2) improve the service's reliability in production and minimize the number and severity of expected incidents; the PRR looks at all production aspects of interest to SRE. Before starting, SRE weighs the importance of the service and the availability of SRE teams, and the two organizations agree on the staffing level. There are three related models (Simple PRR, Early Engagement, Frameworks and SRE Platform); the Simple PRR usually targets a service **already launched** that an SRE team is going to take over. [from source] (SRE p.308)
- Analysis phase checklist: service-specific and maintained by SRE (based on experience with similar systems and on the Production Guide). The book gives only **examples** of items: do updates hit a large part of the system at once? does the service talk to the right instance of each dependency (an end-user request must not depend on a system designed for batch)? does it request high enough network QoS for a critical remote dependency? does it report errors and exceptional conditions to central logging? are user-visible failures instrumented and alerted on? The checklist may include the operational standards ("gold standard") of a specific SRE team. The book does not give the complete list of any team. [from source] (SRE p.308)
- Limitations and costs of the Simple PRR: communication overhead between teams (an extra process for dev and cognitive load for SRE reviewers); need for the right SRE reviewers, available and able to manage time and priorities given the commitments they already have; the SRE's work needs to be highly visible and reviewed by the development team so there is knowledge exchange (SRE acts as part of the dev team, not as an external unit). The main limitation: the service is already launched and at scale, so SRE enters late in the cycle; entering earlier would greatly increase the chance of fixing problems. [from source] (SRE p.310)
- Early Engagement candidates: requires identifying early the service's importance or business value and whether it will have scale or complexity that justifies SRE. Typical services: (a) implements significant new functionality and will be part of a system SRE already manages; (b) is a significant rewrite or alternative to an existing system for the same use cases; (c) the development team asked SRE for advice or asked it to take over at launch. SRE enters at Design and the following phases and takes over the service at any point during or after Build; the model relies on active collaboration. [from source] (SRE p.310)
- Alternatives: documentation (Production Guide) and consulting (LCE). [from source] (SRE p.307)
- Early Engagement (Design, Build, Launch, Post-launch; dark launch) and disengagement as a positive outcome. [from source] (SRE p.310-311)
- **Disengaging (p.311):** (a) a service that does not need full-time SRE, or that SRE never officially took over, is a **positive** outcome (it was designed to be reliable and low-maintenance and stays with the development team); (b) if SRE engages early and the service **does not reach projected usage**, the effort spent is merely part of the **business risk** of new projects and a small cost compared with the success of those that reach the expected scale; the SRE team can be **reallocated** and the **lessons learned** go into the engagement process. [from source] (SRE p.311)
- Frameworks and SRE Platform: codified best practices, reusable solutions, common control surface; onboarding drops from 2-3 SREs and 2-3 quarters to typically one SRE in one quarter; shared responsibility (SRE on platform infra, devs on functional on-call). [from source] (SRE p.311-314) [DATED] **Detail from p.312-314**: the old model (PRR) took months and did not scale with microservices, hiring was hard and training long; the principles of the frameworks are codified practices, reusable solutions, common platform and control surface (uniform interfaces, operational controls, monitoring, logging and configuration) and smarter automation (p.312). **"One framework per environment" (Java, C++, Go) is on p.312** (not on p.313-314): platform and service frameworks maintained by SRE and development, so the product is designed on the SRE-blessed solution instead of adapting later; each concern becomes one or more modules (instrumentation and metrics, request logging, traffic and load control systems) (p.312-313). **Definition (p.313)**: a framework is a prescriptive implementation for using a set of software components and a canonical way of combining them, which can expose the components' controls in a cohesive way; examples from the book: business logic in semantic components with standard terms, standard monitoring dimensions, standard request debug log format, standard load shedding configuration format and a semantically consistent capacity/"overload" measure. Implementations in different languages (C++ x Java) **do not share code**, but the goal is to expose the same API, behavior, configuration and controls (p.313). Gains: less operational overhead (conformance tests, built-in deploy/monitoring/automation, idea in production in days), universal support by design (services without SRE use SRE-maintained capabilities and the staffing barrier is broken) and faster PRR/onboarding, normally one SRE in a quarter (p.313-314). **Shared responsibility model (p.314)**: the original model had only two options (full SRE support or practically none); now SRE supports the platform infrastructure and takes on development and maintenance of large parts of the software infrastructure (load shedding, overload, automation, traffic management, logging, monitoring), while the developers handle on-call for functional problems (bugs in the application code). [from source] (SRE p.312-314)

## Practices for production services (Appendix B, p.329-331)
Fail sanely; progressive rollouts ("rollback first, diagnose later"); define SLOs like a user; error budgets; monitoring (page/ticket/logging); postmortems; capacity planning (N+2); overloads and failure; SRE teams (≤50% on ops, at least 8 on-call, 6 per site in dual-site, ≤2 events per shift). [from source] (SRE p.329-331)

## Details from Ch. 28-32 (numbers, narratives and lists that were missing) [from source]
**Ch. 28: training.**
- Suggested curriculum for a serving stack in 5 steps, in order of execution: how the query comes in (network, frontend load balancing, proxies) → frontend serving (query logging, experience SLOs) → intermediate services (caches, backend balancing) → infrastructure → "putting it all together" (debugging, escalation, emergencies). The Search SRE "on-call learning checklist" (Mixer example) lists contacts, backends, "what to know before moving on" (e.g., which clusters Mixer is in, how to roll back, which backends are on the critical path and why), documents and comprehension questions; it does not codify procedures (it is "future-proof"); layered access: read first, mutate later ("powerups"). (SRE p.276-277)
- Initial projects: a trivial user-visible change followed through to production; monitoring where there is a blind spot; automating a pain that has not yet hurt enough. Desired characteristics: reverse engineering, statistical/comparative thinking ("which of these things is not like the others?": kernel, CPU, binary version, regional traffic mix) and improvisation. Class "Reverse Engineering a Production Service": the Google News team went on a cruise in the Bermuda Triangle and vanished for **30 days**; the students, the new News SRE, trace the query end to end and draw the stack. **Guided, interactive exercises:** at each step of the query's entry path from the browser, the emphasis is on learning several ways to discover connectivity between production servers, so as not to miss connections; in the middle of the class the students are challenged to find **another entry point** for traffic (the initial assumption was too narrow) and then other ways into the stack. **Sources used:** the heavily instrumented production binaries, which **self-report their RPC connectivity**, plus the available **white-box and black-box** monitoring, to discover which paths users' queries take; throughout the class a **system diagram** is built and the shared infrastructure the student will see again is discussed. **Final task:** each student returns to their home team and asks a **senior SRE** to help choose a stack (or slice) on which they will be on-call; with what they learned, they draw this stack on their own and present it to the senior. The student will miss some subtle details, which makes for good discussion, and the senior can also learn (it reveals deviations in the earlier understanding of a fast-changing system); the book recommends that the team welcome relearning from its newest members. (SRE p.278-279)
- Quarterly exercise "Let's burn a search cluster to the ground!" (Search SRE): starts from a good configuration and degrades the stack at chosen bottlenecks; the group discusses what should change, collects guesses before the damage and then validates the assumptions; it yields new bugs because systems do not always degrade as well as expected (p.281).
- Wheel of Misfortune: the game master (GM) leads primary and secondary through **30 to 60 minutes** of root-cause; the GM redirects from red herrings and injects stimuli; "break and fix real things" with the participants' predictions before the damage. (SRE p.280-282)

**Ch. 29: interrupts.**
- Three categories of load: pages (with a response SLO in minutes), tickets (hours to weeks) and ongoing responsibilities (rollouts, flag flips). A single engineer per on-call shift (avoids the bystander effect), with a secondary. (SRE p.284)
- **A 20-minute interruption in a project implies two context switches and in practice costs "a couple of hours" of productive work**; ideal: polarize time (a week, or a day or half a day), no project during the on-call week (if the project is too important, the person should not be on-call), tickets as a full-time role (two people if there are many), `push manager` for pushes and flag flips, formal ticket handoff; **do not distribute tickets randomly "to victims"**. To reduce: analyze root causes of tickets and pages ("don't run the gauntlet"), silence interruptions whose root cause will be fixed, use policy ("policy can be as powerful as code") and give the pager back. (SRE p.285-289)

**Ch. 30: embedding.**
- Google practice: split time equally between projects and reactive ops; **one** embedded SRE is enough (two can generate defensiveness). Phase 1: learn the service; "ops mode" vs nonlinear scale (a service at **100 req/s can become 10 thousand in a year**); identify the biggest sources of stress and the **kindling** (knowledge gaps from over-specialization, SRE-built services that grow without attention, dependence on "the next big project", common undiagnosed alerts, a service without SLI/SLO/SLA, a capacity plan of the "add servers" kind, postmortems whose actions only revert the change, a critical component that no one can explain; example: a capacity that passes the short test with 1.99 GB when the model predicts 2 GB is not resolved). Phase 2: write a good blameless postmortem for the team (refute the **Bad Apple Theory**) and classify fires as toil vs part of the job. Phase 3: **start with an SLO**, "probably the most important lever" to get out of reactive ops; do not fix it yourself (identify work for a team member, explain how it solves it permanently, be a reviewer, repeat for two or three issues); explain the reasoning and ask guiding (not loaded) questions; after-action report ("postvitam") and follow-up for a few months. (SRE p.290-294)

**Ch. 31: communication and collaboration.**
- Production meeting: 30 to 60 minutes, not so frequent that people run away, standard agenda; roles tech lead, manager and project manager (the manager has two exclusive responsibilities: performance management and being the "catchall"; trade-off defined x fluid roles, specialization and diversity: see the "Formal roles", "Specialization" and "Diversity" bullets above, p.298) (p.296, 298); **Viceroy** (framework and service for monitoring dashboards): several teams (Spanner, Ads Frontend and others) duplicated console efforts for **12 to 18 months** while migrating to Monarch (SRE is deeply conservative about monitoring, so Monarch was slow to catch on in SRE; Borgmon had awkward consoles, with its own HTML templating, full of special cases and hard to test; Monarch's console support failed for two reasons: consoles were easy for a small service but did not scale to complex consoles, and did not support the legacy system, making the transition difficult: p.299), until they discovered each other and joined forces in **mid-2012**; in early 2013 interest grew, but teams with their own solutions had little incentive to migrate; the first integration analysis with Consoles++ (1st half of 2013) concluded the differences were large (Viceroy almost without JavaScript, Consoles++ almost entirely in JavaScript); at the end of 2013 Viceroy started using JavaScript in the graphs and the integration became easy; integrated prototypes in early 2014; the combined system was complete at the end of 2014 and Viceroy was declared the general monitoring solution for all of SRE (recommended, not mandatory). (SRE p.298-300)
- **DFP case: MySQL → F1**: the ad indexing system of DoubleClick for Publishers used about **1,000 CPUs and 8 TB of RAM** to index **100 TB of data per day**; live migration with no user impact, with the output of the new service identical to the old one (index validated ad by ad against the old one), a design doc by two SREs reviewed by both teams, interfaces between infrastructure and business logic defined early, extraction of only the changed data and SRE and dev collaborating from the start with weekly meetings. (SRE p.302-303)

- PRR: **Engagement** (one to three SREs; SLO/SLA, design changes, training), **Analysis** (own checklist, examples: do updates affect a large percentage of the system? network QoS adequate for a critical dependency? errors reported to central logging? user-visible failures instrumented?), **Improvements and Refactoring** (prioritize and negotiate with dev), **Training**, **Onboarding** (progressive transfer; devs cover and guide for a period) and **Continuous Improvement**. Frameworks (one per environment: Java, C++, Go; p.312) and SRE Platform: onboarding used to require **2 to 3 SREs for 2 to 3 quarters**; with frameworks an idea reaches SRE production quality "in a matter of days"; microservices raise the fixed operational cost per service. (SRE p.310-314)

## Appendix B in detail: "Fail Sanely" and examples [from source]
- **Fail Sanely** (p.329): sanitize and validate configuration inputs and, faced with implausible input, **continue in the previous state and alert**; incorrect data (validate syntax and, if possible, semantics; watch for **empty, partial or truncated** data, for example alert if the configuration becomes N% smaller than the previous one) and delayed data (alert well before it expires). It is safer to continue with the previous configuration and wait for a human's approval before using the new data.
- Examples: in **2005** Google's global DNS load-balancing system received an **empty DNS entries file** because of file permissions, accepted it and served **NXDOMAIN for six minutes** for all Google properties; today it does sanity checks (including confirming the VIPs of google.com) and keeps serving the previous entries until it receives a file that passes; in **2009** incorrect but valid data led to the entire Web being flagged as malware: the file with the list of suspect URLs was replaced by a **single slash (/)**, which matched all URLs; checks for drastic size changes and for URLs believed unlikely would have prevented it. (SRE p.329)
- **Progressive rollouts** (p.329): non-emergency rollouts in stages, with small fractions of traffic and capacity, in different geographies (diurnal cycles and geographic mix), supervised by an engineer or, preferably, by monitoring proven reliable; if something unexpected occurs, **roll back first and diagnose later**.
- **SLOs like the user** (p.330): measuring error and latency at the Gmail **client**, instead of the server, substantially reduced the availability assessment and took Gmail from about **99.0% to more than 99.9%** in a few years. Email as an alert channel is "the moral equivalent of sending to /dev/null" (p.330).
- **Capacity and overload** (p.330-331): N+2 = peak served by N instances (possibly degraded) with the 2 largest unavailable (p.330); validate forecasts against reality, load test instead of tradition and do not confuse day-1 traffic with steady state (p.331); under overload, reasonable but worse results (Search searches less of the index and turns off Instant); well-behaved load shedding; **tarpitting** (responding with delay) and choosing **a consistent subset of clients** to receive errors; retries amplify low errors: drop a fraction of the traffic (including retries) upstream when total load exceeds capacity; every RPC client must have exponential backoff with jitter (mobile clients are especially problematic: weeks to fix).
- **SRE teams** (p.331): ≤ 50% on operational work; include the developers in on-call and tickets; minimum of eight on-call (six per site at two sites); at most two events per 12 h shift; final irony: with so few incidents the team may get out of practice and turn a short outage into a long one, hence practicing hypothetical outages.

## Corrections after the blind test (round 3): production meeting (p.296-297, 339), ticket scrub (p.288-289) and notes [from source]
**Production meeting (Ch. 31, p.296-297; minutes in App. F, p.339-340).**
- Purpose: the team articulates to itself and to the invitees the state of the services, to raise general awareness and improve operations; it is **service-oriented**, not about individuals' status; the goal is for everyone to leave with the same idea of what is happening, linking operational performance to design, configuration and implementation, with fix recommendations: an "immensely powerful" feedback loop. Frequency **weekly**, **30 to 60 minutes** (shorter: something cut or a small portfolio; longer: sinking into detail or there is too much and it is worth splitting the team or the set of services). It has a **chair**, preferably rotating (sense of ownership, trains whoever will coordinate incidents); in videoconference with teams of different sizes, the chair goes to the **smaller** side (the larger side tends to drown out the other). (SRE p.296)
- Standard agenda (not prescriptive): **upcoming production changes** (start, duration, expected effect; the default is to allow the change); **metrics** (latency, CPU, resources, efficiency; slow trends); **outages** (postmortem size, learning); **paging events**; **nonpaging events**; **previous action items** (assign an owner and follow up; a "catchall" item; consistent delivery builds credibility and trust). (SRE p.296-297)
- **Paging events (p.296).** These are the pages from monitoring about problems that may be postmortem-worthy, but often are not; while "Outages" looks at the bigger picture, here the view is **tactical**: the list of pages, who was paged, what happened. There are **two implicit questions**: (1) **should the alert have paged the way it did?** (2) **should it have paged at all?** If the answer to the second is no, **remove the non-actionable pages**.
- **Nonpaging events (p.297): three items and the action for each.** (a) Something that **probably should have paged and did not**: fix the monitoring so that it **pages** (often discovered while fixing something else, or tied to a metric that is tracked without an alert). (b) Something **not pageable, but that requires attention** (low-impact data corruption, slowness in a non-user-facing dimension): **track it**, and this is also where **reactive operational work** is tracked. (c) Something **not pageable and that does not require attention**: **remove the alert**, since it only creates noise that distracts from problems that deserve attention.
- Participation (p.297): attendance is mandatory for the service's SRE team (especially if spread across time zones), plus the main stakeholders and the development partners; if it is not possible to invite the developers, "fix that relationship" (start with a representative or a trusted intermediary). Techniques for too many invitees: less active services with a single representative or a commitment to read and comment on the minutes; a subset of representatives if the team is large; whoever is crucial but busy gives feedback beforehand or uses the **pre-filled agenda** (a real-time collaborative document, with a well-known address, for "bottom-up" ideas and parallel preparation). The meeting can be split, leaving SRE-only topics for the first half.
- **Example minutes (App. F, p.339):** 2015-10-23; attendees agoogler, clarac, docbrown, jennifer, martym; **Announcements:** outage #465 blew the error budget; **previous action items review:** certify the "Goat Teleporter" for use with cattle (bug 1011101; mass acceleration nonlinearities already predictable, precise target in a few days); **Outage Review (New Sonnet, outage 465):** 1.21 billion queries lost due to a cascading failure after the interaction between a latent bug (file descriptor leak on searches with no result), a new sonnet outside the corpus and unprecedented traffic; bug fixed and deployed (bug 5554825); evaluation of flux capacitor for balancing (5554823) and of load shedding (5554826); availability error budget annihilated, production pushes **frozen for 1 month** unless docbrown obtains an exception (the consensus is that the exception is unlikely); **Paging Events:** `AnnotationConsistencyTooEventual` paged 5 times in the week, probable replication delay between Bigtable regions (bug 4821600), no fix soon, so **the acceptable consistency threshold is raised** to reduce non-actionable alerts; **Nonpaging Events: None**; **Monitoring Changes and/or Silences:** delay threshold from 60 s to **180 s** (TODO martym); **Planned Production Changes:** cluster USA-1 offline for maintenance between 2015-10-29 and 2015-11-02, no response needed because traffic is routed to other clusters in the region. p.339 is only the example minutes; the action by event type is on p.297. (SRE p.339)

**Interrupt and ticket scrub (Ch. 29, p.288-289).**
- "**Be on interrupts, or don't be**" (p.288): sometimes an interrupt arrives that only one off-duty person knows how to handle; the goal is for this to be rare. And there are people who touch tickets without being scheduled "to look busy": this **does not help**, makes the person less effective and **distorts the numbers on how manageable the queue is** (with one owner and two or three more people chipping in, the queue may be unmanageable without anyone noticing). That is the book's reason; it does not talk about "breaking polarization" at this point.
- **Actually analyze the tickets** (p.288): ticket/on-call rotations become a "gauntlet": each person passes through, sighs with relief and goes back to work, and the root cause is never investigated. There should be a **ticket handoff** as there is for on-call (few teams do it), and a regular **scrub of tickets and pages**: examine classes of interrupts to find the root cause; if it can be fixed in a reasonable time, **silence the interruptions until the expected fix date**, which relieves whoever handles them and creates a "deadline reminder" for whoever fixes. That is, silencing conditioned on a date is **endorsed** by the book.
- **Respect yourself and respect the customer** (p.288-289): the team defines the level of service it provides and it is acceptable to give some of the effort back to customers; "policy can be as powerful as code", temporary or permanent. If the component is fragile, with few development resources and few needy customers, assess the value of your time and consider **giving the pager back**, discontinuing or replacing; slow or complicated steps that do not require your privileges can be returned to the requester (for example donating computing resources or preparing a code/configuration change and sending it for review). Guiding principle: the request must be **meaningful, rational and bring the information and prior work** you need; in return, your response must be helpful and **timely**.

**Notes on Ch. 28 and 31.** Ch. 28 (p.283): the trainee enters on-call when they reason about most of the stack and improvise on the rest; some teams give a final exam, others accept the completed *on-call learning checklist*; it is a **rite of passage to celebrate**; afterwards, a **regular learning series** on stack changes (presented by SREs who drive the change, with developers if needed, recorded to build a library) and talks by SREs to the developers. Note 139: the "follow the RPC" approach (following the request through the stack) also applies to batch/pipeline systems: start with the operation that triggers the system (data arriving, transaction to validate). Ch. 31 (p.303-304): the SRE and development teams held **weekly meetings** during the DFP migration; the SREs led the design of the new infrastructure (extracting the F1 tables, filtering and joining, extracting only what changed, surviving machine loss, resource use linear with the extracted data, capacity planning); two SREs wrote the design doc and both teams reviewed it; the interfaces between infrastructure and business logic (BL) were defined early; the new service was deployed in a test environment similar to production and the ad index of the old service had to be **identical** to that of the new one (differences due to edge cases of the new format were debugged ad by ad); the SRE team was already preparing resources in another datacenter, monitoring rules and on-call training, and put together a basic release process with validation; the launch took place with no visible impact on users. ## Corrections after the blind test (round 6): shadow on-call, postmortem co-authoring, reverse shadow, interrupt factors, flow, human cost and Ch. 30 with examples [from source]
### Shadow on-call, step by step (Ch. 28, p.281-283)
- **Prerequisite for shadowing (p.281).** Many teams maintain an *on-call learning checklist*, an organized list of reading and understanding of the system's technologies and concepts; the student needs to **internalize this list before being eligible as a shadow on-caller**. It serves different people: the student (it delimits what the team supports and shows what matters most and why, so they do not get lost in esoteric details learned over time); mentors and managers (progress is observable: which sections they are studying today, which confuse the most); everyone (it becomes a **social contract**: by mastering it, the student joins the on-call rotation, and the list sets the standard everyone must uphold). (SRE p.281-282)
- **Living documentation (p.282).** Outdated documentation weighs less on seniors, who keep the state of the world in their heads, and more on newcomers, who do not feel authorized to fix it. At Search SRE, before the newcomer arrives, the team reviews the list and orders the sections by how up to date they are; the newcomer receives the whole list and the task of reworking one or two of the most outdated sections; each technology brings the contacts of specialist SRE and development, and the student is encouraged to connect with them early; afterwards they contribute a revised section, **peer reviewed** by one or more seniors listed as specialists. (SRE p.282)
- **How to set up shadowing (p.282).** No hypothetical exercise replaces real on-call, but it is unfair to make the newcomer wait for the first real page. After completing the system fundamentals (for example, the checklist), consider configuring the alerting system to **copy incoming pages to the newcomer, initially only during business hours**, counting on their curiosity. These shadow shifts give the mentor visibility into the student's progress and the student visibility into the on-call responsibilities; shadowing several people makes the team comfortable with the idea of them joining the rotation; this confidence lets the more senior disconnect when they are not on call, which helps avoid burnout. (SRE p.282)
- **The newcomer is not the on-caller (p.282).** When the page arrives, the new SRE is **not the designated on-caller**, which removes the time pressure; they get a front-row seat while the outage happens, and not only after it is resolved; they can share a terminal session or sit nearby to compare notes. At a convenient moment after the outage, the on-caller reviews with the student the reasoning and processes followed, which also increases retention for the one who shadowed. (SRE p.282)
- **Postmortem co-authoring (tip on p.282).** If the outage warrants a postmortem, the on-caller should **include the newcomer as co-author**; the text should not be dumped on the student alone, because they might learn that postmortems are grunt work pushed to the most junior, and creating that impression would be a mistake. This is different from "the newcomer writes the postmortem alone". (SRE p.282)
- **Reverse shadow (p.282).** Some teams include a final step: the experienced on-caller does **reverse shadow** of the student. The newcomer becomes the primary on-call and owner of all escalations, and the experienced person lurks, diagnosing independently **without modifying any state**, available for active support, validation and tips as needed. (SRE p.282)
- **Rite of passage (p.283).** When the student can reason about most of the stack and improvise on the rest, they should join the service's on-call. Some teams give a final exam, others accept the completed checklist as evidence; whatever the criterion, joining on-call is a rite of passage and should be celebrated as a team. Afterwards, a regular learning series (presentations of stack changes by those driving them, recorded to build a library) and talks by SREs to the developers. (SRE p.283)
- **Ch. 28 notes (p.283).** Note 135 (attached to the sentence on p.274 about colleagues knowing how the system works): "and doesn't work!", a humorous comment. Note 136: examples of **proactive** SRE work are software automation, design consulting and launch coordination. Note 137: examples of **reactive** work are debugging, troubleshooting and handling on-call escalations (both attached to the sentence on p.276 about the natural mix of proactive and reactive work). Note 138: reference to old-time video games, tied to the term "powerups" (p.277). Note 139 (tied to the sentence in the reverse engineering class about binaries that self-report RPC and white-box/black-box monitoring, p.279): "follow the RPC" also applies to batch and pipeline systems; start with the operation that triggers the system (data arriving, transaction to validate). Note 140: pointer to "Life in the Trenches of healthcare.gov", tied to the account of a former Search SRE (p.280). Notes 141-142: examples of game master stimuli in role-play: "you are being paged by another team that brings more information" and "we are losing money fast, how do we stop the bleeding in the short term?". (SRE p.274, 276-277, 279-281, 283)

### Opening of Ch. 28 and Figure 28-1: blueprint from newcomer to on-call (p.274-275)
- **Opening questions (p.274).** Successful SRE teams are built on trust: you must trust that on-call colleagues know how the system works (note 135), diagnose atypical behavior, ask for help and react under pressure. Thinking about education only through the question "what does the newcomer need to learn to go on-call?" is essential but insufficient; the book also asks: how do current on-callers assess the newcomer's readiness? How can the enthusiasm and curiosity of the new people be harnessed so that current SREs also benefit? What activities benefit everyone's education and that everyone enjoys? (SRE p.274)
- **There is no single style (p.274).** Students have varied learning preferences; catering to only one style would be shortsighted, and there is no magic formula for all teams. Table 28-1 lists recommended training practices and the corresponding anti-patterns (the anti-patterns column appears truncated in the extracted PDF; the recommended practices are the eight cited above). (SRE p.274)
- **Figure 28-1 (p.275).** It is a blueprint for taking an SRE to on-call and beyond, from which each team picks the activities that serve it: the x axis goes from abstract to applied and the y axis is time. From top to bottom, the newcomer knows little about the system, so postmortems about past failures are a good starting point, and they can reverse engineer from the fundamentals, since they start from zero; after understanding more and doing hands-on work, they are ready for **shadow on-call** and to start fixing incomplete or outdated documentation. Joining on-call is a milestone after which learning becomes more nebulous and self-directed, so the activities from there on have dashed lines. (SRE p.275)

### Interrupts: types, factors, human cost and flow (Ch. 29, p.284-286)
- **How each type of load is managed (p.284-285).** Pages usually go to a dedicated primary on-call, a single engineer per shift (reduces interruption to the team and avoids the bystander effect), who can escalate if the problem is not well understood; the secondary is the backup, and in some rotations its only role is to contact the primary if pages fall into the void (it can even be from another team) and it may or may not consider itself on interrupts. Tickets: the primary or secondary works tickets during the shift, or there is a person dedicated to tickets outside on-call; they can be distributed randomly and automatically or handled ad hoc. Ongoing responsibilities: the on-call does pushes and flag flips, or they are assigned ad hoc, or the on-call takes on a responsibility that lasts beyond the shift week (multi-week rollout). (SRE p.284-285)
- **The five factors (p.285).** Some Google teams use these metrics to decide how to handle interrupts: (1) the SLO or expected response time of the interrupt; (2) the number of interrupts normally in backlog; (3) the severity; (4) the frequency; (5) the number of people available to handle a certain type of interrupt (for example, some teams require a certain amount of ticket work before joining on-call). Therefore "only response time and severity" is an incomplete list: there are five factors. (SRE p.285)
- **Human cost (p.285).** All these metrics serve to achieve the shortest possible response time, **without considering human costs**; surfacing the human and productivity cost is hard. Then come the "imperfect machines": humans get bored, have poorly understood "processors" (and sometimes UIs) and are not very efficient; recognizing the human element as "working as intended" and working around it would take far more space than is available, but some basic ideas help decide how interrupts should work. (SRE p.285)
- **Cognitive flow state (p.285).** The concept of *flow state* is widely accepted by those who work in software engineering, sysadmin, SRE and other areas that require periods of concentration: being "in the zone" increases productivity and also artistic and scientific creativity, and leads the person to master and improve the task or project. A disruptive interruption can pull the person out of flow, and the goal is to **maximize time in that state**. Flow also applies to less creative activities, requiring less skill, as long as the essential elements are present (clear goals, immediate feedback, sense of control and time distortion): household chores or driving. You can get in the zone with problems of **low skill and low difficulty** (like a repetitive video game) or of **high skill and high difficulty** (like an engineer's): the paths differ, the result is essentially the same. Therefore flow is not exclusive to creative and difficult tasks. (SRE p.285)
- **Creative and engaged flow (p.285).** The person works on a problem for some time, knows and is comfortable with the parameters, feels they can solve it, loses track of time and ignores interruptions as much as possible; maximizing this time is highly desirable, as it produces creative results and good volume of work, and the person is happier. Unfortunately many in SRE-type roles spend a good part of their time trying and failing to enter this mode, frustrated, or do not even try and sit "moldering" in the interrupted state. (SRE p.285)
- **"Angry Birds" flow (p.286).** People like to perform tasks they know how to do, one of the clearest paths to flow; some SREs enter flow when they are on-call (chasing causes, working with others, improving system health in a tangible way). The stress of most on-callers, however, comes from the volume of pages or from treating on-call as an interruption: trying to program or do projects while on-call or on full-time interrupts, living in a state of constant interruption (*interruptability*), which is extremely stressful. (SRE p.286)
- **When you only do interrupts (p.286).** When someone focuses full-time on interrupts, they stop being interruptions: making incremental improvements, killing tickets and fixing problems and outages becomes a clear set of goals, limits and clear feedback (close X bugs, or stop being paged); only distractions remain, and the projects become the distraction. Even if it is satisfying in the short term, in a mixed environment of projects and on-call people end up happier with **a balance between the two kinds of work**, and the **ideal balance varies from engineer to engineer**; it is important to know that some engineers may not know which balance motivates them best (or think they know and the manager disagrees). So "everyone prefers the same balance" contradicts p.286. (SRE p.286)
- **Distractibility (p.286).** The chapter's suggestions come from teams managed by the author and are aimed at managers and influencers: the focus is the structure of how the team handles interrupts, so that people are not set up to fail by the role or structure of the team. The example is SRE "Fred": even when not on-call or on interrupts on a Monday, he may receive a randomly distributed ticket with a deadline for today, a question from an on-call colleague about a component he masters, a user who bumps up the priority of a ticket from the previous week, a 3-4 week rollout assigned to him that goes wrong, or a user who asks directly "because Fred is helpful". Part of the distraction he resolves himself (closing email, turning off IM); part is caused by policy or by assumptions about interrupts and ongoing responsibilities. Some level of distraction is inevitable (people end up with bugs for which they are the main contact), but the team can manage the response to interrupts so that more people, on average, arrive in the morning and feel "undistractible". (SRE p.286)

### Ch. 30 with the book's examples: explaining the reasoning and guiding questions (p.291-293)
- **Team postmortem (p.292).** Instead of trying to fix past mistakes, take on the next postmortem: there will be an outage during the immersion; if you are not the on-call, join them to write a great blameless postmortem, which demonstrates how the SRE model makes fixes more permanent. If "Why me?" appears (typical of someone who sees the postmortem as retaliation, tied to the **Bad Apple Theory**, shown false by evidence from several disciplines, including aviation safety), the most effective formulation is to say that mistakes are inevitable in systems with many subtle interactions, that you trust the on-call's decision, and to ask them to write down what they were thinking at each moment to discover where the system misled them and where the cognitive demands were too high. (SRE p.292)
- **Classify the fires (p.292).** Two kinds, in the simplified model: fires that should not exist (ops work or toil) and fires that cause stress or furious typing but are part of the job; in both the team needs tools to control the fire. Classify them into toil and non-toil and present the list explaining why each is work to automate or acceptable overhead. (SRE p.292)
- **Phase 3, start with the basics (p.292).** Teams that confuse SRE with traditional ops cannot say why certain aspects bother them; instead of attacking point by point, work from the principles of the early chapters. The first goal is to write an **SLO**, if there is none: it gives a quantitative measure of the impact of outages and of the importance of a process change, and is "probably the most important lever" to get out of reactive ops. Without an SLO, no other advice in the chapter helps; without SLOs, read the SLO chapter and bring together technical leadership and management to arbitrate. (SRE p.292)
- **Resist the urge to fix it yourself (p.292-293).** Doing so reinforces the idea that "change is for others". Steps: find useful work a team member can do; explain clearly how it solves a postmortem problem permanently (even healthy teams produce short-range actions); be the reviewer of code and document changes; repeat for two or three issues. The other problems you identify go into a **bug report or document** for the team to consult: it distributes information and encourages writing documentation (the first victim of deadline pressure), always explaining the reasoning and emphasizing that good documentation avoids repeating old mistakes in a slightly new context. (SRE p.292-293)
- **Explain your reasoning (p.293).** Be prepared to be challenged ("explain why, right now, in the middle of the production meeting"); the way out is to explain all decisions, whether asked or not, referring back to the basics, to build the team's mental model and make it able to predict your comment in a review. Good explanations: "I'm not blocking the release because the tests are bad, but because the **error budget** defined for releases has run out"; "releases need to be safe to roll back because the SLO is tight and MTTR needs to be small, so in-depth diagnosis before rollback is not realistic". Insufficient explanations: "I think it's unsafe for each server to generate its own routing configuration because we can't see it"; "the automation should give up if it finds a conflicting deploy". These are probably correct decisions with poor or poorly explained reasoning, and the team may imitate the poor reasoning; better versions cite the risk of correlated failure from a bug in the code and the delay of rollback, and the simplifying assumption that all changes go through automation. (SRE p.293)
- **Guiding questions (p.293).** *Leading questions* are not *loaded questions*; they model the behavior of thinking from first principles, something a team in ops mode rejects. Book examples of a guiding question: "I see the TaskFailures alert fires often, but the on-calls usually do nothing; how does that impact the SLO?" and "this turnup procedure seems complicated; do you know why there are so many configuration files to update when creating a new instance?". Counterexamples (loaded questions): "what is going on with all those stuck old releases?" and "why does the Frobnitzer do so many things?". (SRE p.293)
- The passages on blameless postmortem culture (Ch. 15, p.126-128) deal with the **postmortem**: the two examples "finger pointing × blameless" (rewriting the entire backend) and the good practices of collaboration and review. p.127-128 does not deal with the tone of chat conversations or communication about releases; for tone of communication, the book relies on p.292-293. (SRE p.127-128, 292-293)
