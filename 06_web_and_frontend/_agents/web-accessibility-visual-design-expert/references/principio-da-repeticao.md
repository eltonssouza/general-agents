# The Principle of Repetition (NDDB, chapter 4, p.55-68)

Source: **NDDB**, Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, 4th ed. The only part of the book with complete content in the sample. `(NDDB p.N)` citations = printed page. Rewritten in the collection's own words; the book is print-centered, so anything about the web is **[extrapolation]**.

## 1. Definition and purpose

- **Principle** [from source]: repeat some aspect of the design throughout the whole piece. The repeated element can be a bold font, a rule (line), a bullet, a graphic element, a color, a shape, spatial relationships — anything the reader recognizes visually (NDDB p.55).
- **Repetition = consistency**, but goes beyond it: it is a **conscious** effort to unify the parts. In a 16-page brochure, if p.13 inherits nothing from p.4, the whole loses cohesion (NDDB p.55).
- Designers already use repetition without noticing (headings of the same size/weight, a rule in the footer, the same bullet in lists); the leap is to **push that discreet repetition until it becomes a visual key** that ties the publication together (NDDB p.55).
- **Purpose**: unify and add visual interest; an interesting piece is more likely to be read (NDDB p.68).
- The book's analogy: people in the same uniform are read as an organized team (NDDB p.55).

## 2. Mechanisms (what repetition does)

1. **Unity**: makes items look like they belong to the same set, even without being identical (NDDB p.56, 63).
2. **Guiding the eye**: in the card example, the repeated strong font makes the eye jump between the bold items, instead of "leaving" the card; designers use this to keep attention on the page (NDDB p.56).
3. **Organization and orientation**: helps organize information, guides the reader through the pages and connects disparate parts, even in a one-page piece (NDDB p.61).
4. **Sign of authority**: gives a sense of professionalism, since it shows a deliberate design decision, even in playful pieces (NDDB p.67).
5. **Highlighting the special**: with strong consistency, you can insert "surprises" to draw attention; if everything is inconsistent, nothing looks special (NDDB p.59).

## 3. How to get it (the author's recipe)

Order of work [from source] (NDDB p.57, 68): **find → strengthen → create**.

1. **Find** the consistency that already exists (headings, rules, bullets, numbering).
2. **Strengthen**: turn the discreet element into a stronger graphic. Examples from the book: replace headings in a plain font with a striking sans; replace a 1-point rule with a 4-point one (NDDB p.57, 68). Headings and subheadings are a good start since they are already consistent (NDDB p.57).
3. **Create** elements whose only function is to repeat: e.g. numbers in a numbered list in a distinct or reversed font, repeated in all lists (NDDB p.68).
4. Sources of new elements: clip art or a dingbat font; a simple element used in variations of size/color/angle; very similar objects; a motif extracted from a graphic (e.g. a heart) and applied to related materials; beautiful letters or characters at large sizes, in gray or a soft second color, at various angles (NDDB p.62-64). An element apparently unrelated to the subject also works if it looks **intentional, not random** (NDDB p.63).
5. **Overlapping** an element or taking it off the edges unifies pieces, foreground and background, or publications with a common theme (NDDB p.63).
6. **Deriving** new elements from the layout itself: the dashed letters inspired dashed ovals suggesting a sound wave (NDDB p.64).
7. The element **does not have to be an image**: consciously repeated spacing, rules, fonts and alignments count (NDDB p.65).
8. **Partial suggestion**: if the reader already knows an image from other materials, using just a piece is enough (NDDB p.66). Precondition [inference]: the element has already been established.

## 4. Repetition across pages and between pieces

- When opening the document, it should be immediate that p.3 and p.13 are from the same publication (NDDB p.58).
- Annotated examples (NDDB p.58-59): a double rule at the top of every page; the same font and the same space above in headings/subheadings; page numbers in the same place and font; a single rule in the footer; articles and photos starting at the same top guide; a triangular shape repeated in a list and a caption; a wide column taking the same space as two, keeping the outer edges.
- **Alignment anchor**: text can "hang from the clothesline" (align at the top) or "sit on the ground" (align at the bottom, ragged skyline-type top). **Choose one technique and use it consistently** (NDDB p.58).
- **Identity package** (card, letterhead, envelope): strong repetition within each piece and between pieces, so the recipient recognizes the same sender; the letter can be aligned to an element of the stationery (NDDB p.60). Several one-page pieces that form a set require repetition (NDDB p.61).
- In the exemplified résumé, the following are repeated: bold font, light font, square bullets, indents, spacing and alignments; the cover letter could reuse these elements (NDDB p.61).

## 5. Variety within structure (NDDB p.67)

Repeating is not duplicating. In the example, headings have different colors but the same font; illustrations have different styles but the same mood. Condition: **enough repetitions that the differences look like choices, not a mess**. Recipes with the same format and strong alignments give a structure that authorizes flexibility. [inference]: varying one attribute requires keeping others fixed.

## 6. Worked example: lecture ad (NDDB p.65)

- "Typical" version: centered Times New Roman, typewriter quotation marks, grouped information, but weak centered alignment and an attempt to fill the corners.
- Redone versions: decide on a **focus** (the speaker or the topic); in one of them the black bar appears repeated in a thinner version in the footer — that is, a repeated element can be very simple and still stitch the piece together.
- The book notes that alignment and contrast also operate there, and that contrast is the topic of the next chapter (NDDB p.65); the text of that chapter is **not** in the sample.

## 7. What to avoid (NDDB p.68)

- Repeating so much that it becomes annoying or overwhelming. Analogy: a black dress with a black hat and *touches* of red (shoes, lipstick, brooch) works; all red becomes excess and confuses the focus.
- Losing sight of **contrast**: the book says to consider its value, especially the contrast of types (NDDB p.68; the content is outside the sample).
- Repetition that looks accidental (NDDB p.63) or insufficient repetitions to sustain the variety (NDDB p.67).

## 8. "Designer Eye" exercises (p.62-64)

The book proposes naming repeated elements in example cards; the suggested answers are on p.227-228, **outside the sample**. Do not invent the official answers.

## 9. Bridge to the web [extrapolation, not from the book]

These bridges are in neither source; always use them with the label:

- Consistency of headings, spacing, icons and components across the pages of a site corresponds to the "same element repeated between p.3 and p.13" (NDDB p.58).
- Design tokens/CSS variables/reusable components are a technical means of ensuring repetition; the book does not talk about CSS.
- The same navigation and label pattern can help predictability for everyone, including users with cognitive disabilities (the relation to WAI p.3 is **[inference]**, not a claim of the sources).
- The book focuses on **printed pieces**; when applying it to interfaces, check against WCAG, which is outside the sources.
