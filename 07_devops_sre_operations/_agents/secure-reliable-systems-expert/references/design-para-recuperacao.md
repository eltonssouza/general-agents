## Ch. 9 — Design for recovery (BSRS p.219-251)

### Premise (BSRS p.219-220)
- [from source] All complex systems have reliability problems and attackers try to make them fail; plan for recovery early; recovering can be complex: rolling back an unstable release can reintroduce vulnerabilities and rolling out a patch can introduce reliability problems; the fastest wins the race against attackers but limits testing (BSRS p.219-220). Only conscious design decisions prepare the system; do not discover the lack of preparation during the incident (BSRS p.220).

### What we are recovering from (BSRS p.220-222)
- [from source] Four categories: random errors (hardware fails; bit flips, silent corruption by a faulty instruction on one core, external events such as an earthquake or power outage; more hardware, more errors) (BSRS p.220); accidental errors (well-intentioned humans; Google's internal analysis of outages from 2015 to 2018 indicated a significant fraction, not the majority, caused by unilateral human action without a safety check) (BSRS p.221); software errors (a special, late case of accidental error; they can mimic other types, such as automation without a safety check mimicking an attacker; they magnify other errors) (BSRS p.221); malicious actions (privileged insiders, social engineering, coercion, third-party compromise; the design mitigation is the same for an insider or an attacker with a credential) (BSRS p.221-222). Do not spend time classifying edge cases of error; be ready to recover (BSRS p.222).

### Principle: go as fast as possible, guarded by policy (BSRS p.222-226)
- [from source] Pressure for fast recovery; fast change mechanisms can accelerate the error and, in a compromise, premature actions can alert the adversary (BSRS p.222). Design the update system to operate as fast as you may need and add controls to restrict the rate according to risk policy; decoupling capability from the rate and frequency policy allows adapting to policy changes, to new information in the middle of the rollout (vulnerability already exploited) and to sudden events (Shellshock, Heartbleed) (BSRS p.223). The emergency push system should be the regular system "at most", and the emergency rollback the same system; untested emergency practices do not work; exercise them frequently (BSRS p.223-224).
- [from source] Isolate the rate limiting mechanism: an independent microservice, simple and rigorously testable (e.g., issues a short-lived cryptographic token attesting review and approval of the change), also a good point for change logs; redundant rate limiting code elsewhere is a sign of insecure design (BSRS p.224). "Base/golden" image case: monthly rollout of the base image to the whole fleet; with growing security patch exceptions, the monthly policy stopped making sense; solution: per-package release units, an API that specifies the exact set of packages per machine, decoupling rollout and rate, config store and rollout actuator; result: different speeds per package, test machines following the newest, use for emergency releases by adjusting rate limits (BSRS p.224-226).

### Principle: limit dependencies on external notions of time (BSRS p.226-228)
- [from source] Clock time is state; embedding wall-clock time threatens recovery (replaying signed transactions with already-expired certificates fails if the process does not consider the original date); external notions of time you do not control (Y2K, Unix epoch rollover, certificates with validity too far out), NTP in the clear or unauthenticated; fixed dates in code are a time-bomb code smell (BSRS p.226). Prefer rates, manually advanced notions of progress (epoch or version numbers) and validity lists (BSRS p.226). ALTS does not use expiration in certificates, it uses revocation through vectors of serial number ranges (BSRS p.226-227).
- [from source] Disabling certificate validation to recover is a cure worse than the disease; better to omit the SSH key's expiration than to skip validation; exception: deliberately time-limited access (reauthenticate daily), with a repair path that does not depend on the clock; absolute time complicates recovery from crashes and databases that expect monotonic time; logs with inaccurate clocks increase errors (BSRS p.227). Advanced epoch or version (ratchet, forward only; a service announces or starts the advance; the advance can be stopped in a crisis); beware of overflow and how many intermediate values can be skipped; an adversary can speed up the advance or cause rollover; choose a large range (64 bits) with a rate limit backstop (at most 1 increment per second), an exception to "as fast as possible" (BSRS p.227-228). Even when you want to wait for elapsed time, measure elapsed time without time of day (BSRS p.228).

### Principle: rollbacks are a tradeoff between security and reliability (BSRS p.228-235)
- [from source] The first recovery step is usually to mitigate by reverting suspect changes to the last known good state; but when fixing a vulnerability, after the patch is stable one must prevent attackers from rolling back to reintroduce it, while keeping the option of voluntary rollback (the patch is also code and can have bugs) (BSRS p.228). In application software, deny lists (names of unwanted versions: strings, numbers or hashes) or allow lists in the release policy (BSRS p.228-229).
- [from source] Self-updating components (package daemon that overwrites itself; firmware such as BIOS that rewrites itself): extreme policies and failures: allowing arbitrary rollbacks (insecure) and never allowing rollbacks (unreliable, always requires a new forward version); alternatives: deny lists, SVNs and MASVNs, signing key rotation; it is assumed updates are signed with a signature covering the image and version metadata; implement one capability at a time; every healthy organization should have key rotation, deny list and MASVN serve high-speed responses (BSRS p.229-230).
- [from source] Deny lists: embedded in the component, it protects only against accidental errors; vulnerable to "unzipping" (attacker rolls back incrementally to a vulnerable version); preventing rollback for critical updates leads to the extreme of "never"; good for incidents with several updates in flight and for adding versions quickly (BSRS p.230). Better: ComponentState[DenyList] outside the component, surviving upgrades and downgrades, the union of the lists known per release; allows deciding the tradeoff by policy; disadvantages: it must be monitored outside the central deploy's intent, removal of an entry by accident opens the door to unzipping, unbounded growth requires garbage collection (BSRS p.230-231).
- [from source] Security Version Number (SVN) and Minimum Acceptable Security Version Number (MASVN): SVN independent of other notions of version, comparable and compact, incremented when applying a critical fix and proving stability; MASVN is a low-water mark below which the system must never operate, an ordered value (not a hash), preferably an integer; an update is allowed if Release[SVN] >= ComponentState[MASVN]; maintenance occurs when the new release initializes (ComponentState[MASVN] = max(self[MASVN], ComponentState[MASVN])); the scheme emulates "arbitrary rollback" (never change MASVN) or "never rollback" (MASVN in lockstep with SVN); practice: release i includes the patch and raises SVN, without changing MASVN (patches also have bugs); once stable, release i+1 raises the MASVN, making the patch mandatory (BSRS p.231-233). There can be an API to increment ComponentState[MASVN] by command of the central deploy in an active compromise or severe vulnerability (BSRS p.233); ComponentState is delicate, local to the component (BSRS p.234). Combine a fast deny list (incident) with MASVN maintenance in calm times to garbage collect the deny list (BSRS p.234). Similar example: Intel microcode SVN (note) (BSRS p.232).
- [from source] Signing key rotation: removing the old or compromised key from the set of trusted public keys invalidates old releases; sudden key change exposes reliability problems; gradual alternative: introduce key k+1 alongside k, accepting both, and retire k after stability (requires multiple signatures per artifact and multiple verification keys); rotation is exercised regularly (BSRS p.234). Serious attack: an attacker who temporarily controls release management signs a release with maximum MASVN and renders the scheme unusable; response: revoke the compromised key in new releases signed by a new key and dedicated (delicate) logic to reset the abnormally high MASVN, revoking those releases as soon as they have served their purpose (BSRS p.235).
- [from source] Firmware and hardware: OTP/fuses can implement MASVN only forward (reliability risk); additional layer: a small bootloader with its own MASVN logic and a mutable region; public keys in OTP with a limited number of rotations and revocations, same bootloader solution; spare parts with old firmware may not verify updates signed only by new keys: walk through a sequence of upgrades that trust old and new keys, or multiple signatures per release (new images carry a signature from the old key so that old firmware can recover); size the space for keys and signatures (BSRS p.235-236).

### Principle: use an explicit revocation mechanism (BSRS p.236-240)
- [from source] Revocation for an active compromise (e.g., an SSH key pair for cluster access in the attacker's hands) allows regaining control, but, once it exists, accidental or malicious behavior has consequences; immediate revocation is not always the best choice (Ch.17) (BSRS p.236-237).
- [from source] Centralized revocation service: a central certificate validity database is an authoritative record and requires monitoring; if it goes down, everything that depends on it goes down; strong temptation to fail open: be careful (BSRS p.237). Fail open avoids lockout but bypasses protection; partial failure too (if the validity database depends on a time/epoch service that suffers DoS, old revoked credentials become valid again); alternative: distribute known good revocation lists, with local cache, and nodes proceed with the best understanding of the world, far safer than time-out and fail open (BSRS p.237).
- [from source] Handling emergencies directly in authorized_keys or KRL per server scales poorly and spreads the truth; manage keys and certificates centrally, distributing state through a revocation list with the normal mechanisms (including rate limiting) (BSRS p.238). Explicit revocation removes the dependence on an accurate notion of time (wrong time re-enables old certificates or invalidates correct ones); it depends on root key files and revocation lists that you control and monitor (BSRS p.238).
- [from source] Revocation at scale: a partially inside attacker can try to revoke all credentials; updating the KRL by blindly replacing the file is dangerous; safeguard: the target server evaluates the new KRL and refuses an update that revokes its own credentials (a KRL that revokes all hosts is ignored by all; in the worst case half the machines keep working) (BSRS p.239); avoid risky exceptions: a rarely used emergency list fails when needed; shard the revocation list and always use the same incremental update mechanism; beware of special accounts that bypass revocation, attractive targets (BSRS p.239-240).

### Principle: know the intended state, down to the bytes (BSRS p.240-245)
- [from source] Recovering requires returning to a known good state: encode the intended state and reduce mutable state at each layer; it is the basis of automation, security, intrusion detection and recovery (BSRS p.240). State includes everything the system needs, even "stateless" (code version, port, auto-start), plus configuration and environment (BSRS p.240).
- [from source] Host management (Google): each machine watches its filesystem keeping a map of names and cryptographic checksums, sent to a central service that compares with the assigned packages and lists deviations; unifies recovery (bit corrupted by a cosmic ray, rollout of a component that alters another's file, local change outside the tool and review); simpler and more disruptive alternative: reimage; in-memory state: each package requires an idempotent post_install command (e.g., restart the SSH daemon) and pre_rm; Google's package system guarantees that package changes succeed or the machine returns fully to the previous state (BSRS p.240-241). The deviation record also helps forensics: an attacker who leaves shellcode that the system reverted will have difficulty erasing traces (BSRS p.241-242). All state changes are equal at this level (rollback of a failed canary and emergency rollout of bash are routine), with rate limits against collisions (BSRS p.242).
- [from source] Device firmware: at least track versions and ideally all settings; same system and processes; activation check (script or binary in the package that verifies it is correctly installed and reports deviations), delegates to subsystem owners; automation has no opinion about intermediate versions (BIOS v1 to v3) (BSRS p.242). Examples: time hardware (GNSS, atomic clocks, Spanner) with two firmwares and leap second bugs; BIOS with boot order preferring SATA over USB, the database of keys authorized to sign BIOS, forgetting to specify the secondary boot device (BSRS p.243). State includes inactive code or config that can become active during recovery (BSRS p.243).
- [from source] Global services: storage, naming and identity are the hardest to recover; when creating global singletons (such as Spanner, Hadoop) support multiple instances from the first deployment; capture the state of how the service is created (imperative or declarative turnup, e.g., Terraform, as TDD captures behavior); containers capture a good part of the state by default, but restoring from scratch exercises a complex chain of dependencies: capacity (machines, disk, network, cloud quota) and circular dependencies; test disasters under controlled conditions (BSRS p.243-244).
- [from source] Persistent data: "nobody cares about backups, only about restores"; backups need the same level of integrity protection as primary storage (an insider can alter the backup and force a restore); signatures are useless if the restore does not validate them or if access control does not restrict who signs; compartmentalize the protection to restore only the corrupted subset (0.01%) with integrity validation without reading the rest; chunk size is a tradeoff between storage, computation and MTTR; data migrations exercise and give confidence in the restore system (similar to a low-priority partial restore); restores must not resurrect data that is supposed to be destroyed (legal retention and deletion); distinguish erasing keys from erasing encrypted data (requires compartmentalizing keys compatible with granular deletion) (BSRS p.244-245).

### Principle: design for continuous testing and validation (BSRS p.245-246)
- [from source] Test recovery processes, which perform unusual tasks under unknown conditions; a good test design reveals assumptions (e.g., a single global instance); example of the key management protocol on Arm and x86, UEFI and bare-metal, MSVC, Clang and GCC compilers: implement the core logic once in a portable way, unit test it and create interfaces to read and write flash bytes, key storage and monitoring primitives, to simulate failures; humans are unpredictable: validate the readability of instructions and the interoperability of recovery flows (BSRS p.245-246).

### Emergency access (BSRS p.246-250)
- [from source] Emergency access = the absolute minimum of technologies that responders use to reach central administrative interfaces (root on network equipment, OS, consoles) and to communicate during the worst outages, preserving access controls as much as possible (networks, access controls and recovery tools) (BSRS p.246). Be aware of the layers outside your control: "you are only as available as the sum of your dependencies"; Google's strategy: self-contained critical services in geographically distributed racks, with remote access, efficient local communication, alternate network and fortified points; in a global outage smaller regions try to fix things and the recovery expands radially (BSRS p.247).
- [from source] Access controls: they cannot be a single point of failure; remote access credentials do not derive from dynamic SSO or federated IdP, unless replaced by low-dependency implementations; credential lifetime is a dilemma (short credentials become a time bomb if the outage lasts longer; proactive scheduled issuance may expire at the start of the outage); a network with dynamic authorization (SDN) requires more static alternatives and monitoring to locate where access breaks (BSRS p.247-248). Google's fallback for zero trust: alternate offline credentials and alternate authentication and authorization algorithms with equivalent security and far fewer dependencies, restricted to those who need immediate access (BSRS p.248).
- [from source] Communication: choose technology with few dependencies (Google Chat, Skype, Slack as examples); if outsourced, verify reach; phone bridges as an old option (increasingly IP); a self-run IRC is reliable and self-contained but lacks security aspects; verify providers' authentication and confidentiality (BSRS p.248-249). Responder habits: if end-to-end usability is not prioritized, the human makes the breakglass ineffective; minimize the distinction between normal and emergency processes (Google centralized on Chrome and extensions with an emergency mode); integrate emergency access into the routine of whoever is on-call, with a minimum period between mandatory exercises, lead notifications and waiver when there is equivalent activity; documentation (policies, how-tos, architecture diagrams) available (BSRS p.249-250).

### Unexpected benefits and conclusion (BSRS p.250-251)
- [from source] A server with firmware authentication, rollback, locking and attestation serves compromise recovery and "bare metal" hosting that wipes and resells machines; it reduces attention to the supply chain of components whose integrity is recovered automatically (the first operation is to run the recovery procedure), exercising the recovery capability regularly; recovery is an advanced topic whose value appears when the system leaves the intended state; since operating with an error budget is recommended, systems will be in an error state regularly (BSRS p.250).
- [from source] Conclusion: flexibility in deploy rate; rollback capability is essential but sometimes rollback to insecure or old versions must be prevented; understand, monitor and reproduce the state (software versions, memory, clock); emergency access as a last resort; balance policy vs procedure, central source vs local functions and expected vs actual state (BSRS p.250-251).
