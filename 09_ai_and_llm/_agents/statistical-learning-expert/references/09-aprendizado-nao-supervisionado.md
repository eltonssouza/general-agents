## 1. The challenge
- `[from source]` Unsupervised learning = only features X₁…X_p, no response; it is more subjective, part of exploratory analysis, and **there is no universal validation mechanism** (there is no "true answer" to check against) (ISLR p.388-389). Applications: subgroups of patients/genes, shopper segmentation, recommendation (ISLR p.389).

## 2. PCA (principal component analysis)
- `[from source]` With p variables there are p(p − 1)/2 scatterplots (p = 10: 45); PCA finds a low-dimensional representation that retains as much variation as possible (ISLR p.390). The **first component** is the normalized linear combination (Σφ²ⱼ₁ = 1) of highest variance, Z₁ = φ₁₁X₁ + … + φ_p1X_p (10.1); the φ are the *loadings*, the values zᵢ₁ the *scores*; (10.3) is solved by eigendecomposition, assuming centered columns; the second component is the one of highest variance uncorrelated with the first (equivalent to φ₂ ⟂ φ₁); there are at most min(n − 1, p) components (ISLR p.390-392).
- `[from source]` USArrests (50 states, 4 variables, standardized): PC1 weights Murder, Assault and Rape almost equally (general measure of crime) and PC2 weights mainly UrbanPop (urbanization); the *biplot* shows scores and loadings (ISLR p.392-394). Another reading: the first M components give the **best M-dimensional approximation** (in the Euclidean sense), x_ij ≈ Σ z_im φ_jm (10.5), exact if M = min(n − 1, p) (ISLR p.394-395). ESL: reconstruction f(λ) = μ + V_qλ, SVD X = UDVᵀ, components = columns of UD (ESL p.553-555); on the digit "3" (256 pixels), ~50 components explain 90% and 12 explain 63% (ESL p.556).
- `[from source]` **Scale**: always center; standardizing (standard deviation 1) is recommended, since PCA depends on scale (unlike regression); unstandardized variances in USArrests: 18.97; 87.73; 6,945.16; 209.5, and Assault would dominate; if the variables have the same units (e.g. gene expression), one may skip standardizing (ISLR p.395-397). The loadings are unique up to sign (ISLR p.397).
- `[from source]` **Proportion of variance explained (PVE)**: total variance Σ_j (1/n)Σ_i x²_ij; PVE_m = Σ_i(Σ_j φ_jm x_ij)²/Σ_jΣ_i x²_ij; the PVEs sum to 1 (in the data, PC1 62.0% and PC2 24.7%, almost 87% together) (ISLR p.397-398). **How many components?** There is no objective method: look at the elbow of the *scree plot* and at interpretability; but, if the components are used in a supervised model (PCR), M is a tuning parameter chosen by CV (ISLR p.398-399). Components can reduce noise in other techniques (ISLR p.400).

## 3. K-means
- `[from source]` Partitions into K disjoint clusters, K pre-specified; minimizes the total within-cluster variation, Σ_k (1/|C_k|) Σ_{i,i′∈C_k} Σ_j (x_ij − x_i′j)² (10.9-10.11) (ISLR p.401-402). There are about K^n partitions; **Algorithm 10.1** (random initial assignment; iterate: compute centroids, reassign to the nearest) never increases the objective, by identity (10.12) (the sum of pairwise distances divided by |C_k| is 2Σ‖x − x̄_k‖²), and converges to a **local optimum**; run with **several random initializations** and choose the lowest objective (235.8 in the example) (ISLR p.403-405). Choosing K "is far from simple" (ISLR p.404).
- `[from source]` ESL: W(C) = ½ΣΣΣd (14.28) and T = W + B (14.29); number of assignments S(N, K): S(10, 4) = 34,105 and S(19, 4) ≈ 10¹⁰ (14.30); greedy search leads to local optima (ESL p.527-528). K-means uses squared Euclidean distance: W(C) = Σ N_k Σ‖x_i − x̄_k‖² (14.31); Algorithm 14.1; Hartigan-Wong variant; use many initializations and choose the lowest objective; the regions are a Voronoi tessellation; **Gaussian mixtures are a "soft K-means"** via EM (ESL p.528-529). K-medoids requires only distances (ESL p.536-537).
- `[from source]` ESL, choosing K: W_K decreases with K even on test data, so **CV does not work**; one looks for an "elbow" in W_K; the **gap statistic** (Tibshirani et al., 2001) compares log W_K with that of uniform data and chooses the smallest K whose gap is within one standard deviation of the gap at K + 1 (ESL p.537-539). The choice of K depends on the goal (segmentation requires a K defined by the problem) (ESL p.537).

## 4. Hierarchical clustering
- `[from source]` Does not require K; produces a **dendrogram** (view of all clusterings, from 1 to n); in *bottom-up* (agglomerative) each observation starts as a cluster and the two most similar are fused until one remains (Algorithm 10.2) (ISLR p.405, 409-410). The **height** of the fusion measures dissimilarity; horizontal proximity does **not** indicate similarity (there are 2^{n−1} reorderings); one cuts horizontally to obtain clusters (ISLR p.406-408). The hierarchy presupposes **nested** clusters, which may be unrealistic (e.g. best split into 2 by gender and into 3 by nationality), and can give a worse result than K-means (ISLR p.409).
- `[from source]` **Linkage** (Table 10.2): *complete* (largest pairwise dissimilarity), *single* (smallest; produces chain-like clusters, "trailing"), *average* (mean) and *centroid* (can cause **inversions**); *average* and *complete* are preferred (more balanced dendrograms); the dendrogram depends strongly on the linkage (ISLR p.410-411). ESL: (14.41)-(14.43); single has *chaining*; complete has compact clusters but can violate "closeness"; average is a compromise but depends on the numerical scale of d (a monotone transformation can change the result), whereas single and complete are invariant to monotone transformations; on the microarray, single gave unbalanced groups (ESL p.542-544). ESL reminds us that hierarchical methods **impose** hierarchical structure even if none exists; the cophenetic correlation coefficient measures fidelity and the cophenetic distance is ultrametric (14.40) (ESL p.540-542). Divisive is less studied (ESL p.545).
- `[from source]` **Dissimilarity**: Euclidean or **correlation-based** (focuses on the shape of the profiles, not the magnitude); in the online store example, Euclidean distance would group low-volume shoppers, and correlation is better; standardizing variables (socks versus computers) changes the result and also applies to K-means (ISLR p.411-414).

## 5. Practices and cautions (ISLR 10.3.3)
- `[from source]` "Small decisions with big consequences": standardizing, the dissimilarity, the linkage, where to cut, K (ISLR p.414-415). **Clusters are always found**: one must validate whether they are real subgroups or noise; cluster p-values have no consensus (ISLR p.415). K-means and hierarchical force all observations into clusters (outliers distort; mixture models are an alternative) and are not robust to perturbations (ISLR p.415). Recommendations: several choices of parameters, cluster subsets to check robustness, report the clusters as a **starting point for a hypothesis**, preferably validated on independent data (ISLR p.416).

## Pitfalls
- Not standardizing before PCA or clustering (ISLR p.395-397, 413-414).
- Running K-means only once (ISLR p.403-404).
- Reading horizontal proximity in the dendrogram as similarity (ISLR p.406-407).
- Using CV to choose K (ESL p.538).
- Treating clusters as truth rather than as a hypothesis (ISLR p.415-416).
