## Ch. 7 — Design for a changing landscape (BSRS p.157-177)

### Premise and types of security change (BSRS p.157-158)
- [from source] The ability to adapt while maintaining SLOs depends on frequent builds, releases with automated tests, containers, and microservices; many of the chapter's practices can be adopted at late stages of the lifecycle (BSRS p.157). The number of devices and vulnerabilities grows, and the expectations of users and regulators rise; infrastructure must be changed often and quickly while maintaining high reliability (BSRS p.157-158).
- [from source] Types: in response to incidents (Ch.18), to new vulnerabilities, product or feature changes, internal ones to improve posture, external ones (e.g., new regulatory requirements); special cases: an optional feature as a step toward making it mandatory requires feedback from early adopters; swapping a dependency requires checking that it meets security requirements (BSRS p.158).

### Characteristics of every change (BSRS p.158-159)
- [from source] Incremental (small and independent, without coupling refactorings); Documented (the how and why: requirements, systems and teams in scope, lessons from the proof of concept, rationale for decisions, points of contact); Tested (unit, integration where possible, peer review); Isolated (feature flags; the binary must not change behavior with the flag off); Qualified (normal release process with stages before real traffic); Staged (gradual, with instrumentation for canary and before/after comparison) (BSRS p.158-159). Conscious tradeoff: "slow and steady" is worth more than risking downtime or data loss (BSRS p.159).

### Architecture decisions that make changes easier (BSRS p.159-163)
- [from source] Keep dependencies up to date and rebuild frequently: large open source projects have response plans and backports; up-to-date dependencies let you apply a critical patch directly without merging a backlog; patches only enter the environment when you rebuild, so frequent rebuilds and redeploys prepare emergency rollouts (BSRS p.159-160).
- [from source] Frequent releases with automated tests: smaller releases have fewer changes, are less prone to rollback, and are easier to debug; automate tests and validation so good releases are pushed and deficient ones blocked (BSRS p.160).
- [from source] Containers: decouple binaries and libraries from the host OS (smaller host, portable applications, can be protected independently; kernel patch without touching the container); immutable (rebuild and redeploy instead of SSH); patch images in the registry and launch them as a unit with the same rollout process as code (monitoring, canary, tests); blue/green avoids downtime; immutability gives content addressability: knowing what is running and finding susceptible versions via the registry, instead of scanning clusters; monitor the age of containers in production and enforce that only recent builds are deployed (BSRS p.160-161).
- [from source] Microservices: scale, balance, and roll out independently; several independent, sequential defenses (defense in depth); they make zero trust easier (no trust for being on the same network; trend toward a segmented network down to per-service segmentation); convergence of security tooling (common cryptographic libraries and monitoring, critical services separated and maintained by few owners); requires containment to keep services as simple as possible; allows addressing security early and in a standardized way (BSRS p.161-162).
- [from source] Example, Google Front End (GFE): frontend layer as a microservice that does not expose services directly to the internet, terminates HTTP(S), TCP, and TLS, offers DDoS countermeasures, and routes/balances; benefits: global load balancing (redirect traffic during a datacenter outage), independently scalable layers, an overload mitigation point (drop or absorb connections before the backends), adoption of new protocols and requirements (IPv6, certificate termination; an SSL renegotiation control in the GFE protected all services behind it; ALTS adoption via the RPC library) (BSRS p.162-163). In the cloud, similar benefits come from microservices, layers of controls, and service mesh; separation of data plane (requests) and control plane (policy and configuration) (BSRS p.163).

### Different speeds and deadlines (BSRS p.163-165)
- [from source] Factors that determine speed: severity (a critical vulnerability that is exploited and applicable to your infrastructure calls for a fast patch; accelerated deadlines are disruptive; ideally apply the critical patch independently so as not to accelerate other rollouts); dependent systems and teams (other teams, vendor patches, clients patched before the server); sensitivity (a nonessential change can go team by team and avoid critical windows such as shopping season); deadline (regulatory date or embargo) (BSRS p.163-164).
- [from source] Embargoed vulnerabilities: if applying the patch would break the embargo, wait for the public announcement; for the response before the announcement, agree on a date with the parties (e.g., a Monday) (BSRS p.164). There is no fixed rule: the same adjustment can take weeks in one organization and months in another; full adoption has a long tail (BSRS p.164).

### Short term: zero-day (BSRS p.165-168)
- [from source] Zero-day: known to at least some attackers, undisclosed or undiscovered by the provider; patch nonexistent or not applied; ways to discover vulnerabilities: code review, internal scanning, fuzzing, pentests, infrastructure scans, bug bounty (BSRS p.165). Zero-days are not necessarily the most exploited: first, be up to date with the "top hits" of recent critical vulnerabilities (BSRS p.165).
- [from source] Steps: triage of severity and impact (which systems use the binary, version in production, monitor for active exploitation); obtain the patch and verify it fixes the issue (a working exploit helps, but failing to exploit does not prove it is safe; the patch may cover only one exploit of a larger class); roll out gradually, even if accelerated, in hours or days, with the same tests and canary; if it cannot be fixed, mitigate by restricting access to the vulnerable component (temporary or permanent); if an adequate mitigation already exists, no action may be needed (BSRS p.165-166).
- [from source] Shellshock example (09/24/2014): the incident response team started the "Black Swan" protocol; identify systems and risk, communicate internally, patch as fast as possible, communicate externally; low-risk servers were patched with an automated rollout much faster than usual; higher-risk workstations too; a few nonstandard servers and high-risk legacy infrastructure required manual intervention with notifications to the teams; software to detect vulnerable systems was created and incorporated into standard monitoring (BSRS p.166-167).
- [from source] Shellshock lessons: standardize software distribution (the owner of a nonstandard service is responsible for maintaining and patching the alternative); use public distribution standards; have an accelerated emergency push mechanism without skipping validation; monitor progress and identify unpatched systems and whether there is active exploitation; prepare external communication early; have a reusable response plan (start from the postmortem of a previous event); know the nonstandard systems (BSRS p.167-168).

### Medium term: improving security posture (BSRS p.168-171)
- [from source] Proactive changes rarely require a sudden rollout; gradual action plan with success criteria per phase; it cannot be expressed as a percentage: phase by who is affected (group by group: team, system, or set of users) and by which change (BSRS p.168). Two philosophies for the initial population: the easiest case (proves value, wins buy-in) or the hardest (finds more bugs and edge cases); choose according to leadership support; a proof of concept guides; the team making the change must live the change (dogfooding) (BSRS p.168-169).
- [from source] Incremental change: progressively stricter requirements, opt-in before mandatory, dry run in alert or audit mode before enforcement, to find wrong scope and hard cases (BSRS p.169).
- [from source] FIDO/U2F example at Google: OTP is vulnerable to phishing; starting in 2011 it evaluated 2FA methods and chose U2F tokens; security, privacy, and usability requirements defined up front (fast enough to be hard to use wrong, not delay SREs during outages, simple APIs for developers, no additional hardware per account, easy to learn and recover); pilots showed shorter authentication time and negligible failures (BSRS p.169-170). Rollout starting in 2013: self-service, "nano" keys, pickup at any TechStop, registration on a self-service site, trust on first use (TOFU) using OTP the first time, several keys per user, user-updatable firmware; focus on applications used daily, working with vendors, discovering the long tail by tracking clients that requested OTP; in 2015 reminders and OTP blocking, an exception for the web OTP generator after verification with a security key; rollout completed in 2015 (BSRS p.170-171).
- [from source] General lessons: a solution that works for everyone (accessibility, visually impaired users); easy to learn and frictionless; self-service; tangible proof of benefit; fast feedback on noncompliance; track progress and the long tail with dashboards (BSRS p.171).

### Long term: external demand (BSRS p.172-174)
- [from source] Multiyear efforts require clear goals and measurable progress, documentation (continuity, handoff, leadership support), instrumentation and dashboards, coverage of compliance checks (like test coverage) preferably self-service, a single source of truth for executive communication; keep the pace with finite goals and examples of impact; if the change is not mandatory, 80% to 90% adoption already reduces risk measurably and counts as success (BSRS p.172).
- [from source] Example, increasing HTTPS use (Chrome, Let's Encrypt, and others): data-driven approach (usage by region, research on HTTPS UI, site behavior, developer case studies), monitoring over several years, gradual warnings in Chrome with telemetry to avoid adverse effects, overcommunication through all channels, regional focus (Japan), business incentives (features such as Service Worker restricted to HTTPS); cited result: Chrome users spend over 90% of their time on HTTPS sites (previously ~70% on Windows and 37% on Android) (BSRS p.172-173). Lessons: understand the ecosystem, overcommunicate, tie security to business incentives, build industry consensus (BSRS p.174). [DATED] HTTPS numbers are from the time of the book; HTTPS is even more prevalent today.

### When plans change (BSRS p.174-175)
- [from source] Speed up (active exploitation) with caution: speeding up by breaking systems improves neither security nor reliability; reorder to cover the highest-risk systems first, rate-limit operations, or take a critical system offline; slow down (errors above expected): if it does not resolve, roll back, debug, and relaunch; reasons to postpone or accelerate: embargo, public announcement or leak, little impact (patch only what is affected), third-party dependency (OS patch, hardware on backorder) (BSRS p.174-175). Have an action plan for each stage in case the embargo breaks (BSRS p.175).

### Example: Heartbleed (BSRS p.176-177)
- [from source] OpenSSL heartbeat bug (introduced in Dec/2011; access to 64 KB of private memory); co-discovered in Apr/2014 by a Googler and an engineer at Codenomicon; early public announcement (first bug with a name and logo, great media interest); Google had already quietly patched the few external systems that handled TLS; once public, exploits appeared quickly (e.g., Metasploit), automated scanning found vulnerable systems, teams were notified and tracked in a central spreadsheet; the memory leak implied rotating private keys (BSRS p.176).
- [from source] Lessons: plan for the worst case (embargo broken or lifted early), prepare a fast rollout at scale (continuous builds, canary), rotate keys and secrets regularly (limits blast radius; practice so that replacing compromised keys is not heroic), maintain a communication channel with internal and external users (BSRS p.176-177).

### Conclusion (BSRS p.177)
- [from source] Distinguish types of change so teams know what is expected; start small or with volunteers, have a feedback loop, and make the change self-service; frequent releases, containers, and microservices make both proactive improvements and emergency mitigation easier (BSRS p.177).
