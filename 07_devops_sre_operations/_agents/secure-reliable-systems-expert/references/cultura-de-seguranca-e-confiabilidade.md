## Ch. 21 — Building a culture of security and reliability (BSRS p.507-531)

### Premise (BSRS p.507-509)
- [from source] Opening questions: does the new employee know the importance of security and reliability and the role they play? The chapter describes aspects of a healthy culture, how good change practices affect culture, and how to influence leadership and teams; it is a guide, not something to adopt in full, and Google does not practice all of it perfectly either (BSRS p.507). Culture is everyone's responsibility, from the CEO to those who design and maintain systems (BSRS p.507-508).
- [from source] The "Big Deal" scenario: discovering an attacker in the systems on the eve of a big deal; a healthy security culture encourages reporting the incident immediately despite the risk; at the same time an accidental push from staging to production takes revenue down for over an hour: a reliability culture encourages redesigning the process; norms should encourage blameless postmortems; being hacked once hurts, twice is worse; 100% is never the right reliability goal (error budgets and safe push controls balance reliability and velocity [route to sre-practices-expert]); customers value transparency in the long run and hiding incidents erodes trust (BSRS p.508).

### Defining a healthy culture (BSRS p.509-519)
- [from source] Like healthy systems, culture can be designed, implemented, and maintained; it is a core component of designing, implementing, and maintaining secure and reliable systems (BSRS p.509).
- [from source] Culture of security and reliability by default: postponing speeds up initial velocity at the expense of sustained velocity and of costly retrofits that increase technical debt or are applied inconsistently (Ch.4); the car analogy (buying the seat belt, windshield, and airbag separately); when integrated throughout the lifecycle, they become invisible to the consumer; encourage conversations early (design) and at every iteration; automation (continuous builds, sanitizers, vulnerability discovery, tests), common frameworks and libraries (XSS, SQL injection), and language guidance (memory errors); automatic security reduces friction (slow audits) and errors; see Chs. 12, 13, and 19 (BSRS p.509-510).
- [from source] Culture of review: everyone thinks ahead about their role when approving changes; MPA reviews for least privilege (Ch.5), code reviews (Ch.12), and configuration reviews (Ch.14); document practices and expectations (when access is granted and under what conditions it can be refused, so only valid requests pass and to avoid "us versus them"); educate reviewers (onboarding, a learning scheme with an experienced reviewer also reviewing); everyone participates: code tree owners also have their changes reviewed and system owners take part in MPA for logins; reduce friction: lightweight review options for small changes (minor configuration or MPA for a nonsensitive resource), with clear guidelines on what requires lightweight versus heavyweight; workflow tools that make it easy to start changes, show diffs, and automate small changes; reviewers need context and the power to refuse or redirect if they lack it; automatic checks help (Tricorder at presubmit) (BSRS p.510-511).
- [from source] Culture of awareness: knowing your responsibilities and how to fulfill them (an engineer with sensitive access, someone who deals with outsiders and receives more phishing, an executive on risky trips); educational programs reinforce; light and fun; retention is higher with interactive methods (hands-on labs) than passive ones (video); at Google: mandatory annual training and specialized programs; tactics: interactive talks (root causes and mitigations of significant incidents; encourages reporting), games (the XSS game, which scales better for large organizations), reference documentation (lower retention but critical; searchable internal best practices; ownership and updating), awareness campaigns ("Testing on the Toilet", a weekly flyer in unavoidable places, with a study showing increased awareness), and just-in-time notifications (pop-ups when updating software from an untrusted repository or uploading sensitive data to an unapproved cloud; relation to nudging) (BSRS p.512-515).
- [from source] Culture of Yes: organizations with a history of losses can develop a culture of "no", which leads to stagnation; healthy organizations take risks deliberately, which requires assessing and measuring risk; App Engine example (Ch.8): the security team could have judged running third-party code risky, but collaborated with the product on a layered system, building trust between the teams (BSRS p.515). Balance between accountability and risk: individual fear of getting the decision wrong in design reviews and audits (since humans do not find every problem); people who see themselves as the only line of defense refuse risk; layered defenses (least privilege, resilience, tests) remove the weight of having to be perfect; error budgets allow failures up to a limit, after which teams collaborate to reduce risk (BSRS p.515-516).
- [from source] Culture of Inevitably: no system is perfect; Google assumes failure can happen at any moment; prepare (Ch.16), talk openly about the possibility of failure, and set aside time for simulations (tabletops, Red Team, recovery tests, role playing); study failures, including those of peers; blameless postmortems reduce fear and build confidence; use after-action reports from other organizations (BSRS p.516). Case study: NASA's Columbia Disaster Investigation Board report (2003); chapter 7 of the report deals with how NASA's organization and norms prevented a safety culture, and it can be extrapolated to other engineering organizations (BSRS p.517).
- [from source] Culture of sustainability: continuous efforts, sufficient resources (people and time), defined means to deal with outages and incidents; balance reactive work and proactive investments; distribute the burden (the firefighting analogy from Ch.17); measure the operational load and improvement investments; consider stress, burnout, and morale, add resources or postpone work; avoid heroics with repeatable processes and rotation; treat morale proactively (BSRS p.517). At Google: large SRE and operational security teams in "follow the sun" shifts, part of the time on operations and the rest on infrastructure improvements, development dedicated to long-term initiatives, a virtuous cycle of automating toil; managers trained in team health (Project Oxygen) (BSRS p.518). Exceptional circumstances ("all hands on deck"): make clear the situation is temporary (for example, manual oversight of pushes creates toil), have an on-call group with authority to grant exceptions, and flag the bypasses to handle later; in the postmortem review the reward system that led to the emergency (prioritizing feature launches over reliability or security accumulates technical debt) (BSRS p.518-519).

### Changing culture through good practices (BSRS p.519-526)
- [from source] Resistance comes from fear (chaos, friction, loss of productivity and control, risk); the belief that security and reliability improvements create friction is a myth: with cultural considerations, they can improve everyone's experience; every developer, SRE, and security professional is an instrument of change in their sphere; static cultures may resist, but it is worth trying (BSRS p.519).
- [from source] Align project goals and participants' incentives: trust is hard to build and easy to lose; a common reward system across roles; evaluate by observable metrics (SLOs, threat modeling) and by career advancement that rewards security and reliability (documented high-level expectations, themes and goals, not checkboxes; Google's entry-level software engineer ladder specifies mastering at least one skill outside of code, such as monitoring or security testing); without aligned incentives, those who improve security and reliability are not the ones who get promoted (BSRS p.520).
- [from source] Reduce fear with risk-reduction mechanisms: canaries and staged rollouts (small blast radius; making them routine creates an expectation of care; Chrome's staged releases), dogfooding (adopt the change before imposing it; e.g., MFA first on your own team; endpoint security software tested first on the security team), trusted testers (stakeholders see the change before it is final and give input; explain decisions about the feedback), opt-in before mandatory (adoption at the team's pace), and progressive stringency (example of access justifications: first integrate the library with optional justifications, then require them without blocking, with detailed error messages as a training loop, and only when the metrics show a high hit rate make blocking mandatory) (BSRS p.520-522).
- [from source] Make safety nets the norm: when removing old features (e.g., broad root privileges with proxies), offer breakglass (Ch.5) used sparingly and with heavy auditing, as a last resort, which gives confidence to nervous teams; example: staged rollout with a long canary and a breakglass bypass (Ch.14) (BSRS p.522).
- [from source] Increase productivity and usability: transparent functionality (secure-by-construction APIs, frameworks, and libraries; the secure default being the easy one); focus on usability (security keys were easier than OTPs; the additional security allowed less frequent password changes after risk analysis; NIST 800-63 updated to require changes only on suspected compromise; Password Alert); self-registration and self-resolution (self-service portals; example: Upvote for approving software outside the allow list, with automatic approval or social voting by peers; not perfect but effective and low-friction) (BSRS p.522-524).
- [from source] Overcommunicate and be transparent: document decisions (why, what success looks like, how to roll back, whom to contact; each of Google's Production Excellence SLOs has a rationale), create two-way feedback channels, use dashboards (expectations and progress), write frequent updates (for example monthly) on long changes (BSRS p.524-525).
- [from source] Build empathy: job shadowing or job swapping (from hours to months): SRE Security Exchange Program (an SRE accompanies another SRE or a security engineer for a week and writes a report with recommendations for both teams), Mission Control (six months in SRE), and Hacker Camp (six months in security); Chrome fixits as an onboarding activity; thank-you mechanisms (peer bonuses, digital Kudos, postcards) reinforce incentives (BSRS p.525-526).

### Convincing leadership (BSRS p.526-529)
- [from source] Organizations tend to invest in revenue; "behind the scenes" improvements are hard to sell; even strategies you think will not work may be worth trying (BSRS p.526). Understand the decision process: who decides (VPs in large companies, the CEO in startups and NGOs, the founder or main contributor in open source; it may be a tech lead, a group of stakeholders, or a community, as in the HTTPS case); understand the deciders' pressures (management, boards, shareholders, customers); the DDoS protection example (Ch.10) (BSRS p.526-527).
- [from source] Build the case: collect data (quantify the gain; per-team dashboards; invest in quality data), educate (talks; Red Team postmortems for leaders), align incentives (DDoS: a more reliable site can increase sales; Chrome: fast releases deliver security and reliability; security keys eliminated unpopular password changes), find allies (who also test their assumptions), watch industry trends, change the zeitgeist (HTTPS, Ch.7) (BSRS p.527-529). Pick your battles: constant advocacy is tiring; prioritize initiatives with a chance of success and know when to stop; "lost" causes have value (data, allies, and education ready for when the organization is ready) (BSRS p.529).
- [from source] Escalations: form a group of peers, mentors, tech leads, or managers who give input on both sides; summarize the situation and the decision options concisely and factually, with links and the impact of each option; align with your team's leadership; a session to present to the affected management chains and designate deciders; example at Google: disagreement between a product team and a security reviewer escalates within security and two senior leaders negotiate a compromise or decide among the options; integrated into the culture, it is not confrontational (BSRS p.530).
- [from source] Conclusion: culture can be designed, implemented, and maintained; fear of friction is addressed with buy-in strategies; align goals with stakeholders, including leadership, focus on usability and empathy; adapt and choose the areas most needed, improving over time (BSRS p.530-531).

### DATED
- [DATED] Testing on the Toilet, XSS game, Upvote, Kudos, Project Oxygen, and Google's internal programs; NIST 800-63 (updated in 2017 and revised in 2024); Password Alert (discontinued); gamification and training practices have changed; the book does not address "shift-left"/DevSecOps culture by name or simulated phishing ([extrapolation]).

### What the book does NOT cover in this chapter
- It does not offer cultural maturity metrics or formal models (such as BSIMM or OWASP SAMM); it does not address mandatory regulatory training in detail.

### Conclusion (BSRS p.533-534)
- [from source] Final thesis: there is significant overlap between the technologies and practices that protect systems and those that keep them reliable; traditionally treated as separate disciplines, security and reliability are inherent properties of systems and everyone's responsibility across the lifecycle (BSRS p.533). Context: the Fourth Industrial Revolution, growing dependence on the reliability of solutions, extending data surfaces to third parties requires trust; it applies from three-person open source projects to multinationals; complexity and speed demand integrating security and reliability from the outset (BSRS p.533-534).
- [from source] DevOps and DevSecOps are at the center of conversations about system sustainability, but the integrated model will take time; many organizations split work among development, testing, security, reliability, and operations, and this model needs to transform; the book was written by Google teams (developers, SREs, and security engineers); invest in designing teams, responsibilities, and incentives; agree on common requirements before debating technical solutions; do not underestimate building trust and a common language; no checklist or "silver bullet" replaces the ability to help the organization adapt (BSRS p.534).
