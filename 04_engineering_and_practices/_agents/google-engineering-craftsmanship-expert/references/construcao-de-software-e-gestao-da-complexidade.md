# Software Construction and Complexity Management (CC2 Ch. 5, sample)

> Convention: pages = PDF page (see `INDEX.md`). Source: `Code-Complete-SampleChapter-Pearson.pdf` (120 pages), which is a SAMPLE: it contains the cover and table of contents of the full book (PDF p.1-37), **Chapter 5 "Design in Construction"** (PDF p.38-88, printed pages 73-123) and an excerpt of the index (PDF p.90-119). Relation: printed page = PDF page + 35 (for example, printed 77 = PDF 42). The book is by Steve McConnell, Microsoft Press, 2004 (2nd edition). Nothing outside Ch. 5 was audited as "from source"; chapters such as "Working Classes" (Ch. 6) and "High-Quality Routines" (Ch. 7) exist only as cross-references in the table of contents.

## 1. Design challenges (CC2 p.39-41; printed 74-76)
- [from source] (CC2 p.39) Design is the link between requirements and coding; the author describes it as a "wicked problem" (clearly defined only by solving it), with the Tacoma Narrows bridge example (p.39-40).
- [from source] (CC2 p.40-41) Design is a "sloppy" process even if it produces a clean result; it involves trade-offs and priorities, constraints, is nondeterministic, heuristic and "emergent" (it evolves through reviews, conversations and experience with the code).

## 2. The primary technical imperative: managing complexity
- [from source] (CC2 p.42-43) Brooks: essential and accidental difficulties (the idea comes from "No Silver Bullet").
- [from source] (CC2 p.43) "Software's Primary Technical Imperative" is managing complexity; the author states that, when projects fail for technical reasons, the cause is often uncontrolled complexity. Dijkstra's sentence about spanning from one bit to hundreds of megabytes appears on the same page.
- [from source] (CC2 p.44) The goal is to minimize the amount of the program you have to think about at the same time; a two-pronged approach: minimize the essential complexity that a brain has to deal with and keep accidental complexity from proliferating.
- Fidelity note: the v1 package attributed the "George Miller 7 ± 2 chunks rule" to CC2 p.77. In the sample, "Miller" does not appear and "7 ± 2" does not appear. There is only the observation that fan-out above about seven suggests a complex class (CC2 p.46). It also cited the sentence "managing complexity is software's primary technical imperative" in quotes as literal from p.77; the idea is on p.43 (printed 78), with the wording "Software's Primary Technical Imperative has to be managing complexity".

## 3. Desirable characteristics of a design (CC2 p.45-46; printed 80-81)
- [from source] (CC2 p.45) List of internal characteristics: minimal complexity, ease of maintenance, loose coupling, extensibility, reusability, high fan-in.
- [from source] (CC2 p.46) Low to medium fan-out (above about seven suggests excessive complexity), portability, leanness (no extra parts), stratification and standard techniques. Studies cited on fan-out (Card and Glass 1990; Basili et al. 1996).
- In total the book lists eleven internal characteristics (the six above, plus fan-out, portability, leanness, stratification and standard techniques); the v1 package showed only six. The levels of design run from p.47 onwards: system, subsystems/packages, classes, routines and internal design of routines (CC2 p.47-52).

## 4. Design heuristics (CC2 p.52-75; printed 87-110)
- [from source] (CC2 p.73) Summary: find real-world objects; form consistent abstractions; encapsulate implementation details; inherit when possible; hide secrets (information hiding); identify areas likely to change; keep loose coupling; look for design patterns. Additional heuristics: strong cohesion, hierarchies, class contracts, assignment of responsibilities, design for test, avoid failure, choose binding time consciously, central points of control, brute force, diagrams and modularity.
- [from source] (CC2 p.55-56) Abstraction and encapsulation: encapsulation forbids looking at details beyond the exposed level.
- [from source] (CC2 p.57) Information hiding: idea linked to Parnas's 1972 paper; "secrets" are design and implementation decisions hidden from the rest of the program; Brooks acknowledges having been wrong to criticize the concept.
- [from source] (CC2 p.58-59) A class's interface should reveal as little as possible (iceberg analogy); example of the ID generated by `NewId()` and the `IdType` type; two kinds of secret: complexity and sources of change (p.59). The question to ask is "what does this class need to hide?" (p.62).
- [from source] (CC2 p.60-61) Barriers: excessive distribution of information, circular dependencies, class data confused with globals, perceived performance penalties. Value: large programs with information hiding were found easier to modify by a factor of 4 (Korson and Vaishnavi 1986), cited on p.61 as a concrete figure.
- [from source] (CC2 p.62-64) Identify areas likely to change in three steps (identify, separate, isolate). Examples of areas: business rules, hardware dependencies, input and output, nonstandard language features, areas that are hard to design or build, status variables (prefer enumeration to boolean and use access routines) and data-size constraints (p.63-64).
- [from source] (CC2 p.64) The magnitude of a change's effect should be proportional to its probability. 
- [from source] (CC2 p.65-67) Coupling: criteria of size, visibility and flexibility (p.65-66). Types: simple-data-parameter (acceptable), simple-object (ok), object-parameter (stronger) and semantic coupling (the most insidious; examples: control flag, global data modified by another module, implicit call order) (p.66-67).
- [from source] (CC2 p.68-70) Design patterns as managed complexity; table of patterns (Abstract Factory, Adapter, Bridge, Composite, Decorator, Facade, Factory Method, Iterator, Observer, Singleton, Strategy, Template Method) and two risks: forcing the code into a pattern and "feature-itis".
- [from source] (CC2 p.70) Cohesion: "it has been useful at the routine level for decades; at the class level it has been largely absorbed by the well-defined abstractions heuristic". The book, in this sample, does NOT classify types of cohesion (functional, sequential, communicational, temporal, logical, coincidental); that taxonomy belongs to Ch. 7 of the full book, which is not in the sample.
- [from source] (CC2 p.71-73) Class contracts (pre- and postconditions), responsibilities, design for test, "avoid failure", binding time, "One Right Place", brute force, diagrams, modularity.

## 5. Design practices (CC2 p.75-83; printed 110-118) and summary
- [from source] (CC2 p.75-83) Iterate; divide and conquer; top-down and bottom-up (p.77-78); experimental prototyping (p.79); collaborative design (p.80); how much design to do before coding (p.81-82); recording the design (p.82-83). Final sections: additional resources (p.84-87) and checklist/key points (p.88).

## 6. What the sample does NOT cover
- Numeric limits for routines (lines, number of parameters, cyclomatic complexity) do NOT appear in Ch. 5. The v1 package used "<= 4 parameters", "<= 50 lines" and cohesion notes attributed to CC2 p.77-81; those values are not in the sample. [extrapolation] Heuristics such as "few parameters" and "short routines" are in common use and Ch. 5 says that "keeping routines short helps reduce mental load" (CC2 p.44), but without numbers.
- Chapters on class construction (Ch. 6), routines (Ch. 7), defensive programming (Ch. 8), refactoring (Ch. 24) and other chapters are known only from the table of contents (CC2 p.7-17). Any claim about their content must be labeled [extrapolation] or declined.
