## Ch. 16: structured probabilistic models
- `[from source]` (DLB p.574) Deep learning practice uses model structures, learning algorithms and inference that are quite different from those of the graphical models community in general (explained in section 16.7, p.600-604, **not read**).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.583) **Partition function**: p(x) = (1/Z)p̃(x) (16.4), Z = ∫p̃(x)dx (16.5); the term comes from statistical physics; generally **intractable** in deep learning (leading to ch. 18). Z may not even exist: φ(x) = x² diverges (16.6) and φ(x;β) = exp(−βx²) only normalizes for β > 0 (p.583-584); the **domain** of the variable changes the distribution: with cliques exp(bᵢxᵢ), x ∈ {0,1}ⁿ gives independent sigmoid(bᵢ) and canonical vectors give softmax(b) (p.584).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.584-586) **Energy-based model**: p̃(x) = exp(−E(x)) (16.7), guarantees positivity and allows unconstrained optimization; it is a Boltzmann distribution; today "Boltzmann machine" refers to models with latent variables, and without latents it is called a Markov random field/log-linear model; several energy terms = **product of experts** (Hinton 1999) (p.586). Free energy F(x) = −log Σₕexp(−E(x,h)) (16.8, p.587).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.587-588) **Separation** (undirected graphs) and **d-separation** (directed): active and inactive paths; the graph never implies a nonexistent independence, but may fail to encode independences (for example, context-specific independences).

## Ch. 17: Monte Carlo
- `[from source]` (DLB p.606) Monte Carlo: rewrite the sum/integral as an expectation, s = E_p[f(x)] (17.1-17.2), and approximate it by ŝₙ = (1/n)Σf(x⁽ⁱ⁾) (17.3), an unbiased estimator.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.614) **Gibbs sampling**: pick a variable and sample it given its neighbors; *block Gibbs* in an RBM (hidden units independent given the visible ones and vice versa); in deep learning anything beyond Gibbs is rarely used (Metropolis-Hastings is common in other areas).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.614-616) Poor **mixing** is the main difficulty of MCMC; between modes separated by energy barriers the transition is exponentially unlikely; example E(a,b) = −wab: P(b=1|a=1) = σ(w) (p.615); Fig. 17.1: correlation and separated modes hinder Gibbs.

## Ch. 18: the partition function
- `[from source]` (DLB p.621-622) MLE in undirected models is hard because Z depends on the parameters: ∇θlog p(x;θ) = ∇θlog p̃(x;θ) − ∇θlog Z(θ) (18.4): **positive phase** and **negative phase**; ∇θlog Z = E_{x∼p}[∇θlog p̃(x)] (18.15) is the basis of the Monte Carlo methods for learning; the positive phase lowers the energy of the data and the negative phase raises the energy of samples from the model (Fig. 18.1, p.624; the samples are "hallucinations" or *fantasy particles*; the analogy with sleep is speculative).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.623) Naive Algorithm 18.1: *burn-in* of Markov chains at every gradient step (for example k ≈ 100 Gibbs steps for an RBM on a small image patch): infeasible.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.625-627) **Contrastive divergence (CD-k)** (Hinton 2000/2010): initializes the chains at the data (k from 1 to 20 in an RBM on a small image patch); fails to suppress **spurious modes** (Fig. 18.2); it is biased (Carreira-Perpiñan and Hinton 2005) and its update direction is not the gradient of any function (Sutskever and Tieleman 2010); useful for pretraining shallow RBMs. **SML/PCD** (Younes 1998; Tieleman 2008): keeps the chains between steps; more resistant to spurious modes and able to train deep models.
