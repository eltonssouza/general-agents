## Postmortem culture (Ch. 15)
- Postmortem = a written record of an incident: impact, mitigation/resolution actions, root cause(s) and follow-up actions to prevent recurrence. [from source] (SRE p.126)
- Write a postmortem for all significant incidents, whether or not they generated a page; those that did NOT generate a page are even more valuable, since they probably point to clear monitoring gaps. Google operates in a blameless postmortem culture. [from source] (SRE p.25)
- Goals: incident documented, contributing root causes well understood, effective preventive actions. [from source] (SRE p.126)
- Common triggers (define before the incident; any stakeholder can request one): downtime/degradation above a threshold; data loss; on-call intervention (rollback, rerouting); resolution time above a threshold; monitoring failure. [from source] (SRE p.126)
- Blameless: assume good intent and the information available; focus on systemic reasons; "you can't fix people, but you can fix systems and processes". Blaming leads to hiding problems. [from source] (SRE p.126-127)
- Desirable writing tool: real-time collaboration, open commenting, email notifications. [from source] (SRE p.127)
- Formal review criteria (5): key data collected, complete impact, root cause deep enough, appropriate action plan with priority, outcome shared with stakeholders. [from source] (SRE p.127)
- "No postmortem left unreviewed"; repository of past incidents. [from source] (SRE p.128)
- Dissemination: postmortem of the month, reading clubs, Wheel of Misfortune, discussion group. Publicly reward those who do the right thing (peer bonus). Ask for feedback on effectiveness (toil of writing?). [from source] (SRE p.128-129)
- Privacy: no information that identifies an end user in postmortems. [from source] (text on p.127; note 80 is on PDF page 129) (SRE p.127, 129)
- Caveat: Ch. 15 does **not** list the template fields; it refers to Appendix D. [from source] (SRE p.127)

## Appendix D in full: incident #465 (Shakespeare Sonnet++, 10/21/2015, times in UTC) [from source] (SRE p.334-336)
**Header.** Summary: Shakespeare Search was down for 66 minutes during a period of very high interest because of the discovery of a new sonnet. Impact: estimated 1.21 billion queries lost, no revenue impact; note 163 (p.336) defines Impact as the effect on users, revenue etc. Status: complete, actions in progress. (SRE p.334)
- **Root cause** (note 164 suggests the 5 Whys technique): cascading failure from the combination of exceptional load with a resource leak (file descriptor exhaustion, according to the logs) that occurred when a search failed because the term was not in the corpus. The new sonnet used a word that had never appeared in a work of Shakespeare's and that was precisely the term users were searching for. Under normal conditions the failure rate from this leak is low enough to go unnoticed. **Trigger**: latent bug triggered by a sudden traffic increase. (SRE p.334-335)
- **Resolution**: traffic directed to the **sacrificial cluster** and **10x** capacity to contain the cascade; updated index deployed, resolving the interaction with the latent bug; extra capacity kept until public interest dropped; leak identified and fix deployed. **Detection**: Borgmon detected many HTTP 500s and paged the on-call. (SRE p.334)
- **Actions** (9 legible items): update the playbook for cascades; use the "flux capacitor" to balance load between clusters; schedule a cascade test in the next DiRT; investigate running the MapReduce/index merge continuously; plug the file descriptor leak in ranking; add load shedding; create regression tests for queries of death; deploy the fixed ranking; freeze production until 11/20/2015 due to error budget exhaustion, or request an exception (text cut off in the PDF). Note 165 warns that "reflex" actions are usually extreme or expensive and that there is a risk of over-optimizing for one problem (e.g., creating a specific alert when unit tests would catch the error much earlier). (SRE p.334, 336)
- **Lessons**: what went well: monitoring alerted quickly on the HTTP 500 rate (near 100%); the updated corpus was distributed quickly to all clusters. What went wrong: the team was out of practice responding to a cascade; the error budget was exceeded by several orders of magnitude because the traffic spike resulted almost entirely in failure. Luck: there was a list of enthusiasts with the text of the sonnet; the server logs had stack traces pointing to the file descriptor exhaustion; the query of death was resolved by pushing an index that contained the popular term. Note 166 says "Where we got lucky" is for near misses (SRE p.335-336).
- **Timeline** (note 167 calls it the "script of the incident" and says to start from the incident management document's timeline): 14:51 news of the sonnet; **14:53 Shakespeare Search traffic rises 88x** (a post on /r/shakespeare points to the search engine as the place for the sonnet, which was not even in the corpus yet); **14:54 OUTAGE begins** (backends melting); 14:55 pager storm (ManyHttp500s in all clusters); 14:57 all traffic failing; 14:58 the on-call (docbrown) investigates, very high crash rate; **15:01 INCIDENT begins**: incident #465 declared, coordination in #shakespeare, jennifer named incident commander; 15:03 incident list notified; 15:04-15:10 sonnet text located, corpus update documentation found, sonnet added and indexing started; 15:06 identical symptoms in all clusters; 15:12 two devs called; **15:18 "smoking gun" in the logs**: file descriptor exhaustion, confirmed in the code (leak if the term is not in the corpus); 15:20 index MapReduce finishes; 15:21 decision to increase instances enough that each instance does useful work before dying and restarting; **15:23 all traffic to the USA-2 cluster** (sacrificial), freeing the instance increase in the other clusters without the servers failing immediately; 15:25 replication of the new index; **15:28 2x increase of** instances begins; 15:32 balancing goes back to increasing traffic to non-sacrificial clusters; **15:33 the tasks in the non-sacrificial clusters fail again**, same symptoms; **15:34 order-of-magnitude error discovered in the whiteboard math** for the instance increase; 15:36 balancing goes back to sacrificing USA-2 to prepare another global 5x increase (total **10x** the initial capacity) and **OUTAGE MITIGATED** (updated index replicated to all clusters); 15:39 second wave of increase up to 10x; **gradual return of traffic**: 15:41 **1%**, 15:43 nominal HTTP 500 rate and intermittent task failures at a low level, 15:45 **10%**, 15:47 within SLO with no task failures, 15:50 **30%**, 15:55 **50%**, **16:00 OUTAGE ends** with all traffic balanced across all clusters; **16:30 INCIDENT ends**, exit criterion of **30 minutes of nominal performance** reached. (SRE p.335)
- **App. C (incident state document, p.332-333)** follows the same story: active status, command post in #shakespeare on IRC; hierarchy (IC jennifer; Ops docbrown; Planning and Comms also jennifer; next IC to be defined, update at least every 4 hours and on the Comms lead change); exit criteria: sonnet in the corpus and 99.99% availability with p99 latency < 100 ms for more than 30 minutes; TODOs: reindex (done), borrow emergency capacity (done), turn on the flux capacitor (bug 5554823, pending); timeline from most recent to oldest, with the same sequence (15:28 +2x; 15:21 everything to USA-2 "sacrificial cluster" and draining the others so they recover while more tasks come up). App. C marks 15:21 and App. D marks 15:23 for the diversion to USA-2: **the book itself has a 2-minute discrepancy**. (SRE p.332-333, 335)
- Calculations [derived]: outage 14:54 to 16:00 = **66 min** (matches the summary); incident 15:01 to 16:30 = **89 min**; the incident was only declared 7 min after the start of the outage and 8 min after the 88x peak; from the mitigated outage (15:36) to the end (16:00) = 24 min of gradual traffic return; 1.21 billion / 66 min ≈ 18.3 million queries lost per minute (simple average, assumes a uniform rate; the book does not give this number). The book does not say how many instances were the "initial" ones nor the numeric value of the math error beyond "order of magnitude"; the jump from 2x to 10x is what the timeline shows (15:28 2x; 15:36 another 5x, total 10x).
- Useful patterns from the case: (a) **sacrificial cluster** = concentrate traffic on one cluster so the others recover and receive capacity before reintroducing load (p.332, 335); (b) **reintroduce traffic in steps** (1%, 10%, 30%, 50%, 100%) watching the error rate and task failures at each step (p.335); (c) **exit criterion by time of nominal performance** (30 min) instead of "looks resolved" (p.332, 335); (d) the capacity calculation done on a whiteboard failed by an order of magnitude: recheck emergency calculations (p.335, lesson [inference]).

## Tracking outages (Ch. 16) [DATED in the tooling]
- Postmortems cover only large incidents; aggregated data answers: how many alerts per shift, actionable/non-actionable ratio, which service generates the most toil. [from source] (SRE p.130)
- Escalator tracks the acknowledgement and escalates if nobody acknowledges; Outalator groups alerts into incidents, keeps annotations and allows statistics. [from source] (SRE p.130-131)
- Hierarchical colon tagging (cause:network:switch), "bogus" tag for false positives; free tags avoid a fixed list. [from source] (SRE p.131-133)
- Tags with typos (the book's example is "cause:netwrok") and not very useful tags ("problem-went-away") **exist and are accepted**: the book says that avoiding a predetermined list and letting each team find its own preferences and patterns results in a more useful tool and better data; the tags were "remarkably powerful" for seeing a service's pain points even with little or no formal analysis. The book does not propose normalizing or fixing tags; any normalization routine is [extrapolation]. [from source] (SRE p.131, 133)
- "Building your own Outalator": messaging systems such as **Slack**, Hipchat or IRC, used for internal communication and updating status dashboards, are good integration points for a system like this. This is the only mention of Slack as a tool (the other occurrences of the word in the book are "slack capacity" and "slack CPU", spare capacity, a different topic). [from source] (SRE p.131)
- Analysis: basic counts, comparisons between teams and over time, semantic patterns; the number of incidents does not indicate severity or difficulty. [from source] (SRE p.133-134)
- Unexpected uses: "dummy escalator" (configuration with no human recipient; notifications appear in the Outalator and can be tagged, annotated and reviewed) to record and audit the use of privileged or role accounts and to annotate runs of periodic jobs that are possibly not idempotent (e.g., applying schema changes from version control). The book caveats that the functionality is basic and serves **technical, not legal** audits: it does not claim legal or compliance value for this trail. [from source] (SRE p.133)
- More details from Ch. 16: Escalator was conceived as a nearly transparent tool that received copies of the emails sent to on-call aliases, without changing the behavior of whoever alerts (p.130); Outalator shows the queues of several teams interleaved in time and keeps a copy of the original notification and of the email replies; annotations can be marked "important" (the other parts of the message are collapsed) (p.130); alerts grouped into an incident allow analyzing "incidents per day" separately from "alerts per day" (p.131); one event usually triggers several alerts (e.g., a network failure notifies all teams, including the NOC) and reducing alerts per event is desirable but there is always a trade-off between false positives and false negatives (p.131); colon tags form namespaces (cause:network:switch vs cause:network:cable; suggested prefixes "cause" and "action", derived from each team's historical use); "bogus" for false positives; tags such as "customer:132456" become links (p.131-133). Analysis layers: basic counts (incidents per week/month/quarter, alerts per incident); comparison between teams and over time ("it's the third this week" only makes sense knowing whether before it was 5 per day or 5 per month); semantic analysis (component that causes the most incidents; note 85: "more incidents" may be an artifact of over-sensitive monitoring, and the number of incidents measures neither severity nor difficulty) (p.133-134). The shift handoff report and the "report mode" for the weekly production reviews use the important annotations (p.133). Non-obvious benefit: seeing that a dependency team (e.g., Bigtable) was not alerted and notifying it manually (p.133). [from source]

## Round 4 sweep: Ch. 15 in more detail (p.126-129) and App. D notes [from source]
- Why it exists: without formally learning from incidents, they may repeat "ad infinitum" and multiply in complexity or cascade; the chapter provides criteria for when to write one, practices and advice for cultivating the culture. Writing the postmortem is **not punishment**, it is a learning opportunity for the whole company; there is a cost in time and effort, so one is deliberate about when to write (the objective triggers on p.126 plus any stakeholder's request). A detailed analysis of root cause analysis techniques is beyond scope; each team chooses the appropriate technique (p.126).
- **Blameless in practice (p.127):** the culture originated in **healthcare and aviation** (where mistakes can be fatal); the focus becomes why a person or team had incomplete or incorrect information. Book example: "finger pointing" ("we need to rewrite the entire complicated backend... if I get paged one more time, I'll rewrite it myself") versus "blameless" (an action to rewrite the backend might prevent these pages, the manual for this version is long and hard to train on; future on-calls will thank you). Removing blame gives confidence to escalate problems; one should also not **stigmatize** those who produce postmortems frequently, since an atmosphere of blame leads to hiding incidents. (SRE p.127)
- **Flow (p.127-128):** the tool should have real-time collaboration, open commenting and email notifications; the draft circulates internally and a group of senior engineers assesses completeness with five questions (key data collected? complete impact? root cause deep enough? action plan and bug priority appropriate? outcome shared with stakeholders?); then it is disseminated to the widest possible audience; nothing that identifies an end user (note 80). **"No postmortem left unreviewed"**: regular review sessions close discussions, capture ideas and finalize the state; then it goes to a repository of past incidents (note 81, printed on PDF page 129: Etsy released **Morgue**, a tool for managing postmortems). (SRE p.127-129)
- **Introducing the culture (p.128-129):** active participation of senior management, but ideally it is the fruit of the engineers' self-motivation. Activities: **postmortem of the month** (newsletter), discussion group (Google+), **reading clubs** (sometimes of postmortems that are months or years old), **Wheel of Misfortune** (an old postmortem is re-enacted, with the original incident commander present). Against the cost objection: introduce gradually with a trial period (several complete and successful postmortems, which also help define the triggers); reward and celebrate the practice (publicly and in performance management); seek leadership support. Example: at the 2014 TGIF, "The Art of the Postmortem", an SRE told how a freshly pushed release, despite being tested, took down a critical service for **four minutes**, and that it only lasted that long because he rolled back immediately; he received **two peer bonuses** (note 82 describes Google's Peer Bonus program as a way for colleagues to recognize exceptional effort, with a token cash reward; the book does NOT state the amount) and a big round of applause. The team is also surveyed on the effectiveness of the process (does the culture help? does writing involve much toil? what tools are missing?). (SRE p.128-129)
- **Today (p.129):** it is a cultural norm that every significant incident has a complete postmortem; the "Postmortems at Google" working group coordinates templates, automates creation with data from the incident tools and extraction for trend analysis; the template gained additional **metadata fields**, without listing them; App. D is an example postmortem, not the list of those fields; future work: machine learning to predict weaknesses and reduce duplicate incidents. (SRE p.129)
- **CAPA and blameless postmortem in other industries (p.319):** *corrective and preventative action* is the well-known concept of systematically investigating root causes to prevent recurrence and corresponds to SRE's blameless postmortem culture. What is assessed: **what happened, the effectiveness of the response, what would be done differently next time and which actions will prevent repetition**, without pointing at individuals; postmortems are done after incidents and **published among the SRE teams** so that everyone learns. (SRE p.319)
- App. D notes (p.336): note 164 suggests the 5 Whys technique for understanding contributing factors; 165 says "reflex" actions are often extreme or expensive and there is a risk of over-optimizing for one problem (for example, creating specific monitoring when unit tests would catch the error earlier); 166 that "Where we got lucky" is for **near misses**, with the example of goat teleportation available for emergency use with other animals despite the lack of certification; 167 that the timeline is a "script" of the incident, filled in from the timeline of the incident management document and supplemented. All the App. D footnotes (163-168) are on p.336: 163 defines Impact as the effect on users, revenue etc.; **168 says "Supporting information" gathers useful information, links, logs, screenshots, graphs and IRC/IM logs**. (SRE p.336)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 6): what p.127-128 supports and what it does not [from source]
- **What p.127-128 support.** Blameless culture originated in healthcare and aviation; the blameless postmortem does not complain by pointing fingers, but points out where and how services can improve (book example: "we need to rewrite the whole backend!" × "an action item to rewrite the backend might prevent these pages and the manual for this version is long"); avoiding blame and keeping a constructive tone gives people confidence to escalate problems; do not stigmatize those who produce many postmortems; real-time collaboration, open commenting and email notifications; formal review by seniors with criteria (key data collected, complete impact, deep root cause, action plan and priority, outcome shared with those involved); no postmortem without review (p.128). (SRE p.127-128)
- **What they do not support.** The texts deal with the **postmortem document** and the process around it; they are not a guide to the tone of chat conversations, team emails or review comments on releases and alerts. For giving feedback constructively to a team (explaining the reasoning and asking guiding, not loaded, questions), the book's basis is Ch. 30 (p.292-293); see `gestao-treinamento-interrupts-e-engajamento.md`. (SRE p.127-128, 292-293)

## What the book does NOT say
The **duration in minutes** of third-party incidents cited in other chapters (see Knight Capital in `toil-automacao-e-simplicidade.md`); how many instances were Shakespeare's "initial" capacity in App. D, the exact whiteboard calculation that was off by an order of magnitude and why the error occurred (the timeline only records the discovery at 15:34); Deadline for publishing a postmortem; numeric trigger thresholds; severity; MTTD; "just culture" by name; who approves the postmortem beyond "senior engineers" (SRE p.127).
