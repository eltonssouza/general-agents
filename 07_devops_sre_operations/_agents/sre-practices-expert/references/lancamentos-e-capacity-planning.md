# Launches, capacity planning and software engineering in SRE (Ch. 18, 27, App. E)

## Reliable launches (Ch. 27)
- Launch = any new code that introduces an externally visible change; Google reaches ~70 per week. [from source] (SRE p.261)
- Launch Coordination Engineers (LCE): audit, act as liaison, drive the technical aspects, are gatekeepers who sign off "safe" launches, and educate. [from source] (SRE p.262)
- Process: lightweight, robust, thorough, scalable, adaptable; tactics simplicity, high touch, fast common paths. [from source] (SRE p.263)
- Checklist: every question has proven importance (ideally from a prior disaster); concrete instructions; reviewed once or twice a year. [from source] (SRE p.263-264)
- Checklist themes: Architecture and Dependencies, Integration, Capacity Planning, Failure Modes, Client Behavior, Processes and Automation, Development Process, External Dependencies, Rollout Planning. [from source] (SRE p.265-267)
- Capacity: peaks up to 15x larger than estimated; if 3 replicas serve the peak, keep 4 or 5. [from source] (SRE p.265)
- Techniques: gradual, staged rollouts (canaries), feature flag frameworks (1 to 10 percent in the limited group), abusive client behavior (backoff with jitter, server-controlled config), load tests required for most launches. [from source] (SRE p.267-270)
- History: LCE formalized in 2004; one LCE drove 350 launches in 3.5 years through the LCE checklist; since the team averaged **five engineers** ("five engineers") in that period, Google's throughput was **more than 1,500 launches in 3.5 years** (the book's arithmetic: 350 per LCE, times the average team of five) (the 70 launches/week on p.261 is another figure, from another context); low-risk launch = no new server executables and traffic +<10%; 30% of reviews were low-risk by 2008. [from source] (SRE p.270-271) [DATED]
- Unsolved problems: scalability changes, growing operational load, infrastructure churn (churn reduction policy). [from source] (SRE p.271)

## Intent-based capacity planning (Ch. 18)
- The traditional 4-step cycle is fragile and laborious; bin packing is NP-hard. [from source] (SRE p.149-150)
- Intent-based capacity planning: specify requirements, not implementation; abstraction levels: 50 cores in clusters X/Y/Z → 3 clusters in the region → N+2 per region → 5 nines. In Google's experience the biggest gains come at level 3. [from source] (SRE p.150-151)
- Auxon: performance data, demand forecast, supply, prices, intent config, solver (linear/mixed-integer program), allocation plan. [from source] (SRE p.152-153) [DATED]
- Lessons: launch and iterate, set expectations (MVP), right customers, right level of agnosticism, seed team with generalists and specialist consulting. [from source] (SRE p.153-156)
- Good candidates for engineering projects in SRE: they reduce toil, have a technical foundation, are aligned with the organization; bad ones: all-or-nothing, too abstract. [from source] (SRE p.156)
- "Team size should not scale directly with service growth". [from source] (SRE p.148)
- Capacity (App. B): **p.330** covers N+2 (peak served by N instances, possibly degraded, with the 2 largest out); **p.331** covers validating demand forecasts against reality, load testing instead of tradition for the resource/capacity ratio, and not confusing day-1 load with steady-state load. [from source] (SRE p.330; p.331)

## Launches: the Santa/Keyhole case and Ch. 27 numbers [from source]
- **Keyhole (Maps/Earth satellite imagery) and NORAD Tracks Santa**: on a normal day it serves up to a few thousand images per second; on Christmas Eve 2011 it received **25x the normal peak, over one million requests per second**. The launch had a fixed deadline, lots of publicity, millions of users and a steep ramp. The experiment's kill switches were called **"make-children-cry switches"**. (SRE p.261)
- Definition of launch and pace: any new code with an externally visible change; up to **70 launches per week**; someone who launches every three years does not need a detailed process. (SRE p.261-262)
- LCE: audit, act as liaison, drive technically, sign off "safe" launches and educate; most audits happen before the launch; LCEs have SRE technical requirements plus communication and leadership; advantages: breadth, cross-functional view, objectivity; being SREs, they prioritize reliability. A new LCE needs **about six months of training**. (SRE p.262, 271) The book says that, because it is an SRE role, the LCE is incentivized to prioritize reliability over other concerns, and that a company that does not share Google's reliability goals but shares its high rate of change may choose another incentive structure. [from source] (SRE p.262) When it pays off: fast-growing companies, with a high rate of change in products and services, can benefit from something equivalent to the LCE, especially if they plan to double developers every year or two, scale to hundreds of millions of users, and reliability despite change matters to users; the LCE was Google's solution over about 10 years. [from source] (SRE p.272)
- Launch process refined over **more than 10 years**: criteria lightweight, robust, thorough, scalable, adaptable (in tension with each other); tactics simplicity, high-touch approach and fast common paths (e.g., launching a product in a new country). Engineers work around processes they find heavy. At one point, **new questions on the checklist required a vice president's approval** to curb growth. (SRE p.263)
- Checklist: example question/action pairs (new domain; persistent data → backups; abuse → rate limiting and quotas); convergence on common infrastructure allows replacing long sections with one line ("implement rate limiting using system X"); for a new product (e.g., **Android**), start from the abstract principles and re-specialize with specialists. (SRE p.263-264)
- Checklist themes with examples: dependencies (one page view can become many requests), integration, **capacity** (peaks up to **15x** above the estimate; launch region by region; if **3** deployments serve the peak, keep **4 or 5**), failure modes (deadlines, load shedding), client behavior (exponential backoff and **jitter**), documented manual processes, development (branches per release), external dependencies (filtering proxies, caches), rollout planning (contingency: the "we will launch in the next few days" slide). (SRE p.265-267)
- Techniques: gradual rollouts (canaries; Android apps released to a subset of installs; invitation system and sign-ups limited per day); feature flag frameworks (1 to 10% of the group, independent rollback of each change, new paths failing without affecting users; for UI, an HTTP payload rewriter by cookie subset; in stateful services, by user/entity IDs); abusive client behavior: **syncing every 60 s instead of 600 s generates 10x the load**; retries without backoff and in lockstep (every client downloading at 2 a.m.) create thundering herd, and the fix is that **each client picks the time of that request at random**, with randomness in other periodic processes as well; delays of 1, 2, 4 s without randomness repeat in spikes, hence **jitter**; do not retry 4xx errors; server control of the client (periodically downloaded configuration file) and dormant functionality in the client to abort a launch by turning the feature off. (SRE p.267-269)
- Overload: the naive model of CPU linear with load rarely holds (CPU/JIT/data caches make services slower at low load); example in which **logging debug information on backend errors cost more than handling the normal response**, and the service stopped completely when overloaded (like "GC thrashing" in the JVM). Load tests are mandatory for most launches. (SRE p.269-270)
- History: Google's engineering doubled every year; volunteer "Launch Engineers" created checklists and "Launch Reviews"; in **2004** the full-time LCE team was formed; low-risk launch = no new server executables and a traffic increase of **< 10%**; **30% of reviews were low-risk in 2008**; in 2009 launching a small service had become "legendary" for difficulty. Problems the LCE did not solve: scalability changes (usage grows **more than two orders of magnitude** and forces re-architecture), growing operational load (ops goal < 50%), and infrastructure churn (solution: churn reduction policy, only release resources incompatible with automatic migration tools). (SRE p.270-271)

## Software engineering in SRE and the Auxon case (Ch. 18), details [from source]
- Principle: "team size should not grow directly with service growth" (linear growth of people against exponential growth of the service). Software projects in SRE provide career development, balance with interrupts and diversity of profiles. (SRE p.148-149)
- Traditional capacity planning: collect demand forecast (quarters to years), build/allocation plans, review/approval, deploy and configure; it is an endless cycle, **fragile** (any change, such as a cluster delivery delay, requires cascading review) and **laborious and imprecise** (spreadsheets; bin packing is NP-hard; requests such as "X cores in cluster Y" lose the rationale and the degrees of freedom). (SRE p.149-150)
- **Intent-based capacity planning**: "specify the requirements, not the implementation"; encode dependencies and parameters (intent) and generate the allocation plan automatically; abstraction chain: "50 cores in clusters X, Y, Z" → "50 cores in 3 clusters in the region" → "meet demand per region with N+2" → "5 nines"; Google sees the best gains on reaching level 3. Precursors of intent: dependencies (e.g., Foo requires Bar at no more than **30 ms** of network latency; nested dependencies), performance metrics (convert high-level resources into low-level ones, via load testing and monitoring) and prioritization (what to sacrifice when capacity is short). (SRE p.150-152)
- **Auxon**: built by a small group of software engineers and a TPM within SRE in **two years**; plans the use of "many millions of dollars" in machines; requirements such as "my service must be N+2 per continent" or "frontends at most 50 ms from backends" become a giant linear/mixed-integer program; components: performance data, per-service demand forecast, resource supply (upper bound), prices (objective to minimize), Intent Config, configuration language engine (protocol buffer), Solver (scales to thousands of machines) and Allocation Plan (which also lists unmet requirements). (SRE p.152-153) [DATED]
- Auxon lessons: initial **"Stupid Solver"** with simple heuristics because linear programming was unknown territory, with the solver interface abstracted for later replacement ("launch and iterate"); agnosticism (the allocation plan serves any turnup/forecasting tool) and "come as you are"; initial focus on teams with no capacity-planning process; case studies quantifying gains; "white glove" support for early adopters (including the fear of being replaced by a shell script); do not define success as 100% adoption (diminishing returns in the long tail); seed team with generalists and consulting from operations research specialists. An L3 load balancer developed by SREs became the Google Cloud Load Balancer product. (SRE p.153-156)
- **General advice from Ch. 18 for starting a software program in SRE (not Auxon lessons)** [from source]: communicate clearly the strategy and the benefit to SRE (SREs are skeptical by nature; consistent software speeds up the onboarding of new SREs and makes knowledge portable across teams); assess the organization's capabilities, because a software program is in practice a product team and SRE may lack a product manager and experience running an agile process (SRE p.157). To fill these gaps, ask the product development team for **training or coaching in agile practices** and consulting hours from a **product manager** to define requirements and prioritize; when there is a large enough opportunity, hire people for these roles, which gets easier with positive results from experiments. "Launch and iterate": first products should aim at straightforward targets, **without controversy or existing solutions**, with a release cadence of every **six months** (focus on deciding the features and learning to be a software team); after the first version, some teams moved to **push on green**. "Don't lower your standards": treat the product as if it came from another dev team (would you adopt it?), with code review, integration/end-to-end tests and a production readiness review by **another SRE team**; credibility takes time to build and little to lose. (SRE p.157-158)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 3): gradual canary and feature flags (Ch. 27, p.268) [from source]
- **Gradual rollout (p.268).** Few launches at Google are of the "push-button" kind: a new product to the whole world at the same instant. Almost every update follows a defined process with checks: the new server goes to **a few machines in one datacenter**, is observed, then goes to all machines in the datacenter and then to the world; the first stages are the **canaries**, which detect dangerous effects under real traffic. The installation tools watch the newly started server and, if the change fails validation, perform an **automatic rollback**; the same goes for configuration file changes. In Android apps, the new version is offered to a subset of installs and the percentage rises to 100% (useful when the new version generates more backend traffic); the **invitation system** (sign-ups limited per day and invitations limited per user) is another kind of gradual rollout. (SRE p.268)
- **Feature flag frameworks: the 6 requirements (p.268).** Several Google products built feature flag frameworks, hardened so that most applications need no LCE involvement. They usually meet: (1) roll out **many changes in parallel**, each to a few servers, users, entities or datacenters; (2) gradually increase to a larger but limited group, **usually between 1 and 10 percent**; (3) route traffic through different servers according to users, sessions, objects and/or locations; (4) automatically handle, per project, the failure of the new code paths **without affecting users**; (5) **independently and immediately roll back** each change in case of a bug or serious side effect; (6) **measure** how much each change improves the user experience. Reasons: pre-launch tests are not always enough (realistic environments impractical, complex launches); not every change is equal (small interface tweaks should not require thousands of lines or a heavy process, and hundreds can be tested in parallel); validate with a small sample whether a prototype pleases before spending months hardening it. Two classes of frameworks: those that make **interface changes** easier and those that support **arbitrary server and business logic changes**; the simplest for the interface in a stateless service is an **HTTP payload rewriter** in the frontend servers, limited to a subset of cookies or a similar attribute. (SRE p.268)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 6): economics of intent-based planning without a percentage (p.150-151, 155) and the rollout rule of note 89 (p.147) [from source]
- **Economics: the book cites the benefit but does not quantify it (p.150).** When moving to intent-based capacity planning, computational optimization maps intent to implementation with **much more precision, which ends up resulting in cost savings** for the organization ("cost savings"); with bin packing delegated to computers, human toil drops drastically and service owners focus on high-level priorities (SLOs, production dependencies, infrastructure needs) instead of scavenging for resources. The passage is qualitative: no page of Ch. 18 (p.148-158) gives a **percentage** of savings between level 1 and level 3 of the abstraction chain. (SRE p.150-151)
- **Auxon (p.155).** The gains promised to teams were two: immediate benefit by relieving the pain of manual bin packing of short-term requests, and, with new features, "much larger long-term cost savings" and other benefits; the case study of a Google Business Area compared before and after, and it was the **time savings and the reduction of human toil**, by themselves, that served as a big incentive to other teams (no number in the text). (SRE p.155)
- **Chain of levels (p.151).** (1) "I want 50 cores in clusters X, Y and Z for service Foo" (explicit request); (2) "I want a footprint of 50 cores in any 3 clusters in region YYY" (more degrees of freedom); (3) "I want to meet service Foo's demand in each geographic region with N+2 redundancy" (greater flexibility and consequences understandable in more human terms); (4) "I want to run Foo with 5 nines of reliability" (the most abstract requirement; maybe N+2 is not even sufficient or ideal). Ideally all levels are supported at the same time, with services gaining the more they specify intent instead of implementation; in Google's experience, the **best gains appear on reaching level 3**, and sophisticated services can aim for level 4. Prerequisites (precursors) to capture intent: dependencies, performance metrics and prioritization. (SRE p.151)
- **Gradual rollout rule from note 89 (p.147).** "Standard rule of thumb": start with the release affecting **0.1% of user traffic** and scale by orders of magnitude every 24 hours, varying the geographic location of the updated servers; on day 2, **1%**; on day 3, **10%**; on day 4, **100%**. The sequence 0.1% → 1% → 10% → 100% is therefore the book's rule for the Ch. 17 canary, and not a fixed percentage of machines; the book does not present it as an obligation for every service, since App. B (p.329) says that the service's size and risk profile inform the capacity percentages and the interval between stages. (SRE p.147, 329)
- A rule of "no canary percentages in the book" would be wrong: note 89 gives the sequence above; what the book does not give is a percentage of machines for the p.268 canary, nor automated canary analysis under that name, nor Argo Rollouts.
