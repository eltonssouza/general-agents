# Monitoring and alerts (Ch. 6 and 10)

Pages = PDF (printed = N−12).

## Definitions (Ch. 6)
- White-box: metrics exposed by the internals of the system. Black-box: tests externally visible behavior. Dashboard, alert, root cause, node/machine and push are also defined on p.55: the wording is in the section "Chapter 6 definitions in the book's wording (p.55)" below. [from source] (SRE p.55)
- Reasons to monitor: trends, comparisons, alerting, dashboards, retrospective analysis. [from source] (SRE p.56)
- Alerting = something broke or is about to break soon; never for "something odd". Paging is expensive; too many pages lead to ignoring them. [from source] (SRE p.56)
- Three valid outputs: alert (immediate action), ticket (action within days, no harm if a human acts within a few days), logging (nobody needs to look). Expecting a person to read e-mails and decide whether something requires action is, in the book's words, "fundamentally flawed": monitoring should never require a human to interpret the alert domain; software interprets and only notifies humans when they need to act (the sentence begins at the end of p.25 and ends on p.26). P.330 (App. B) reinforces: dumping alerts into an e-mail hoping someone notices the important ones is equivalent to sending them to /dev/null; it works for a while and depends on eternal human vigilance, and the inevitable outage comes out worse. [from source] (SRE p.25-26; p.330) The Ch. 6 definition (page 55) only classifies "email alerts" as one of the three categories of alert (ticket, e-mail, page), without judging the channel, and the Alertmanager example (page 95) does not treat e-mail as a flawed channel; these two pages do not support the claim.
- Emergency response: reliability is a function of MTTF and MTTR; the book calls MTTR (how fast the team returns the system to health) **the most relevant metric** for assessing the effectiveness of emergency response, and says that a previously written playbook gives roughly a 3x improvement in MTTR versus "improvising". [from source] (SRE p.26)

## Symptom vs cause; white vs black
- Monitoring must answer "what broke" (symptom) and "why" (cause); one person's symptom is another's cause. [from source] (SRE p.57)
- **Table 6-1 (p.57)**, hypothetical symptom → cause examples: serving HTTP 500/404 → database servers refusing connections; slow responses → CPUs overloaded (by a bogosort) or a faulty Ethernet cable; users in Antarctica missing their cat GIFs → the CDN "hates scientists" (the book's joke); private content readable by everyone → a new software push caused the ACLs to be forgotten (the cell is cut off in the PDF text; meaning verified). [from source] (SRE p.57)
- **In a layered system, one person's symptom is another's cause** ("slow database" example, p.57): slow database reads are a symptom for the database SRE who detects them, but a cause for the frontend SRE who observes the slow site; so white-box is sometimes symptom-oriented, sometimes cause-oriented, depending on how informative it is. For debugging, white-box is essential: you need to know how fast the web server perceives the database and how fast the database thinks it is, otherwise you cannot distinguish a slow database from a network problem between them. [from source] (SRE p.57)
- Heavy use of white-box with modest but critical use of black-box; black-box is symptom-oriented and about active problems; for paging it forces discipline. [from source] (SRE p.57)

## The four golden signals
latency, traffic, errors, saturation. [from source] (SRE p.57-58)
- Latency: separate the latency of successes and errors; a slow error is worse than a fast error. [from source] (SRE p.58)
- Errors: explicit (HTTP 500), implicit (200 with wrong content) and by policy (above 1 s is an error). [from source] (SRE p.58)
- Saturation: emphasize the most constrained resource; systems degrade before 100%; p99 in a short window is an early signal; saturation also involves predicting imminent saturation (e.g. "disk fills in 4 hours"), and you page when the signal is problematic or, in the case of saturation, "almost" problematic. [from source] (SRE p.58) Note 25 (p.62): zero-redundancy situations (N + 0) count as imminent, as do "almost full" parts of the service. [from source] (SRE p.62)
- If you measure the four and page when one is problematic, the service is "decently covered". [from source] (SRE p.58)

## Tail and resolution
- Do not rely on averages: the p99 of a backend can become the median of the frontend; use latency buckets (histogram) with approximately exponential boundaries (factor ~3). [from source] (SRE p.58)
- Resolution (p.59): granularity must follow the objective. Observing CPU over a one-minute window does not reveal long spikes that cause high tail latency (so CPU calls for a finer measurement than 1/min). On the other hand, for a web service with a 99.9% annual target (about 9 h of aggregate downtime per year), **probing for status 200 more than once or twice a minute** is probably too frequent; **checking disk occupancy** more than **once every 1-2 minutes** is also probably unnecessary. These are two distinct rules: "1-2 per minute" applies to probing for 200; "every 1-2 minutes" applies only to disk. For high resolution without high cost: internal sampling in the server (CPU every second, incrementing 5% buckets) and aggregation every minute by an external system. [from source] (SRE p.59)

## Simplicity
- Simple, predictable alert rules; remove rarely exercised collections and alerts (book example: a collection, aggregation or alert exercised **less than once a quarter** ("rarely exercised", "less than once a quarter") for some SRE teams is a removal candidate); signals collected but with no ready dashboard or alert are also removal candidates; separate monitoring from profiling, logging etc. [from source] (SRE p.59)
- Granularity (p.59): for a service with a 99.9% target (about 9 hours of unavailability per year), probing for status 200 more than once or twice a minute is probably too frequent, and checking for a full disk more often than every 1-2 minutes also is; this 1-2 min threshold is on **p.59** (printed 47). [from source] (SRE p.59)
- Care with pages: "alert only on the urgent and actionable" (the questions and the philosophy of pages) is on p.59-60, whereas p.56 deals with why paging a human is expensive and with never alerting on "something odd". [from source] (SRE p.56, 59-60)
- Five questions for a new alert (urgent/actionable/user-visible; will I ever be able to ignore it?; users affected?; can I act?; others paged?). [from source] (SRE p.59-60)
- Philosophy of pages: urgency a few times a day, actionable, requires intelligence, concerns a new problem. [from source] (SRE p.60)
- Cases: Bigtable (SLO by the mean generated too many alerts; remedy: improve, pull the SLO back to the p75, turn off email alerts) and Gmail (alerts per task; scripted stopgap vs definitive fix); red flag: a page with a robotic response. [from source] (SRE p.60-61)
- **Gmail/Workqueue (p.61)**: in the early days Gmail ran on Workqueue (a process manager adapted from search index batch) and alerts fired when each task was "de-scheduled"; with thousands of tasks, each a small fraction of users, this alert was unsustainable. The Gmail SRE built a tool that "poked" the scheduler without impacting users; the team debated automating the whole loop (detection → poke) until a better fix existed, but some feared delaying the real fix. The book says this tension reflects distrust of the team's **self-discipline** (the stopgap being forgotten or the fix being deprioritized), credible because patches create layers of **technical debt**; **managers and technical leads** must support and prioritize long-term fixes even when the initial pain of the pages passes. **Red flag**: pages with a routine, algorithmic response; refusing to automate them suggests a lack of confidence in paying down technical debt, a "bigger problem that deserves escalation". The book reviews paging statistics (incidents per shift) in quarterly reports with management. [from source] (SRE p.61)
- An SRE team of 10-12 people typically has 1-2 dedicated to monitoring (a number that decreased with the centralization of infrastructure; every team has at least one "monitoring person"); teams avoid any situation that requires someone to "stare at a screen" to find problems. [from source] (SRE p.56) [DATED]
- **"Magic" systems and dependencies (p.56)** [from source]: Google moved toward simpler and faster monitoring, with better tools for later analysis, and **avoids "magic" systems** that learn thresholds on their own or detect causality automatically (causality). Counterexample: rules about unexpected changes in the end-user request rate, kept as simple as possible, give very fast detection of a simple, specific and severe anomaly. Uses that tolerate more fragility (and complexity): capacity planning and traffic forecasting, and long-term observational experiments (months or years) with low sampling (hours or days), since lost samples do not hide the trend. Complex dependency hierarchies had limited success ("if the database is slow, alert the database; otherwise alert the slow site" is rare); dependent rules only appear in very stable parts, such as the system that drains traffic from a datacenter ("if the datacenter is drained, don't alert me about its latency"); few teams maintain complex hierarchies because the infrastructure is continuously refactored. (SRE p.56)

## Borgmon and time series (Ch. 10) [DATED]
- Borgmon collects metrics in a common format (/varz), keeps series in the "time-series arena" and evaluates rules centrally; Prometheus, Riemann, Heka and Bosun have similar principles. [from source] (SRE p.88-91, 98)
- Counter (non-decreasing) vs gauge; prefer counters; rate() over a window. [from source] (SRE p.92-93)
- Naming convention level:variable:operation (e.g. task:http_requests:rate10m). [from source] (SRE p.93)
- Example alert: error ratio over 10 min > 1% AND errors > 1/s, for 2 min; flapping avoided by a minimum duration; labels with severity=page. [from source] (SRE p.95)
- Alertmanager: inhibits, deduplicates, routes (pager, ticket, dashboard). [from source] (SRE p.95)
- Prober (black-box) pointed at the frontend and the backend; configuration separates rules from targets; unit tests of rules. [from source] (SRE p.96-97)
- It is not good to alert per individual machine at scale; aggregate and prune outliers. [from source] (SRE p.88)
- The maintenance cost must scale sublinearly with the size of the service. [from source] (SRE p.97)

## Borgmon in detail: storage, RAM sizing and rules (Ch. 10) [DATED]
**Storage and the RAM calculation.**
- Borgmon keeps everything in an **in-memory** database with periodic checkpoints to disk; each point is (timestamp, value), in chronological lists (time-series) named by a set of `name=value` labels (var, job, service, zone are the minimum). In practice it is a **fixed-size memory block, the "time-series arena"**, with a garbage collector that expires the oldest entries when the arena fills; the interval between the newest and the oldest entry is the **horizon**, that is, how much queryable data stays in RAM. [from source] (SRE p.90-91)
- Sizing: datacenter and global Borgmon are sized for **about 12 hours** of data (to render consoles) and much less in the lowest-level collectors. **A point costs about 24 bytes of memory**, so "1 million unique series for 12 hours at 1-minute intervals fit in less than 17 GB of RAM". [from source] (SRE p.90-91)
- Calculation [derived]: 1,000,000 series × 720 points (12 h × 60) × 24 B = 17.28 × 10^9 bytes, or 16.1 GiB; so the book's "17 GB" only works out if "GB" means GiB. General form [derived]: RAM ≈ series × (horizon ÷ interval) × 24 B. Example: 2.5 million series × 1,440 points (24 h at 1 min) × 24 B = 86.4 × 10^9 B, or 80.5 GiB. The book does **not** say whether the 24 B include the cost of labels and indexes; treat the result as a floor [inference] and as a 2016 number.
- **"12 hours" is a "magic number"** (note 50, p.98): enough information to debug an incident **in RAM, with fast queries, without costing too much RAM**. [from source] (SRE p.90, 98)
- What leaves RAM: the in-memory state is **periodically archived** to the external **TSDB (Time-Series Database)** system; Borgmon can query it for older data, **slower, but cheaper and larger** than Borgmon's RAM. [from source] (SRE p.91)

**Collection and topology.**
- At each interval Borgmon fetches `/varz` from each target, decodes it and stores it in memory; it spreads the collection of each instance across the interval, so targets do not run in lockstep. It writes **synthetic** variables per target (name resolved to host:port? answered the collection? answered the health check? when the collection finished?), which enables rules that detect unavailable tasks: the collection failure itself is a signal. Varz (HTTP) differs from SNMP, but experience shows this is rarely a problem (note 49). [from source] (SRE p.89-90) **Scripts x common format (p.89)**: instead of running custom scripts to detect failures, Borgmon relies on a common data exposure format (white-box monitoring); this allows mass collection with low overhead and avoids the cost of running subprocesses and establishing network connections on every check; and, since collection does not happen in a short-lived process, the collected history also feeds the alert computation. Claiming that Borgmon "runs scripts per target" contradicts the book. [from source] (SRE p.89)
- Mapped variable: `http_responses map:code 200:25 404:0 500:12` = 25 responses with 200 and 12 responses with 500. Declaring a metric takes one line of code; the price of the schema-less interface is the maintenance of the rules (offset by tools that validate and generate rules). [from source] (SRE p.89)
- Hierarchy that follows the service topology: typically **one Borgmon per cluster and a pair at the global level**; very large services shard below the cluster into "scraper" Borgmons that feed the cluster one. Between Borgmons a streaming protocol is used (saves CPU and network compared with varz text); two or more global ones give diversity in the face of maintenance and failures; the upper layers filter what they want to receive so as not to fill the arena with all per-task series. [from source] (SRE p.89, 95)

**Rules and the numeric example (p.92-95).**
- Borgmon is "a programmable calculator with syntactic sugar to generate alerts". Rules run in parallel in a threadpool, respecting order when a rule uses the output of another; the duration depends on the size of the vectors, so slowness is resolved with more CPU; rule execution time is exported ("monitoring the monitoring"). Aggregation is the cornerstone: summing the series of the tasks of a job to treat the job as a whole. [from source] (SRE p.92)
- `rate()` returns the total delta divided by the total time between the first and last value in the window (e.g. `[10m]`); the window avoids missing points due to collection failure. The naming convention is the triplet level:variable:operation (e.g. `task:http_requests:rate10m`, `dc:http_requests:rate10m`). [from source] (SRE p.93)
- **Note 52 (p.98)**: computing the **sum of rates** (rate per task and then sum) instead of the rate of the sum protects the result against a **counter reset or missing data**, for example due to a task restart or failed collection. Note 53: although untyped, most varz are simple counters and the `rate` function handles all reset cases. **Counters do not lose meaning when there are events between samplings; gauges may lose that activity** (p.92). [from source]
- Book example with 5 tasks (10-min rates in req/s: 1; 0.9; 1.1; 0; 1): the datacenter sum is **4 req/s**. Errors (code 500): 0.6 req/s in total; error ratio = 0.6 ÷ 4 = **0.15 (15%)**. The alert rule requires ratio > 0.01 **and** errors > 1/s: the ratio is far above, but the 0.6 errors/s do **not** exceed 1/s, so **the alert is not active**; when it exceeds 1/s it stays **pending for 2 minutes** (`for 2m`) before firing. [from source] (SRE p.93-95; ratio calculation [derived])
- **Minimum duration**: alerts "flap"; so the rule requires the condition to stay true for a minimum time, **typically at least two rule evaluation cycles**, so that a missed collection does not generate a false alert. [from source] (SRE p.94-95) The example threshold (1% and 1 error/s) is an illustration from the book, not a universal rule.
- Firing sends an Alert RPC to **Alertmanager** (when the rule fires and again when it is considered "firing"), which routes: it can inhibit alerts when others are active, deduplicate across Borgmons with the same labelset and do fan-in/fan-out by labelset. Page alerts go to the on-call, important but subcritical ones to the ticket queue, the rest stays as information in status dashboards. [from source] (SRE p.95)

**Prober, configuration and maintenance scale.**
- The **Prober** runs a protocol check against the target, validates the payload (for example the HTML), can extract values as series and export timing histograms by operation and payload size; pointed at the frontend domain **and** behind the balancer, it distinguishes a localized failure (one datacenter) and suppresses alerts. [from source] (SRE p.96)
- Configuration separates **rules** from **targets**; there are templates (rule libraries) of two kinds: schema of variables exported by code libraries (HTTP server, memory allocator, storage client, RPC) and aggregation from the task up to the global service; unit and regression tests synthesize series; a continuous integration service runs the suite, packages and distributes the configuration, which each Borgmon validates before accepting. Groupings follow a shared destination (tasks by config files, jobs of a shard per datacenter, sites by network). [from source] (SRE p.96-97)
- Chapter conclusion: the maintenance cost must grow **sublinearly** with the size of the service; decouple the size of the monitored system from the size of the rules. Open source tools with similar ideas: Prometheus, Riemann, Heka, Bosun. [from source] (SRE p.88, 97-98)

## Chapter 6 definitions in the book's wording (p.55) and notes of Ch. 6 and 10 [from source]
Post-blind-test correction (round 3): p.55 only appeared as a pointer ("root cause, node, push also defined"). Here are the definitions, paraphrased.
- **Root cause**: a defect in a software or human system that, if repaired, gives confidence that the event will not recur in the same way. The same incident can have **several root causes**; the book's example is the combination of insufficient process automation, software that crashed on invalid input and insufficient testing of the script that generates the configuration. **Each of these factors could be a root cause on its own and each must be repaired.** Note: in that definition the "software that crashed" is one of the root causes; it is not the "trigger", a concept that comes from the Appendix D template (p.334). (SRE p.55)
- **Push**: any change to the running software of a service or to its configuration. (SRE p.55)
- **Alert**: notification meant to be read by a person and pushed to a bug/ticket queue, an e-mail alias or a pager; they are classified, respectively, as **tickets, email alerts and pages**. (SRE p.55)
- **Node and machine**: terms used interchangeably for a running kernel instance, on a physical server, a VM or a container. On the same machine there can coexist services related to each other (a cache server and a web server) or unrelated (a code repository and the master of a configuration system such as Puppet or Chef). (SRE p.55)
- **Monitoring** is collecting, processing, aggregating and displaying quantitative data in real time (counts and types of queries, errors, processing times, server lifetime). **White-box**: metrics exposed by the interior of the system (logs, JVM profiling interface, an HTTP handler that emits internal statistics). **Black-box**: testing externally visible behavior, as a user would see it. **Dashboard**: an application (usually web) with the summarized view of the service's core metrics, pre-assembled, which can include ticket queue size, high-priority bugs, who is on-call and recent pushes. The book itself warns that there is no uniformly shared vocabulary, not even within Google; the definitions are the most common interpretations. (SRE p.55)
- Note 25 (p.62): **zero redundancy (N + 0)** situations count as "imminent" for saturation purposes, as do "almost full" parts of the service. (SRE p.62)
- Notes 45 and 46 (p.98): non-SRE teams often use a **generator** to create the initial boilerplate and the updates of alert rules and find it easier (though less powerful) than editing the rules directly; other applications also export their internal state through their own service protocol: OpenLDAP via the `cn=Monitor` subtree, MySQL with `SHOW VARIABLES`, Apache with the `mod_status` handler. (SRE p.98)
- Notes 43 and 44 (p.98): note 43 (tied to the term varz, p.89) says the name is pronounced "var-zee", because Google was born in the US; note 44 says the plural of Borgmon is Borgmon itself, like "sheep", i.e. the spelling "Borgmen" is wrong. Whoever asks how varz is pronounced or what the plural of Borgmon is finds the answer in these two footnotes. [from source] (SRE p.98)
