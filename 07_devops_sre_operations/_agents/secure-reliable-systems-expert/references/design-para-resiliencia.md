## Ch. 8 — Design for resilience (BSRS p.179-217)

### Premise and principles (BSRS p.179-180)
- [from source] Resilience = the ability to protect against attacks and withstand unusual circumstances that stress the system; resilient systems recover automatically from partial (or even total) failures and ideally keep running, perhaps in a degraded mode; resilience is different from design for recovery (Ch.9): recovery fixes what broke, resilience delays or withstands the breakage (BSRS p.179-180). For smaller organizations: focus on controlled degradation, blast radius controls, and segmentation into failure domains; as you grow, use continuous validation (BSRS p.179).
- [from source] Six approaches: make each layer independently resilient (defense in depth); prioritize features and calculate cost (which to always sustain and which can be limited); compartmentalize into defined boundaries; redundancy of compartments against local failures and, for global failures, compartments with different properties; reduce reaction time with safe automation; maintain effectiveness by validating resilience properties (BSRS p.180).

### Defense in depth and the Trojan Horse (BSRS p.181-183)
- [from source] Defense in depth establishes multiple defensive perimeters, limiting the attacker's visibility and making successful exploits harder (BSRS p.181). Four stages of the Trojan attack: threat modeling and vulnerability discovery, deployment, execution, and compromise; there were opportunities to interrupt before the compromise (BSRS p.181).
- [from source] Discovery: defenders cannot prevent reconnaissance, but should detect it as a signal (monitor port and application scans; track DNS records resembling your domain for spear phishing; buy threat intelligence; an in-house intelligence team only when the company grows); the defender knows their weaknesses best; beware of blind spots in vectors considered unlikely (BSRS p.182).
- [from source] Attack deployment: network traffic inspection, virus detection, software execution control, protected sandboxes, and privilege provisioning that flags anomalous use (BSRS p.182-183). Execution: limit the blast radius (sandboxing); compromise: the response determines how long the infrastructure remains compromised (example: banks with EternalBlue/WannaCry in 2018) (BSRS p.183).

### Analysis of Google App Engine (BSRS p.183-186)
- [from source] Untrusted third-party code running on the internal infrastructure; a VM per user was considered inefficient at the time; initial threats: network access (the assumption that everything on the production network was trusted), local filesystem access, the large Linux kernel surface (BSRS p.184).
- [from source] Measures: remove built-in network and filesystem I/O APIs and replace them with "safe" versions that call the cloud infrastructure; disallow compiled bytecode and user-supplied shared libraries (BSRS p.185). Runtime layers: audit of the runtime's base objects for memory corruption bugs (upstream fixes); assuming some defenses would fail: Python runtime compiled to Native Client (NaCl) bitcode against memory corruption and control-flow subversion, plus a second ptrace sandbox layer that filters and alerts on unexpected syscalls and terminates the runtime with a high-priority alert; over five years, anomalies were detected (security researchers) and contained by the layers (BSRS p.185). Complementary layers: each anticipates the weaknesses of the previous one; as defenses activate the compromise signal gets stronger; external researchers (bug bounty) also found vectors (BSRS p.186).

### Controlling degradation (BSRS p.186-190)
- [from source] Failures reduce the global pool of resources (disk, network, compute) and can compound; under overload the response degrades and can reach total unavailability; without planning the system breaks where it is weakest and not where it is safest; control degradation by choosing in advance which properties to disable or adjust, preserving security (BSRS p.186-187).
- [from source] Tactics: free resources by disabling little-used, less critical, or expensive features (example: disable RSA and keep ECC in TLS, due to the lower cost of the private key in software); fast, automatic responses (easier on servers under your control than on clients, with long update cycles and rollback risk from incompatibility); understand which systems are critical and their interdependencies (Gmail with "simple HTML mode"; under bandwidth limits even that mode could be deprioritized if that preserved network security monitoring) (BSRS p.187-188). Make hard choices in advance, not under incident pressure (BSRS p.188).
- [from source] Security and reliability tradeoff: when ranking criticality, consider security and acceptable risk (2FA outage at login: allowing login without 2FA may be acceptable for some services and unacceptable for a bank that prefers unavailability to unauthorized access) (BSRS p.188).
- [from source] Differentiate failure costs: identify the total cost of each operation (CPU, memory, bandwidth in load tests; prioritize the most impactful) and at which stage the costs are incurred; change to fail early or cheaply (validate access before allocating memory; SYN cookies against connections from forged IPs; CAPTCHA for expensive operations); lame-duck mode (keeps serving, but tells callers to back off); instances drained due to compromise can free resources, but preserve forensic data first (BSRS p.188-189). Aggressive client retries multiply the effect and can cause cascading failure (BSRS p.189).
- [from source] User experience in degraded mode: inform the user, allow interacting with what works, communicate staleness or risks, explicitly disable what is not safe (example: offline mode; E2E chat keeps old messages after a key change; anti-example: GUI frozen by an RPC with timeout; mobile app that only shows fresh content and does not display the cache) (BSRS p.189-190). Mitigation speed: avoid critical points on clients, which are harder to control (BSRS p.190).

### Response mechanisms (BSRS p.190-195)
- [from source] Automatic, preprogrammed responses tend to outperform humans, but humans remain in the loop for checks and balances and unforeseen cases; permanently overprovisioning wastes resources and does not guarantee safety (BSRS p.190-191).
- [from source] Load shedding: return errors instead of serving requests; goal: stabilize components at peak load, preserving critical security functions; crashing takes all capacity offline and shifts the load to others (cascade); choosing what to drop requires notions of priority and request cost, policy according to utilization; priority by business value (critical security functions with high priority); cost measured or estimated, comparable to server utilization (BSRS p.191-192). Throttling: delay responses to indirectly modify client behavior and redirect resources; policies for offending clients or all (BSRS p.192-193). The concepts are detailed in the SRE book (route to sre-practices-expert for general principles).
- [from source] Automated response: utilization statistics trigger load shedding and throttling; slow controls knock out priority requests; a central internal service translates business considerations into distributed policies in near real time; load balancers that respond to throttling, DoS protections, failover preparation from load shedding reports; self-contained failure detection (a server that cannot serve degrades to total load shedding, without depending on external signals an attacker could simulate); log degradation levels for diagnosis and remaining capacity (BSRS p.193-194).
- [from source] Fail safe/open vs fail secure/closed: to maximize reliability, the system fails open (ACL failed to load: "allow all"); to maximize security, it closes (ACL failed to load: "deny all"); they conflict: define the non-negotiable minimum security posture and give reliability to critical security functions (e.g., special QoS for security RPCs; marking to avoid CPU starvation) (BSRS p.194). Critical security operations must not fail open (an isolated DoS would degrade security); a lower-cost alternative component with stronger controls can replace the regular controls and discourage the attacker (BSRS p.195).
- [from source] A foothold for humans: prevent automation from disabling services used to recover the infrastructure (e.g., a SYN flood must not prevent the responder's SSH; low-dependency alternatives continuously validated); do not allow unsupervised policy changes of large magnitude or scope; use a change budget: when exhausted there is no automatic refresh and a human decides (BSRS p.195).

### Controlling the blast radius (BSRS p.195-202)
- [from source] Network segmentation (VLANs and ACLs) as an example of compartmentalization; on a single network, compromised credentials give access to everything; compartments create barriers against attackers and accidental failures, help with quarantine (isolating a compartment for analysis while others recover), and allow discarding a compartment to save the rest (BSRS p.195-196).
- [from source] To establish boundaries you must recognize endpoints and confirm identity: authenticated RPCs with mutually authenticated connections (mutual authentication), possibly with metadata such as location in the certificate (BSRS p.196-197). Granularity tradeoff: one compartment per RPC method aligns with logical boundaries (linear number of features); restricting parameter values gives finer control but complexity proportional to clients; a compartment per whole server is easy to manage but gives less value; consult incident management and operations teams; imperfect compartments are also worthwhile (the attacker may slip and raise an alert; time is gained) (BSRS p.197). Plan and practice sealing compartments to contain incursions, with proportional actions without taking the whole system offline (BSRS p.197). Shared vs per-customer instance; VMs of mutually distrusting entities on the same hardware (virtualization zero-days, cross-VM leaks): dedicated (sole-tenant) nodes at a price premium (BSRS p.197-198).
- [from source] You need to validate that forbidden operations across boundaries actually fail (BSRS p.198). Google compartmentalizes by role, location, and time (BSRS p.198).
- [from source] Role separation: jobs run as roles/service accounts; compromising a job lets one impersonate the role and access everything the role accesses; run different jobs (e.g., photos vs chats) as different roles (BSRS p.198).
- [from source] Location separation: prevents physical intrusion into one datacenter from reading everyone's data; limits powerful administrators to regions; the same service running with different roles in different locations; cryptographic compartments per location; physical location is a natural boundary (disasters, fiber cuts, attacks that require presence); avoid critical dependence on a backend in a single datacenter; tailgating into an office must not give access to production (BSRS p.198-199). Align physical and logical boundaries: segment the corporate network from production in distinct buildings, separate high-visitor-traffic areas; distribute secrets by location (a certificate per server or per datacenter allows draining, revoking only that location's certs, and continuing to serve) (BSRS p.199-200). Isolation of trust: restrict communication by default and allow only what is expected between locations; user-facing APIs open globally, control plane ones restricted, hence per-call control; identities with location metadata; the job control system certifies jobs per location (each location has its own copy), instead of a single central point valuable to the attacker; data ACLs extended with location restrictions (separate where data is stored from who accesses it); a per-location root of trust distributed to the entire fleet, with revocation of a location's identity via an updated list (BSRS p.200-201). Limits of trust by location: at Google the corporate network does not confer trust by location (BeyondCorp; guest VLAN; 802.1x); nor do datacenters, illustrated by a Red Team that left a wireless access point which a considerate technician tidied up with cable ties, assuming it was legitimate; authentication between production services via per-machine credentials (BSRS p.201). Isolation of confidentiality: isolate encryption keys by location (location root keys only available in the right location; global-to-local key tree, gradually: full isolation only when all the keys above are local) (BSRS p.201-202).
- [from source] Time separation: rotating and expiring keys and credentials forces the adversary to maintain presence to reacquire secrets and gives more chances of detection; rotation can close the vector; clock-based expiration is problematic if rotation fails before expiry; balance rotation frequency and downtime risk (Ch.9) (BSRS p.202).

### Failure domains and redundancies (BSRS p.202-209)
- [from source] Combine: separate into independent failure domains, reduce the probability of one root cause affecting several domains, create redundant resources, components, or procedures (BSRS p.203). Failure domain: equivalent and completely independent partitions of the system (functional isolation); each partition supports only a fraction of the capacity; requires continuous effort to maintain isolation (BSRS p.203).
- [from source] Data isolation: each domain with its own copy of data; restrict how updates enter (only after validations; exceptions escalated; breakglass); example: empty or "permit all" ACL due to a bug or an attacker; at Google, services have a data intake endpoint with frameworks to version snapshots and assess validity; centralized push with quality control and rate limit by quota per application (forbids changing several applications or capacity too quickly); store the last known good configuration on disk, preserved for a limited time (BSRS p.203-204).
- [from source] Practical aspects: even two failure domains help (A/B regression, one as a canary without allowing simultaneous updates to both; geographic separation; different software versions); combined, data and functional isolation delay propagation, giving other defenses time; pushing fix candidates in parallel to distinct domains avoids propagating a wrong "fix" globally (BSRS p.204-205). Costs: multiple copies of configuration per domain identifier, consistency, protection against simultaneous corruption, hiding the separation from clients, possibly partitioning dependencies (BSRS p.205).
- [from source] Component types: high capacity (main fleet, absorbs spikes and DoS until mitigation), high availability (copies with fewer dependencies and a limited rate of change; e.g., locally cached data, older code and config; low operational overhead and cost proportional to the fleet), and low dependency (alternative implementation with minimal dependencies, which accepts the aggregate error budget of the simplified stack, possibly removing features; does not share failure domains with the critical component; examples: home alarm system with a local server and a hidden landline; minimal alternative network for global network failure, with few resources and bandwidth) (BSRS p.205-208).
- [from source] Controlling redundancies: attackers can exploit differences between redundant systems (pushing toward the less secure option); more secure alternatives discourage the attacker (BSRS p.208). Failover: a set of backends interchangeable by desired reliability behavior, with logic in the system (flags) instead of asking the same high-availability backend for low-dependency behavior (runtime dependencies that are also startup dependencies keep the restart risk); after failover apply throttling and load shedding adjusted to the alternative component; provide a way to disable failback (BSRS p.208-209).
- [from source] Pitfalls: coming to depend on the alternatives in normal operation (they get overloaded in an outage) or leaving them unused (they rot); uncontrolled growth of dependencies; failing over to an alternative component must not compromise integrity or security (examples of dilemmas: an HA service with six-week-old code that needs an urgent fix; local private keys to make a low-dependency service; reducing the update frequency of ACLs and CRLs); do not allow self-healing at the wrong moment: if failover was manual, automation must not override it (BSRS p.209-210).

### Continuous validation (BSRS p.210-215)
- [from source] Validate by observing the system in realistic but controlled circumstances; unlike chaos engineering (exploratory), validation confirms specific properties; maintenance strategy: discover new failures, implement validators, run them repeatedly, retire them when the behavior ceases to exist; sources: bug reports, fuzzing, fault injection (like Chaos Monkey), expert judgment; automation framework to schedule incompatible checks; audit the automation itself (BSRS p.210-211).
- [from source] Focus areas: validate smaller replicas of systems; analyze logs: attempts to cross role, location, or time boundaries must fail and unexpected successes must be flagged; validate attributes of principles (least privilege, understandability, adaptability, recovery, the latter especially because it involves humans: review the readability of the instructions and the interoperability of the flows); active pentest gives a black-box view; low-dependency components deserve special attention, since there is no fallback beyond them (BSRS p.211-212).
- [from source] Practices at Google: inject behavior changes (arbitrary delays or failures in RPC servers in increasing steps until simulating an outage; abort first if anything fails); exercise emergency components in the normal flow (request mirroring to the HA component, comparing responses with an alert on discrepancy above expected, using the response from the high-capacity one; low-dependency validated by real users, such as on-call engineers); split requests when mirroring is not possible; direct experiments at a single failure domain; oversubscribe without complacency (periodically move the batch service out of the cluster, measuring the time to free disks, with real evidence of the X-hour SLO); measure key rotation cycles: rotation latency and verified loss of access (ensuring the old key became useless); rotate even without need to be ready for an emergency rotation; how to prove loss of access: demonstrate that using the old key fails before destroying it, or use deny lists (CRLs) and alerts if any ACL lists the old key's fingerprint or serial (BSRS p.212-215).

### Where to start (BSRS p.215-217)
- [from source] Order by cost: (1) failure domains and blast radius controls (lowest cost, static change); (2) high-availability services; then (3) load shedding and throttling, (4) evaluate DoS defenses (Ch.10), (5) if building a low-dependency solution, ensure it remains low-dependency (BSRS p.215-216). Arguments to overcome resistance: the lasting effect of failure domains; rotation brings cryptographic agility; HA is cheap and disposable; load shedding reduces resource costs; controlled degradation contributes to the first reaction to DoS; for low-dependency, estimate how long it would take to bring up all the dependencies of the business-critical services (BSRS p.216). Keep everything continuously validated; when resources are lacking, rationalize the costs of the mechanisms already deployed before expanding (BSRS p.216-217).
- [from source] Conclusion: defense in depth is like N+1 redundancy for defenses (do not trust all network capacity to one router, nor security to one firewall); assume and verify failures at different layers; plan for lateral movement with the intent of stopping it (BSRS p.217).
