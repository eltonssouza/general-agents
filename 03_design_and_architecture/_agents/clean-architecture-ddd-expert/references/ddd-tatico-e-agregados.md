# Tactical DDD: building blocks, aggregates and supple design (DDD Reference)

Source: Eric Evans, *Domain-Driven Design Reference* (`DDD`). **Convention**: `(DDD p.N)` = **PDF** page (printed = N − 7). Labels: `[from source]`, `[inference]`, `[extrapolation]`, `[DATED]`.

---

## 1. Layered Architecture, Entities, Value Objects

- **Layered Architecture** `[from source] (DDD p.17)`: when UI, database and support code is written inside the business objects, the domain logic becomes diffuse, automated tests become awkward and the model does not evolve. The prescription is to isolate the expression of the model and the business logic, eliminating dependence on infrastructure, UI and non-business application logic; partition into cohesive layers, each depending only on the layers below. The text states that the **key goal is isolation** and that related patterns, such as "Hexagonal Architecture", may serve equally well or better on this point.
- **Entities** `[from source] (DDD p.18)`: objects defined by identity and continuity over the life cycle, not by attributes; "mistaken identity" can corrupt data; the model must define what it means to be "the same thing", and identification may come from outside or be an arbitrary identifier generated by the system. (The CPF/UUID example from the previous package is `[extrapolation]`.)
- **Value Objects** `[from source] (DDD p.19)`: objects without conceptual identity that describe something; classify as a VO when only attributes and logic matter; **treat as immutable**; operations should be *side-effect-free functions*; do not give identity and avoid the complexity of maintaining entities. The text does not prescribe "validation in the constructor" or "structural equality" in those words `[extrapolation]` (common practice, consistent with the pattern).
- Comparative note: *Out of the Tar Pit* treats object identity as complexity tied to state and describes Value Objects as an OOP countermeasure that reintroduces extensional identity (`TARPIT p.13-14`). The two texts see the same problem from different angles `[inference]`.

## 2. Domain Events, Services, Modules

- **Domain Events** `[from source] (DDD p.20)`: something that happened and that domain experts care about. They are modeled as domain objects, distinct from system events; "ordinarily immutable" because they are a record of the past; they typically contain a description, a timestamp of occurrence and the identity of the entities involved, sometimes a registration timestamp and who registered it. The text ties the pattern to distributed systems: aggregates stay internally consistent at all times while other changes propagate asynchronously. (The table of contents marks `Domain Events` as a new term since 2004, p.3.) The rule "name in the past participle" is **not in the PDF** `[extrapolation]`.
- **Services** `[from source] (DDD p.21)`: when a significant process or transformation is not the natural responsibility of an entity or VO, add an operation as a standalone interface declared as a service, with a contract (assertions) in the ubiquitous language of the context and a name that enters the language. "Stateless" is not stated by that word in the booklet `[extrapolation]`.
- **Modules** `[from source] (DDD p.22)`: modules are part of the model; choose modules that tell the story of the system and group cohesive concepts, with names in the ubiquitous language; low coupling is sought in the sense of independently understandable concepts.

## 3. Aggregates, Repositories, Factories

**Aggregates** `[from source] (DDD p.23)`. The text says, in essence:
- The problem: it is hard to guarantee the consistency of changes in models with complex associations; cautious database locks make users interfere with each other.
- Group entities and VOs into aggregates with a boundary; **choose one entity as the root**; external objects may hold references **only to the root** (references to internal members only within a single operation).
- Properties and invariants are defined for the aggregate as a whole, with **the root (or a designated mechanism) responsible for enforcing them**.
- **Use the same boundaries to govern transactions and distribution**: inside the boundary, synchronous consistency rules; **across boundaries, asynchronous updates**; an aggregate stays together on one server; different aggregates may be distributed.
- If the boundaries do not guide decisions well, reconsider the model: the scenario may be pointing to an important insight.

What is **not** written in the booklet, and circulates as "golden rules" `[extrapolation]`:
- "Reference other aggregates only by ID" (the text speaks of references to the root; the practice of using identities comes from later literature);
- "At most one aggregate modified per transaction" (the text says boundaries govern transactions and that, across aggregates, handling is asynchronous; the "one per transaction" formulation is a derived reading, see `[inference]` below);
- "Small aggregates" as a commandment;
- "Internal entities have only local identity" (not stated in those terms).

`[inference]`: if consistency across aggregates is asynchronous (p.23), then an operation that needs immediate consistency between two aggregates suggests the model should be reconsidered, which is precisely what the text recommends.

**Repositories** `[from source] (DDD p.24)`: the problem is that unrestricted queries break encapsulation or instantiate objects from the interior of an aggregate "ambushing" the root, and the domain logic migrates to queries and application code, leaving entities and VOs as mere data containers. The prescription: for each type of aggregate that needs global access, a service that gives the illusion of an in-memory collection of all objects of the root's type, with methods to add/remove and to select by criteria meaningful to the experts; **repositories only for roots that really need direct access**. Note: "interface in the domain and implementation in the infrastructure" is not in the booklet `[extrapolation]` (it is consistent with p.17).

**Factories** `[from source] (DDD p.25)`: when creating an entire, internally consistent aggregate, or a large VO, is complicated or reveals internal structure, move the responsibility to another object with an interface that encapsulates the assembly, without requiring the client to reference concrete classes; create the entire aggregate "at once", enforcing invariants.

## 4. Supple Design

Chapter III `[from source] (DDD p.26-34)`: complements *deep modeling*; it serves two roles (whoever uses the design and whoever changes it).
- *Intention-Revealing Interfaces* (p.27): name by effect and purpose, not by means; write the test first.
- *Side-Effect-Free Functions* (p.28): put as much logic as possible in functions without observable effects; segregate commands into simple operations that return no domain information; move complex logic into VOs.
- *Assertions* (p.29): make postconditions and invariants explicit; if they cannot be code, write tests; assertions define service contracts and aggregate invariants.
- *Standalone Classes* (p.30), *Closure of Operations* (p.31), *Declarative Design* (p.32), *Drawing on Established Formalisms* (p.33), *Conceptual Contours* (p.34).

## 5. What the booklet does not cover

It has no example code, aggregate diagrams, numbered modeling rules, CQRS, Event Sourcing (only a mention on p.4), Saga, Outbox or ORMs. These items, if used, are `[extrapolation]`.
