# Load balancing, overload and cascading failures (Ch. 19-22)

Pages = PDF (printed = N−12).

## Frontend (Ch. 19)
- DNS load balancing: multiple A/AAAA records; little control; SRV not adopted for HTTP; mitigations: anycast on the authoritative nameservers, network map; recursive resolvers hide the client (EDNS0 as a partial solution, not an official standard in 2016); low TTL; 512-byte limit per response. [from source] (SRE p.160-161) [DATED]
- VIPs with a network load balancer; strategies: least loaded (problem with stateful), packet hash (id mod N) and consistent hashing; forwarding by NAT, L2/DSR and GRE encapsulation, which is **Google's current solution (2016)** (24-byte overhead; mitigated with a larger MTU in the datacenter); with `id mod N`, removing a backend remaps almost all packets and resets almost all connections. [from source] (SRE p.161-162)

## Datacenter (Ch. 20)
- Homogeneous backends; the smallest service has at least 3 processes; typical 100-1,000. [from source] (SRE p.163)
- Waste = sum of the differences between the CPU of the most loaded task and that of each task. [from source] (SRE p.164)
- Flow control (limit of 100 active requests) and **lame duck** (states healthy, refusing connections, lame duck; clean shutdown with SIGTERM; interval 10-150 s). [from source] (SRE p.164-166)
- **Subsetting**: typical subset of 20-100 backends; random subsetting is uneven (with 300x300 and a 10% subset, min 50% and max 150% of the average); **deterministic subsetting** with rounds and a shuffle per round leaves the difference in connections per backend at most 1. [from source] (SRE p.166-170)
- Policies: simple Round Robin (up to 2x CPU spread; causes: small subsetting, variable query costs, machine diversity/GCU, antagonistic neighbors and restarts); Least-Loaded Round Robin (the "sinkholing traffic" trap: counting errors as active requests; two limitations: the active count is a poor proxy for capacity and the client's view is only its own; in practice the most loaded task uses ~2x the CPU of the least loaded, "as badly as" Round Robin, p.173); **Weighted Round Robin** (backend reports rates and utilization; best result). [from source] (SRE p.170-174)

## Overload (Ch. 21)
- Options: degraded responses; errors; balance across datacenters. [from source] (SRE p.175)
- QPS is a poor capacity metric; prefer resources (CPU), "cost of a request". [from source] (SRE p.175-176)
- Per-client quotas (overcommit accepted); reject fast; **client-side adaptive throttling**: each client counts requests and accepts over the last two minutes; starts rejecting locally with increasing probability when requests exceeds K times accepts; K=2 preferred; the backend ends up rejecting ~1 for every 1 processed. [from source] (SRE p.176-177) In **global** overload the book says it is vital that the service deliver errors **only to the misbehaving clients**, keeping the others intact; therefore the owner provisions by negotiated utilization and sets per-client quotas. [from source] (SRE p.176) Global usage is aggregated in real time from the tasks and the effective limits are pushed to each task. [from source] (SRE p.176)
- Doorman: appears only in note 106 (SRE p.183), as an example of a cooperative, distributed client-side throttling system. The book does **not** describe its algorithm or parameters and does not formally recommend it; any internal detail is [extrapolation]. [from source] (SRE p.183)
- The probability formula is in a figure illegible in the PDF; `max(0, (requests − K·accepts)/(requests + 1))` is the form known from later literature. [extrapolation]
- Criticality: CRITICAL_PLUS, CRITICAL, SHEDDABLE_PLUS, SHEDDABLE; propagated by RPC; utilization signals (executor load average). [from source] (SRE p.177-178)
- Retries: if a large part of the datacenter is overloaded, do not retry; if a small part, retry; budget of up to 3 attempts per request and 10% retries per client (worst case drops from ~3x to 1.1x); attempt counter in the metadata; "overloaded; don't retry". [from source] (SRE p.179-180)
- Connections also cost ("Load from Connections"): load models by QPS alone ignore the CPU and memory cost of maintaining a **large pool of connections** and that of a **high rate of connection churn** — negligible in small systems, problematic in very-large-scale RPC. Health checks: the RPC protocol requires idle clients to perform periodic checks; after some idle time the client drops the TCP connection and switches to UDP; with very many clients with low request rates, the health check can cost more resources than serving the requests; adjustments: greatly reduce the frequency of health checks or create and destroy connections dynamically. Bursts of new connections: very large batch jobs that create a huge number of client workers at once can overload a group of backends (negotiating and holding excess connections at the same time). Two strategies: (1) expose load to **cross-datacenter** balancing (balance by cluster utilization, not just by number of requests, redirecting load to datacenters with slack); (2) force batch clients through a **batch proxy** (batch client → batch proxy → backend): only the proxy job suffers when the big batch starts, protecting backends and higher-priority clients; the proxy acts as a **fuse** ("fuse", in the book's words), and also reduces the number of connections on the backend, allowing larger subsets and a better view of backend state. The book gives no numbers for these connections (no health-check threshold nor pool size). [from source] (SRE p.182-183)
- Principle: the provisioned backend should keep serving its rate under excess (~2x to ~10x); common mistake: an overloaded backend shutting itself down. [from source] (SRE p.182-183)

## Cascading failures (Ch. 22)
- Definition: a failure that grows through positive feedback. Most common cause: server overload; resources (CPU, memory with "GC death spiral", threads, file descriptors) and dependencies among them. Definition and overload as the most common cause: p.184; exhaustion of resources (CPU, memory, threads, file descriptors) and dependencies among them: p.187-188 (file descriptors on p.187). [from source] (SRE p.184, 187-188)
- **Service unavailability (numerical example)**: a healthy service at 10,000 QPS that cascades (crashes) at 11,000 QPS; lowering the load to 9,000 QPS almost certainly does NOT stop the crashes, because the demand continues to be served with reduced capacity (only a small fraction of servers is healthy; it depends on how fast tasks start, how fast the binary serves at full capacity and how long a freshly started task survives the load). If 10% of servers are healthy, the request rate would need to drop to about 1,000 QPS for the system to stabilize and recover. [from source] (SRE p.188)
- Prevention (approximate priority order): load test the breaking point, degraded responses, server-side rejection, rejection in upper layers, capacity planning. [from source] (SRE p.188-189)
- Queues: small for steady traffic; load shedding; LIFO or CoDel instead of FIFO; exercised graceful degradation. [from source] (SRE p.189-191)
- **Load shedding, LIFO/CoDel queues and graceful degradation (p.189-190)** [from source]:
  - Load shedding by count: the server returns HTTP 503 to any new request when there are more than a given number of client requests in flight. (SRE p.189-190)
  - Why LIFO or CoDel instead of the default FIFO: take out of the queue the requests with little chance of being worth processing. The book's example: a web search whose RPC sat 10 seconds in the queue was probably already abandoned, the user reloaded the page and fired another request, so answering the first is useless. It works well together with propagation of RPC deadlines through the whole stack (chapter on latency and deadlines). More sophisticated approaches: identify clients to drop selectively or prioritize more important requests, which is usually necessary in shared services. (SRE p.190)
  - Graceful degradation is a step beyond load shedding: reduce the amount of work by worsening the quality of the response, for example a search that queries only a subset of the data in the in-memory cache instead of the on-disk database, or uses a faster, less accurate ranking under overload. (SRE p.190)
  - The book's evaluation questions: which metrics trigger the mode (CPU, latency, queue size, threads in use) and whether entering degraded mode is automatic or manual; what actions to take in that mode; at which layer to implement (all layers, or is a high-level chokepoint enough?). (SRE p.190)
  - The book's cautions: degradation should not trigger often (usually a failure of capacity planning or a sudden load change) and the system should stay simple; the code path that is never used is the one that tends not to work, so keep it alive by regularly running a small subset of servers near overload; monitor and alert when too many servers enter these modes; complex load shedding/degradation causes problems of its own (entering degraded mode by accident, feedback loops), so it must be possible to turn it off quickly or adjust parameters. (SRE p.190)
  - **Where to keep this configuration**: in a consistent system that each server watches (the book's example: Chubby) speeds up deploying the change, but introduces its own risk of synchronized failure of all servers. (SRE p.190)
- Retries: example 4 attempts per layer in 3 layers = 64 attempts on the database (4^3); always randomized exponential backoff, per-request limit and per-server budget (example 60/min), clear codes. [from source] (SRE p.190-192) Recognizing it in an emergency: it may not be obvious that the cause is bad retry behavior; retry-rate graphs can be read as a symptom, and not as an aggravating cause. Mitigation: a special case of insufficient capacity; one must fix the retry (usually a code push), reduce the load significantly or cut the requests entirely. [from source] (SRE p.192)
- Deadlines: propagate the absolute deadline, check before each stage, propagate cancellation; bimodal latency (example 5% of requests stuck consuming 5,000 threads, 80.4% error). [from source] (SRE p.192-194)
- Health checking: process health check ("does this binary respond?", relevant to the scheduler) is distinct from service health check ("does this binary respond to this class of requests now?", relevant to the load balancer); temporarily disabling health checks can stabilize the system if half the tasks are coming up and the other half will be killed for failing the health check. [from source] (SRE p.198)
- Cold cache (slow startup, p.194): several services serve almost everything from caches, so a miss costs much more; with a warm cache there are few misses, but with an empty cache 100% of requests are expensive (other services keep user state in RAM). Without provisioning for a cold cache the outage risk is higher. Scenarios: bringing up a new cluster (empty cache), returning a cluster to production after maintenance (cache possibly stale) and restart of a task with a cache. [from source] (SRE p.194)
- **Latency cache vs capacity cache**: with a latency cache the service sustains the expected load even with an empty cache; with a capacity cache it does not. Whoever adds caches must ensure they are latency caches or well designed to operate safely as capacity caches; sometimes caches are added later to improve performance and become a capacity dependency. Note 111 (p.200) says the same: a relevant part of the real capacity (or of the latency goal) may depend on the cache. [from source] (SRE p.194, 200)
- **Strategies against a cold cache**: overprovision the service; move the cache out of the server into a separate binary (such as memcache), which also allows sharing the cache among tasks, at the cost of an extra RPC (p.194); general prevention techniques (reject requests under overload/degraded modes and test behavior after events such as a large restart); when adding load to a cluster, **increase the load slowly** (the small initial rate warms the cache; with a hot cache, more traffic comes in) and keep all clusters at nominal load so caches stay warm (p.195). [from source] (SRE p.194-195) Restarting servers can worsen a cascade whose cause is a cold cache. [from source] (SRE p.198)
- Always go down the stack; avoid intra-layer communication on the user path. [from source] (SRE p.195)
- Triggers: process death/Query of Death, updates, rollouts, organic growth, planned changes, change in request profile, resource limits. [from source] (SRE p.195-196)
- Tests: up to the point of failure and beyond; popular clients; non-critical backends (blackhole). [from source] (SRE p.196-198)
- **Resource limits and idle CPU (p.196)**: with resource overcommit, there is usually spare CPU ("slack CPU", idle CPU) as a "safety net" against spikes, but the book says **relying on this slack is dangerous**: it depends on the behavior of the other jobs in the cluster and can vanish suddenly (e.g., a large MapReduce scheduled on many machines reduces the aggregate slack and causes starvation in unrelated jobs). When load testing, **stay within the committed resource limits**. [from source] (SRE p.196)
- **Test Until Failure and Beyond (p.196-197)**: understanding behavior under high load is perhaps the most important first step; test components until they break; ideally, past the limit the component serves errors or degraded results without greatly reducing the success rate, instead of crashing or serving errors en masse. The test also reveals the breaking point (basis of capacity planning: regressions, worst-case limits, utilization × headroom trade-off). **Because of cache effects, ramping the load gradually may give a different result than going at once to the expected load: test both gradual and impulse patterns.** Also test the **return to nominal load** after pushing well beyond it: does the component leave degraded mode **without human intervention**? if some servers crash under high load, how far must the load drop for the system to stabilize? In a service with state or a cache, the test must track state between interactions and check correctness under high load (where subtle concurrency bugs appear). **Each component may have a different breaking point**: test each separately. With overload protections in place, the book suggests failures tested on a small slice of production, with extra capacity for manual failover: reduce tasks quickly or slowly, lose a cluster's capacity, blackhole backends. [from source] (SRE p.197)
- Immediate steps (7): increase resources, stop health check failures, restart, drop traffic, degraded modes, eliminate batch, eliminate bad traffic. [from source] (SRE p.198-199) Package note [inference]: the book does not state that the order of this list is a priority ranking (unlike the prevention list, which it calls "rough priority order", p.188). Restarting servers: the book suggests it when Java servers are in a GC death spiral, when requests without a deadline consume threads or when there is deadlock, but only after identifying the origin of the cascade, checking that it does not merely shift the load, in a canary and slowly, since it can amplify the cascade if the cause is something like a cold cache. [from source] (SRE p.198)
- When dropping traffic to get out of a crash loop: address the trigger cause (e.g., add capacity), reduce the load enough to stop the crashes, and "consider being aggressive": if the whole service is in a crash loop, let through only ~1% of the traffic; let most servers become healthy and raise the load gradually (caches warm up, connections get established). It causes visible user harm. [from source] (SRE p.198)
- Closing example (Shakespeare): after a broadcast, the Asian datacenter's traffic exceeds capacity, combined with a simultaneous update; graceful degradation and randomized backoff already existed, but the tasks fail and are restarted by Borg; SRE is paged and restores by increasing the number of tasks; the postmortem proposes that GSLB redirect traffic to neighboring datacenters and that autoscaling be turned on so tasks grow with traffic. [from source] (SRE p.199)
- Closing: steady-state improvements (retries, caching, killing unhealthy servers) can increase the risk of large-scale failure. [from source] (SRE p.199)

## Numerical details and mechanisms of load balancing (Ch. 19-20) [from source]
**Frontend: what is "optimal" and how Google forwards VIP packets (Ch. 19).**
- "Optimal" depends on the level and the traffic: for a search what matters is latency (goes to the closest datacenter in RTT); for a video upload it is throughput (may go by another path, even over an underused link, at the cost of latency). Inside the datacenter all machines are assumed equidistant, and the focus becomes utilization and protecting servers from overload; sometimes traffic is sent to a slightly more distant datacenter to keep caches warm. (SRE p.159-160)
- DNS: the intermediary (recursive resolver) has three consequences: the IP seen by the authoritative server is the resolver's and not the user's (EDNS0 with the client subnet is a partial solution, not an official standard in 2016); non-deterministic response paths; caching (low TTL, which limits propagation speed; not every resolver respects the TTL, note 104). Google monitors the resolvers and the size of the user base behind each one and estimates the geographic distribution; "best location" = closest, with capacity and with a healthy datacenter/network, integrating authoritative DNS with the global control systems. (SRE p.160-162)
- **VIP and mod N**: the VIP network load balancer could prefer the least loaded backend, but that fails with stateful protocols (the balancer would have to track all connections). The stateless alternative is to compute a connection ID from the packet and use `id(packet) mod N`: if a backend leaves the list, **N becomes N−1 and almost all packets now map to another backend**, which **resets almost all connections** when the backends do not share state. **Consistent hashing** (proposed in 1997) keeps the mapping relatively stable; Google usually uses simple connection tracking and **resorts to consistent hashing under pressure** (e.g., a denial-of-service attack). (SRE p.161-162)
- Forwarding: **NAT** requires one entry per connection in the tracking table, which prevents a fully stateless fallback; **layer 2 (swapping the destination MAC)** allows **DSR**, in which the backend responds directly to the sender (a big saving when requests are small and responses large, as in HTTP) and with no state in the balancer, but requires balancers and backends to be in the **same broadcast domain** ("Google overcame this long ago"). The **current solution** (in 2016) is **GRE encapsulation**: the balancer puts the forwarded packet inside another IP+GRE packet with the backend's address as destination; the backend removes the envelope and processes the inner packet; the two need not be in the same broadcast domain, "they can even be on different continents". Cost: the **24-byte overhead (IPv4+GRE)** can exceed the MTU and require fragmentation; inside the datacenter this is avoided with a **larger MTU**, which requires a network with large PDUs. (SRE p.162) [DATED]
- A DNS response fits in 512 bytes (RFC 1035, note 105; otherwise the user must open TCP just to get the list of IPs), which limits the number of addresses per response to something "almost certainly smaller than the number of servers". (SRE p.161-162)

**Datacenter: sizes, waste and subsetting (Ch. 20).**
- The smallest service has at least 3 processes (with fewer, losing a machine costs 50% or more of capacity); the largest can exceed 10,000; the typical one has 100 to 1,000. The GFE (Google Frontend), an HTTP reverse proxy, uses these algorithms to route to applications. (SRE p.163)
- Waste: the sum, over tasks, of (CPU of the most loaded − CPU of task i). Example: reserve 1,000 CPUs and be unable to use more than ~700. (SRE p.164)
- Flow control: a limit of 100 active requests per backend is reasonable for most; it only protects against extreme overload and can backfire (long requests make all backends look unreachable). Lame duck: the state is propagated to active clients and, via UDP health checks, to inactive ones in **1 or 2 RTT**; clean shutdown has 5 steps (SIGTERM, lame duck, in-flight requests finish, counter drops to zero, exits or is killed after an interval of 10 to 150 s). (SRE p.164-166)
- **Subsetting**: typical subset of 20 to 100 backends; larger subset if there are few clients relative to backends or imbalance among clients (bursts, fan-out). If subsetting overloads a backend by 10%, the whole set needs to be overprovisioned by 10%. (SRE p.166-167)
- **Random subsetting (bad)**: with 300 clients and 300 backends, a 30% subset (90 backends per client), the least loaded backend receives **63% of the average load** (57 connections, average 90) and the most loaded **121%** (109); with a 10% subset (30 backends) they go to **50%** (15) and **150%** (45); to spread well, subsets of ~75% would be needed, which is infeasible. (SRE p.167-168)
- **Deterministic subsetting (Google's solution)**: subset_count = backends ÷ subset size; clients grouped into "rounds" of subset_count clients; each round shuffles the backend list with seed = round number; the client's subset is the block `client_id mod subset_count`. Each backend is assigned to exactly one client per round; the shuffle avoids consecutive blocks (which may fall together in an in-order rollout) and different seeds per round spread the load of a failed backend across all the remaining ones, not just its subset. Example: 12 backends, subset 3, rounds of 4 clients; with 300 clients and a subset of 10 out of 300 backends, **every backend receives the same number of connections**; in general the difference is **at most 1**. (SRE p.168-170)

**Load balancing policies: numbers and limitations (p.170-174).**
- **Simple Round Robin**: for years the most common policy and still in use; it is much better than random choice, but can result in **up to 2x spread in CPU consumption between the least and the most loaded task**. Causes: (a) small subsetting with clients of different rates; (b) variable query cost (the most expensive can consume **1,000x or more** CPU than the cheapest; services accept 100, 1,000 or 10,000x; Java backend example: ~15 ms of CPU on average, but some queries up to 10 s; mitigation: limit the work per request, e.g., paginate "the 100 most recent"); (c) machine diversity (equivalence between CPUs; "2 units on the slow machine = 0.8 on the fast one"; virtual unit **GCU**); (d) unpredictable factors: antagonistic neighbors (differences of up to 20%) and restarts (a freshly started task consumes far more for minutes; Google keeps servers in lame duck and pre-warms them). (SRE p.170-172)
- **Least-Loaded Round Robin**: each client counts its active requests per backend and uses Round Robin among those with the minimum count (example with t0..t9). A trap discovered "the hard way": a sick backend that responds with 100% errors quickly **attracts traffic** ("sinkholing"); fix: count recent errors as active requests. **Two limitations**: (1) the active count can be a **poor proxy for capacity**, since many requests spend their lives waiting for the network (a backend on a machine with a 2x faster CPU but the same latency should receive 2x more requests, but is seen as equally loaded); (2) each client's count **ignores the requests of other clients** to the same backends (local view). Practical result: large services with Least-Loaded see the **most loaded task use twice the CPU of the least loaded, "as badly as" Round Robin**. (SRE p.172-173)
- **Weighted Round Robin**: each client keeps a capability score per backend; each response (including to health checks) carries rates of queries and errors per second and the utilization (typically CPU); failures are penalized. In practice it drastically reduced the difference between the most and least utilized task (Fig. 20-6, on migrating from Least-Loaded to WRR). (SRE p.173-174)

**Overload: numbers from Ch. 21.**
- If a datacenter runs 100 tasks that process up to 500 req/s each, the global balancer should not send more than 50,000 QPS to it (and even that may not suffice at scale). (SRE p.175) [derived: 100 × 500]
- Capacity in resources, not in QPS: e.g., 500 cores and 1 TB of memory; CPU works as a signal because, with GC, memory pressure turns into CPU consumption. (SRE p.175-176)
- Per-client quotas with **overcommit**: a service with 10,000 global CPUs gives quotas of Gmail 4,000, Calendar 4,000, Android 3,000, Google+ 2,000 and "the rest" 500 CPU-seconds per second; the sum is 13,500, **1.35x the allocation** [derived], betting that not all reach the limit at the same time. (SRE p.176) In global overload, the book states that only misbehaving clients should receive errors. [from source] (SRE p.176) The 50,000 QPS limit per datacenter (100 tasks x 500 QPS) cited in the book is what cross-datacenter load balancing must not let be exceeded (p.175), and the book notes that even that may be insufficient.
- Rejecting can cost almost as much as accepting (e.g., a simple lookup in RAM), and the backend can become overloaded just rejecting: hence **adaptive throttling** (2-minute window, local rejection when `requests` reaches K × `accepts`; **K=2** preferred; with K=1.1 the backend rejects only 1 in 10 accepted; K=2 wastes a bit at the backend but propagates the state change faster; in large overload the backend ends up rejecting one for every one processed; does not work well with clients that send very sporadically). (SRE p.176-177)
- Criticality: the default for production jobs is CRITICAL and for batch it is SHEDDABLE_PLUS; the client propagates the criticality by default to child calls; it is set near browsers/HTTP frontends; it is orthogonal to latency (search suggestions are sheddable but require low latency); the most general utilization signal is the **executor load average** (active threads, smoothed by exponential decay). (SRE p.177-178)
- Retries: the worst case of retries without a budget is "just under 3X"; with the 10% per-client budget it drops to **1.1x**; the attempt counter (0, 1, 2) goes in the metadata and the backend uses histograms to return "overloaded; don't retry"; retry **only at the layer immediately above** (example DB Frontend → Backend B → Backend A) to avoid combinatorial explosion. (SRE p.179-180)

**Cascade: the 9-step chain and what consumes what (Ch. 22).**
- CPU: more requests in flight, long queues, thread starvation, watchdogs, missed RPC deadlines (wasted work and more retries), less CPU cache benefit. Memory: dead tasks, GC death spiral (less CPU → slow requests → more RAM → more GC), drop in cache hit rate. Threads and file descriptors: starvation, health check failure, even running out of PIDs. (SRE p.187)
- The book's chained example: Java frontend with poorly tuned GC → CPU exhausted → slower requests → more RAM per request → less RAM for cache → more cache misses → more load on the backend → backend out of CPU/threads → health checks fail and the cascade begins; the chain is rarely diagnosed in full during the failure, especially if frontend and backend have different owners. (SRE p.188)

## Ch. 22 numbers that were missing [from source]
- **Capacity planning and cascade** (p.189): if each cluster's breaking point is 5,000 QPS, the load spreads equally and the service's peak is 19,000 QPS, you need "approximately six clusters" for N+2 [derived: 19,000 ÷ 5,000 = 3.8 → 4, plus 2 of redundancy = 6]; the book warns that capacity planning reduces the probability of a cascade but is not enough, and that the assumption of uniform load across clusters often fails because of geography (note 109; the mark is on p.189 and the note text on p.200). Rate limiting can be done in reverse proxies, load balancers and tasks, but, since it ignores global health, it does not stop a failure already under way. (SRE p.189)
- **Queues**: if the queue is 10x the size of the thread pool and each request takes 100 ms on a thread, a request in a full queue takes **1.1 s** (almost all waiting in the queue); queues should be small relative to the pool (e.g., 50% or less) to reject early; CoDel and LIFO. (SRE p.189-190)
- **Amplifying retries** (example code): backend with a limit of 10,000 QPS per task; the frontend calls at 10,100 QPS (100 QPS above); the 100 that fail are retried every 1,000 ms and come back as load, so the backend receives 10,200 QPS, then 10,300 and so on: fewer requests succeed on the first attempt and more file descriptors and memory are consumed; even if the rate returned to 9,000 QPS, the system may remain collapsed. Retry budget per server (e.g., 60 retries per minute per process) and **64 attempts (4^3)** with 3 retries per layer across three layers. (SRE p.190-192)
- **Deadlines**: propagation (example: A sets 30 s, spends 7 s and gives 23 s to B; B spends 4 s and gives 19 s to C); without propagation, B with a fixed deadline of 20 s and C picking up the request after 5 s would work 15 s on a request that A had already abandoned (A gave 10 s and B took 8 s to start: 2 s remained). (SRE p.192-193)
- **Bimodal latency**: 10 servers with 100 threads (1,000 threads); 1,000 QPS at 100 ms occupy 100 threads; if 5% of requests never finish and the deadline is 100 s, those 50 QPS consume **5,000 threads (50 × 100 s)**; only 19.6% of requests are served (1,000 ÷ (5,000 + 95)) and the error rises to **80.4%**, instead of 5%; mitigations: look at the distribution and not just the average, fail fast, deadlines not three orders of magnitude above the average, limit threads per client/keyspace (for example 25% of threads). (SRE p.193-194)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 3): rules of p.189 and p.193 and notes of Ch. 21-22 [from source]
- **Outgoing deadline (p.193).** (a) Reduce the outgoing deadline slightly, by **a few hundred milliseconds**, to cover network transit and post-processing on the client. (b) Consider a **cap (upper bound) on outgoing deadlines**: limit how long the server waits for RPCs to **non-critical** backends or to backends that usually respond quickly. (c) The book's caveat: understand the traffic mix, because a poorly chosen cap can make certain request types (large payload, heavy computation) **always fail**. (d) Exception: in an expensive "catchup" operation that writes checkpoints periodically, it is advisable to check the deadline **only after writing the checkpoint**, and not after the expensive operation. (SRE p.193)
- **Cancellation and hedged requests (p.193).** Propagating cancellations reduces unnecessary work; in "hedged requests" the client sends the RPC to a primary server and, after some time, to other instances, and on receiving the first response cancels the others; these requests can spread in a cascade, so cancellation must be propagated through the whole stack. It also avoids the leak in which the initial RPC has a long deadline but the deeper critical RPCs fail with no chance of success (with deadline propagation alone, the initial call would keep using resources until the timeout). (SRE p.193)
- **Queue under bursty traffic (p.189).** Queues sit in front of the thread pool; if rate and latency were constant there would be no reason to queue. For **steady** traffic, a small queue relative to the pool (50% or less), rejecting early; **Gmail uses queueless servers**, relying on failover to other tasks when threads run out. For **bursty** traffic (patterns that oscillate drastically), the book says the queue size can be based on **three variables: the current number of threads in use, the processing time of each request and the size and frequency of the bursts**. The book gives neither a formula nor a value for this. (SRE p.189)
- **Rate limiting (p.189)** can live in reverse proxies (per IP, against DoS and abusive clients), in load balancers (drop above X req/s, indiscriminately or selectively, for example dropping background sync but keeping interactive sessions) and in tasks (against random fluctuations of balancing); since it generally ignores the global health of the service, it may not stop a failure already under way, and simple implementations leave idle capacity.
- **Retries and balancing (p.179).** To the balancer, a retry is indistinguishable from a new request: the book does not force the retry to go to another task, it bets on probability (the subset has many backends). Rejecting retries and new requests quickly and equally turns the retry into a form of organic balancing. If a **large** fraction of the datacenter's tasks is overloaded, no retry is made and the error propagates up to the caller; if a **small** fraction (the typical case), an immediate retry is preferred; the extra retry costs only a few network round trips because clients are close to the backends. (SRE p.179)
- **Notes (p.200).** Note 108: a **watchdog** is usually a thread that wakes periodically to see whether work has happened since the last check; if not, it assumes the server hung and kills it; requests of a known type can be sent at regular intervals. Note 110: an exercise for the reader, write a simulator and see how much useful work the backend delivers according to the overload and the number of retries allowed. Note 111: sometimes a significant part of the real serving capacity depends on a **cache**, and without it it would not serve as much; it also applies to latency (the cache may be what allows meeting the target). (SRE p.200)
- **The "2x the average" rule for deadlines is not from the book**: the book only says the deadline should not be orders of magnitude above the average latency (p.193-194); "2x the average" (or any fixed multiple) does not appear.
