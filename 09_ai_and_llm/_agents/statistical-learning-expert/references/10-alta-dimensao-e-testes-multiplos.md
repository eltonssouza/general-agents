# 10. High dimension (p ≥ n) and multiple testing

Sources: ISLR 6.4 (PDF 253-258, read) and ESL ch. 18: 18.1-18.2 (PDF 668-671) and 18.7 (PDF 702-711). **Not read in ESL ch. 18**: 18.2 onward from PDF 672 to 701 (linear classifiers with ℓ₂ and ℓ₁ regularization, fused lasso, string kernels, supervised PCA, etc.) and PDF 712-717. **The ISLR 1st edition has no chapter on multiple testing** (the topic is in the 2nd edition; see `datado-edicoes.md`): here the topic is ESL only. Convention: PDF page (ISLR printed = PDF − 15; ESL = PDF − 19).

## 1. What goes wrong when p ≥ n (ISLR)
- `[from source]` Classical regression and classification methods assume n ≫ p; data with p > n (genomics: n ≈ 200 and p ≈ 500,000 SNPs; "bag-of-words") are called **high-dimensional**; the considerations also hold if p is only slightly smaller than n (ISLR p.253-254). With p ≈ n least squares interpolates: with n = 2 and two parameters the fit is perfect and useless (ISLR p.254-255).
- `[from source]` Simulation with n = 20 and variables **unrelated** to the response: R² → 1 and training MSE → 0 as variables are added, while the test MSE explodes (ISLR p.255-256). **Cp, AIC and BIC are not appropriate**: the estimated σ̂² becomes 0; adjusted R² can reach 1 (ISLR p.256).
- `[from source]` What works: *forward* selection, ridge, lasso and PCR, because they are less flexible than least squares. Three lessons (Fig. 6.24 with p = 20, 50 and 2,000 and 20 true variables): (1) regularization is essential, (2) the choice of the tuning parameter is crucial, (3) the test error **grows** with dimensionality unless the new variables are relevant: **curse of dimensionality**; noise variables worsen the result (ISLR p.256-258).
- `[from source]` **Interpretation**: multicollinearity is extreme (any variable is a linear combination of the others); one never knows which variables are truly predictive; 17 SNPs chosen by *forward* are not "better" than others: another data set would give another set, perhaps without overlap; report the result as one of many possible models, to be validated on independent data; **never** use the sum of squares, p-values or R² from training as evidence of good fit in high dimension (an R² = 1 with p > n proves nothing); the MSE or R² on an independent test set is valid, the training MSE is not; report error on an independent test set or CV (ISLR p.258-259).

## 2. ESL 18.1-18.2: "fitting less is better"
- `[from source]` With p ≫ N the problem is high variance and overfitting, and "simple, heavily regularized methods are often the ones chosen" (ESL p.668). Simulation: N = 100, p = 20, 100 and 1,000, signal-to-noise ratio 2; ridge with effective df 20, 35 and 43 (λ = 0.001, 100 and 1000) wins in each case, respectively (ESL p.668-670). With p ≫ N there is no information to estimate the high-dimensional covariance, so it pays to regularize more (ESL p.670).
- `[from source]` Classification with diagonal LDA (independence rule, a special case of *naive Bayes*, equivalent to standardized *nearest centroid*) and *nearest shrunken centroids*, which shrink the class means toward the overall mean, feature by feature (ESL p.670-671).

## 3. Multiple testing and FDR (ESL 18.7; NO equivalent in ISLR 1st ed.)
- `[from source]` With M tests, the p-value of each one is used; radiosensitivity example: M = 12,625 genes, 44 normal samples and 14 sensitive; t per gene = (x̄₂ⱼ − x̄₁ⱼ)/se_j with pooled standard error (18.38-18.39); 1,189 genes with |t| ≥ 2, against 631.3 expected by chance (standard deviation 24.5) (ESL p.703-704). **Permutation** p-values of the labels avoid distributional assumptions: p_j = (1/K)Σ I(|t^k_j| > |t_j|) (18.40) or pooled across genes (18.41), with K = 1,000 of the C(58, 14) ≈ 10¹³ permutations (ESL p.704-705).
- `[from source]` **FWER** = probability of at least one false rejection; for independent tests it is 1 − (1 − α)^M; **Bonferroni** rejects if p_j < α/M and guarantees FWER ≤ α, but is conservative for large M (0.05/12,625 = 3.9 × 10⁻⁶: no gene passed) (ESL p.705).
- `[from source]` **FDR** = E(V/R), the expected proportion of false discoveries among those declared significant (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) (ESL p.706). **BH algorithm (18.2)**: sort the p-values p₍₁₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍M₎; L = max{j: p₍j₎ < α·j/M}; reject all hypotheses with p_j ≤ p₍L₎; under independence, FDR ≤ (M₀/M)·α ≤ α (ESL p.706-707). With α = 0.15, 11 genes are rejected (|t| ≥ 4.101) (ESL p.707-708). **Plug-in** estimate (Algorithm 18.3): FDR ≈ Ê(V)/R_obs, here 1,518/1000/11 ≈ 14%, equivalent to BH with permutation p-values (ESL p.708-709). The book reminds us that FDR is **not** type I error; an FDR of 0.15 can be useful in exploratory studies (ESL p.709). SAM (asymmetric cutoffs) and the Bayesian pFDR: ESL p.709-711.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the individual p-values of the fit as valid selection (ISLR p.92 and ESL p.79).
- Using Bonferroni with thousands of tests without an alternative (ESL p.705).
- Using BH without knowing whether the hypotheses are independent (the book's FDR guarantee assumes independence: ESL p.706-707).
- Doing predictor selection outside cross-validation (ESL p.264-266).
