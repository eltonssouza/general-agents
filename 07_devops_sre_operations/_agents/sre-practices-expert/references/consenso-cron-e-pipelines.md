# Distributed consensus, distributed cron and pipelines (Ch. 23-25)

## Consensus (Ch. 23) [DATED in the implementations]
- Wherever there is leader election, critical shared state or distributed locking, use formally proven and well-tested consensus; informal approaches (heartbeats, gossip) lead to outages and inconsistencies. [from source] (SRE p.201, p.204)
- CAP theorem: consistency, availability and partition tolerance do not coexist; partitions are inevitable. ACID vs BASE (p.201-203). **BASE (p.203)**: Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency; useful for certain data and large volumes that would be expensive or infeasible with ACID; they usually use multimaster replication, in which concurrent writes are resolved by some mechanism, often "latest timestamp wins", and this gives eventual consistency, which can be surprising especially with **clock drift** (inevitable in distributed systems) or a **network partition**. Cost to developers: the book quotes Jeff Shute saying they spend a significant fraction of their time on complex, error-prone mechanisms to deal with possibly stale data, an "unacceptable burden" that should be solved in the database. Conclusion on p.203: **correctness is not sacrificed for reliability or performance, especially in critical state**; the example is a **financial transactions** system, in which performance requirements matter little if the financial data is incorrect; hence consensus to synchronize critical state. [from source] (SRE p.203)
- Three failure case studies: split-brain with STONITH and heartbeats, failover that requires human intervention, faulty group membership via gossip. [from source] (SRE p.203-204)
- Asynchronous consensus; crash-fail vs crash-recover; Byzantine; the FLP result (progress cannot be guaranteed); Paxos (numbered proposals, majority, persistent journal), Raft, Zab, Mencius. [from source] (SRE p.204-205)
- Paxos in detail (p.205): each proposal has a unique sequence number that imposes strict ordering; in phase 1 the proposer sends the number and each acceptor accepts it only if it has not seen a higher number (the proposer may try again with a higher one); with majority agreement, the proposer commits by sending the value. Two different values cannot be committed for the same proposal because **any two majorities overlap in at least one node**; the acceptor writes a journal to persistent storage when it agrees, to honor the guarantees after a restart. **Paxos alone is of little use**: it only agrees on one value and one proposal number, a single time; and since only a quorum needs to agree, **a node may not have a complete view** of the values already agreed (a limitation common to most consensus algorithms). That is why higher-level components are used (datastores, queues, locks, leader election, RSM). The 2f+1 rule is on p.216 (not on p.217). [from source] (SRE p.205)
- Patterns: replicated state machines, replicated datastores, leader election, locks with renewable leases, queues, atomic broadcast. [from source] (SRE p.205-208)
- Performance: Multi-Paxos with a strong leader; dueling proposers; quorum leases; Fast Paxos not always faster; stable leaders; batching and pipelining; disk write per commitment (10 ms per write → about 100 operations/s). [from source] (SRE p.210-216) **Assumptions of the book on p.216 for this 100 ops/s**: negligible network RTTs and the proposer writing the log in parallel with the acceptors; the book does **not** give another number such as 50,000 ops/s for consensus (the 50,000 QPS on p.175 is a per-datacenter load balancing limit, a different topic). Optimizations (p.216): **combine the consensus log and the RSM log** into one (avoids alternating between two locations on disk and reduces seeks); logs go straight to disk, but state writes can go to an **in-memory cache and be written later**, reordered; and **batching** of operations at the proposer, which amortizes fixed disk and network cost. [from source] (SRE p.216)
- Replicas: majority quorum 2f+1 tolerates f failures (3f+1 with Byzantine); minimum 3; 5 tolerate 2 failures; losing quorum can lose data; adding a replica can reduce the percentage availability (5 → quorum 3, 40%; 6 → quorum 4, 33%). [from source] (SRE p.216-218)
- Placement by failure domains; hierarchical quorum (9 replicas in 3 groups) as an alternative to uneven RTTs. [from source] (SRE p.217-222)
- Monitor: members per group, lagging replicas, existence of a leader, leader changes (view/term), consensus transaction number, latencies. [from source] (SRE p.222-223)
- Final message: any approach weaker than consensus for leader election is a "time bomb". [from source] (SRE p.224)

## Distributed cron (Ch. 24)
- Single-machine cron has a one-machine failure domain; anacron recovers lost jobs on single machines. [from source] (SRE p.225-226)
- Prefer skipping to duplicating a launch when the infrastructure allows it ("fail closed"); owners monitor their jobs. [from source] (SRE p.226)
- Decouple processes from machines; partial launch failures (several RPCs). [from source] (SRE p.226-227)
- Replicas with Paxos and a leader that launches jobs; launch announced synchronously (start and end); the new leader completes "open launches"; failover well within one minute. [from source] (SRE p.228-230)
- Resolving partial failures requires idempotent operations or queryable state; precomputed job names that include the time. [from source] (SRE p.230)
- Paxos logs on local disk; snapshots backed up on a distributed filesystem. [from source] (SRE p.231)
- Thundering herd: crontab extension with "?" so that cron picks the value by hash. [from source] (SRE p.231)

## Pipelines (Ch. 25)
- A periodic pipeline is fragile: uneven distribution of work, hanging chunk, low-priority batch with preemptions, minimum average delay, monitoring only at the end, thundering herd, Moiré load pattern. [from source] (SRE p.233-236)
- If the problem is continuous or will grow to be, do not use a periodic pipeline. [from source] (SRE p.240)
- Google Workflow: Task Master (system prevalence), stateless workers, leases, uniquely named output, server token; exactly-once. [from source] (SRE p.238-240)
- Server token (p.240): the scenario it prevents is one Task Master being replaced by another at the same network address, memory corruption that changes the IP or port and leads to another Task Master, or a misconfiguration with a **load balancer in front of independent Task Masters**; the token (a unique identifier of the Task Master) goes in the metadata of each task and client and server check it on every operation, preventing a subtle misconfiguration in which everything runs fine until a task identifier collision occurs. Why not a database (p.240): using Spanner or another database directly is possible, but since each task is unique and immutable (the lease is part of the task) and the transaction log would act as the journal, **every read** would have to be part of a **long-running transaction**, which is possible and "terribly inefficient". Continuity (p.240): the Task Master keeps journals in Spanner (globally consistent, low throughput), elects the writer with the distributed lock service Chubby (result persisted in Spanner) and clients locate the current Task Master via internal **naming services**; local workflows in distinct clusters use reference tasks in the global Workflow, and if the reference task cannot be removed from the global one, the **local Workflow blocks** until the global one returns, to guarantee transactional correctness. [from source] (SRE p.240)
- Business continuity: journals in Spanner, election via Chubby, reference tasks and heartbeat task. [from source] (SRE p.240)

## Consensus as a service, replicas and latency (Ch. 23, details) [from source]
- **Consensus as a service (etcd is only named).** The book says that many systems that use consensus successfully do so as **clients of a service** that implements it, citing **Zookeeper, Consul and etcd**; Zookeeper was the first open source consensus system to gain traction because it is easy to use even in applications not designed for consensus; **Chubby** fills a similar niche at Google. Argument (from the Chubby authors): offering consensus primitives as a service, rather than as an embedded library, frees the application maintainer from deploying the system in the way compatible with a highly available consensus service (right number of replicas, group membership, performance). (SRE p.205) **The book says nothing more about etcd**: no installation, flags, TLS, tuning, operation, comparison with Zookeeper/Consul or version.
- Replicated state machine (RSM): any deterministic program can become a highly available service if implemented as an RSM; operations are globally ordered by consensus; sliding window pattern to synchronize replicas. (SRE p.205)
- Number of replicas: a group of 2f+1 tolerates f failures (3f+1 for Byzantine); the minimum is 3 (with 2 no failure is tolerated); **three replicas tolerate one failure and "most system downtime comes from planned maintenance"**, so 3 allow operating with one replica under maintenance (if the other two can bear the load); if an unplanned failure occurs during maintenance, the consensus system becomes unavailable, which is why **five replicas** (up to two failures) are recommended. With 4 of 5 remaining no intervention is needed; with 3 remaining it is advisable to add one or two replicas. Without quorum the system is, in theory, in an unrecoverable state (the durable logs of at least one missing replica are inaccessible) and forcing a membership change always admits data loss. Raft: if a group of five loses all but the leader, the leader has complete knowledge of the committed decisions; if the lost majority included the leader, there are no strong guarantees about how far behind the others are. (SRE p.216)
- Trade-offs of the number of replicas: reliability, frequency of planned maintenance, risk, performance and cost (in sharded systems such as Photon each extra replica is multiplied by the number of shards). A minority of slow replicas can lag behind without harming the quorum. (SRE p.217)
- **More replicas can worsen availability**: typical Zookeeper/Chubby use 5 replicas (quorum 3; 2 replicas, or **40%**, can be missing); with **6** replicas the quorum is 4 and only **33%** can be missing. If there are five datacenters and each group has one replica per datacenter, losing one datacenter still leaves one spare replica; when the **sixth replica is put in one of the five datacenters**, losing that datacenter takes down two replicas and leaves quorum **with no redundancy at all** (Fig. 23-10). Putting several members in the same failure domain reduces availability. (SRE p.218-219)
- **Widening the tolerated failure domain does not always pay off (p.217).** The greater the distance between replicas, the greater the RTT and the latency of operations, and the greater the tolerated failure; the balance depends on the system. The book's argument: if all clients are in the same failure domain (the New York region) and spreading consensus over a wider area would allow continued serving during a failure there (hurricane Sandy in the original), it is probably not worth it, because the clients will be down too, the system will see no traffic, and the extra cost in latency, throughput and compute would go unrewarded. Disaster recovery is another decision: consensus replicas are online copies, but critical data requires backup snapshots elsewhere. [from source] (SRE p.217)
- Failure domains: two that never go away are the software itself (bugs) and human administration error; what matters is the latency perceived by the client; leaderless protocols (Mencius, Egalitarian Paxos) may have an advantage in a WAN. To read from replicas that are not the leader, read capacity is increased by adding replicas, but in a strong-leader protocol each extra replica increases the leader's load. (SRE p.217-218)
- Hierarchical quorum: nine replicas in three groups of three, forming a quorum by majority of groups; it avoids depending on a central "link" replica whose loss increases the RTT of every quorum. (SRE p.221-223)
- Performance and network: RTT within a datacenter on the order of 1 ms; **45 ms in the US and 70 ms from New York to London**; initial TCP windows of 4 to 15 KB; regional proxies keep persistent connections for distant clients; Fast Paxos can be slower than classic Paxos in some cases; batching and pipelining amortize the fixed cost of the disk log; **a random 10 ms write limits consensus to ~100 operations per second**, so 5 ms per write [derived] would allow ~200 operations/s without batching. (SRE p.213-216)

## Distributed cron: why skip and why state lives in cron itself (Ch. 24, details) [from source]
- **Note 115 (p.232, in the Summary of Ch. 24; called out in the reliability analysis, p.225)**: the failure of individual jobs is outside the scope of the chapter's analysis, which deals with the cron service (Paxos, skipped/duplicated launches) and not with why a specific job fails. [from source] (SRE p.232; p.225)
- **Why prefer skipping to duplicating** (p.226): recovering from a skipped launch is more feasible than from a double one, because owners can and should monitor their jobs (cron can expose state or there is independent monitoring of the effect); **undoing a double launch, such as a newsletter, can be difficult or impossible**; hence "fail closed" so as not to create systemically bad state. It depends on the job: garbage collection is idempotent and can run several times (or skip a run every 5 minutes); a newsletter cannot be launched more than once; a monthly payroll cannot be skipped.
- The failure domain of common cron = one machine; the only state that survives restarts is the crontab, and launches are "fire and forget" (anacron keeps the timestamp of the last launch and reruns daily or less frequent jobs). Hosting cron on 1 machine of a 1,000-machine datacenter would make 1/1,000 of the fleet take the whole service down; so processes are decoupled from machines (launching becomes an RPC to the datacenter scheduler). Rescheduling takes longer than the smallest interval of 1 minute; saving state only in GFS would delay a 5-minute job by 1 to 2 minutes, which may be unacceptable (hot spares help). (SRE p.225-227)
- **Why state belongs to cron itself (p.227-228)**: the information about jobs is very small and small writes on distributed file systems (GFS, HDFS, built for large files) are expensive and slow; base services whose failures have wide impact should have **few dependencies** and work for some time even if parts of the datacenter disappear.
- Paxos: several replicas, **a single leader** that modifies state and launches jobs (the Fast Paxos leader coincides with the cron leader); the leader's death is detected in seconds and the new leader takes over within the tolerable **one minute**; the start and end of each launch are reported **synchronously** to a quorum; a leader that loses leadership must immediately stop interacting with the scheduler (mutual exclusion). Each launch is identified by the job and the **start time** (essential in high-frequency jobs). Partial failure: either the external operations are idempotent or their state is queryable; practical solution: **precomputed job names with the scheduled time**, distributed to the replicas, so that the new leader queries the names and launches those that are missing. (SRE p.228-230)
- Storage: the Paxos log grows, so snapshots are taken; **snapshots are the most critical state** (losing them means starting from scratch), while losing logs is limited loss; logs and snapshots are kept on the **local disk of the 3 replicas** and snapshots also backed up on the distributed file system; logs do not go to the distributed FS (small-write penalty); a new replica fetches state from another replica over the network. (SRE p.231)
- Thundering herd: "0 0 * * *" in 30 teams launching MapReduce with thousands of workers at midnight; solution: the "?" of the extended crontab picks a value by hash of the configuration; even so the global launch graph still has peaks (jobs with a fixed time due to an external dependency). (SRE p.231-232)

## Pipelines (Ch. 25, details) [from source]
- Pipeline depth = number of chained programs (from 1 to dozens or hundreds); periodic pipelines (MapReduce, Flume) are stable only while there are enough workers; the collective experience of SREs is that the periodic model is **fragile**: the initial tuning of workers, periodicity and chunking degrades with growth. (SRE p.233-234)
- Uneven work: an indivisible unit (customer) limits the total time to that of the largest customer; "hanging chunk" blocks the next stage (e.g. sorting requires all the data); the "standard" response of killing and restarting the job wastes all the work because there is usually no checkpoint. Batch runs at low priority (Borg), with open-ended start delay, cost inversely proportional to the accepted delay and risk of preemption; resources in a shared, distributed pool obey supply and demand, and development teams resist acquiring resources that go to a common pool, so the book asks to distinguish **batch scheduling** resources from **production priority** resources to rationalize acquisition cost (p.234-235); below a certain interval the gain turns into undesirable behavior (stacked runs or the nearly finished one killed): in the Fig. 25-1 example, reducing the interval well below **40 minutes** for a **~20-minute** job causes overlapping runs. (SRE p.234)
- Monitoring only at the end of the job (no statistics if the job fails midway); thundering herd of thousands of workers and naive retry; the human error of **adding more workers** when the job does not finish; "nothing is harder on the infrastructure and on SREs than a pipeline job with 10,000 buggy workers". **Moiré load pattern**: two or more pipelines whose runs sometimes overlap and together consume a shared resource; in the example (Fig. 25-3) the peak that causes the on-call pain occurs when the aggregate load approaches **1.2 M** (the text on p.236 writes "1.2M" and Fig. 25-3, on p.237, is image only: **the book does not state the unit** (requests, bytes, operations per second?); do not convert nor assert "req/s"). (SRE p.235-237)
- **Google Workflow** (created in **2003**): leader-follower pattern with system prevalence; model-view-controller analogy: the model is the **Task Master** (in-memory job states, mutations written synchronously to a disk journal; preferably keeps only pointers to the work); the view is the **stateless workers** (disposable); an optional controller does scaling, snapshots, rollback, "global interdiction". Stages are groups of tasks. **Four correctness guarantees**: configuration barriers (versioned configuration tasks), a valid lease to commit, uniquely named output files, and a **server token** that validates the Task Master on every operation; tasks are unique and immutable (even changing the lease creates a new task), which would be inefficient in an ordinary database. Exactly-once. (SRE p.238-240)
- Business continuity: the journal goes to **Spanner** (globally consistent, low throughput), the writer is elected via **Chubby**; global Workflows use two or more local Workflows in distinct clusters plus **reference tasks** in the global Workflow and a **heartbeat task**: if the heartbeat stops, the helper of the other Workflow takes over the work. Summary: if the problem is continuous or will grow to be, do not use a periodic pipeline. (SRE p.240-241)

## Post-blind-test corrections (round 3): Moiré and consensus patterns/performance [from source]
- **Moiré load pattern (p.236).** It is a problem **related to thundering herd** that can go unnoticed when the latter is viewed in isolation. It occurs when two or more pipelines run at the same time and their sequences of runs **sometimes overlap**, making them consume a shared resource together. **It can occur even in continuous pipelines, although it is less common when the load arrives more uniformly.** It is most evident in usage graphs of the shared resource; in the example (Fig. 25-2 and 25-3, three stacked periodic pipelines) the peak that causes the on-call pain arises when the aggregate load gets close to 1.2 M (the text on p.236 writes "1.2M" and Fig. 25-3, on p.237, is image only: **the book does not state the unit** (requests, bytes, operations per second?); do not convert nor assert "req/s"). Therefore, claiming that Moiré is "only" a periodic pipeline problem contradicts the book. (SRE p.236)
- **Consensus usage patterns (p.206-208).** Reliable replicated datastores use consensus on the critical path (performance and scale matter); systems outside consensus tend to rely on timestamps, which are problematic because clocks are not guaranteed to be synchronized (Spanner models the worst-case uncertainty and slows processing when needed). **Leader election** is a problem equivalent to consensus: the single leader is coarse-grained mutual exclusion (used in GFS and Bigtable) and there consensus is not on the critical path, so throughput matters less. **Barriers** split a distributed computation into phases (for example, the Map phase finishes entirely before Reduce) and can be an RSM, avoiding the single point of failure of a coordinator. **Locks** should use **renewable leases with timeout**, and are a low-level primitive: most applications should use something higher level, such as distributed transactions. **Queues**: prefer a lease over permanent removal of the item; losing the queue brings the system down, and implementing it as an RSM reduces the risk; **atomic broadcast** (everyone receives the messages reliably and in the same order) is equivalent to consensus (Chandra and Toueg). Queues tolerate high latency but require throughput. (SRE p.206-208)
- **Performance (p.210-215).** The belief that consensus is too slow for high throughput and low latency "is simply not true"; Google's scale is rather a disadvantage (large data times several replicas, geographic distances that increase latency). There is no "best" algorithm for everything: performance depends on the workload (peak proposal throughput, proportion of operations that change state, read semantics, request size) and on the deployment (LAN or WAN, where the majorities are, shards, pipelining, batching). **Multi-Paxos** with a strong leader needs one round trip to the quorum; phase 1 (Prepare/Promise) establishes a "view" (leader's term) and afterwards only Accepts are sent; switching the proposer costs an extra round trip and can cause **dueling proposers** (livelock); the practical solution is to elect a proposer or rotate slots, with timeouts and **random** backoff in the election (Raft is cited as an example). **Reads**: reading without consensus from any replica relaxes consistency (Photon reads from any replica because stale data only generates extra work); strongly consistent reads require a read-only consensus operation, a read from the stable leader or **quorum leases** (read leases on a subset of the state, for a short time, to a quorum of replicas; any write to that data needs the ack of all of them and, if one becomes unavailable, the data does not change until the lease expires; useful in read-heavy workloads, in which reads of a subset of the data are concentrated in a geographic region: the sentence on regional concentration is on p.213). (SRE p.210-213)
- **Network and disk (p.213-215).** Fast Paxos replaces two steps (client→proposer and proposer→replicas) with a parallel send from the client to all acceptors, but is **not always faster** (client with high RTT and acceptors with a good connection among themselves: one round trip over a slow link usually beats a quorum, due to the tail latency effect) and gets in the way of batching. Stable leaders (Multi-Paxos, Zab, Raft) allow optimizing reads, but every write goes through the leader (latency for distant clients), the leader's outbound bandwidth is a bottleneck (the Accept carries the data) and a bad leader machine reduces the throughput of the whole system; Mencius and Egalitarian Paxos use pre-assigned rotating leadership (modulo of the transaction id). **Pipelining** allows several proposals in flight (like the TCP sliding window) and combines with batching without violating global order. **Disk**: it is necessary to write before making commitments (an acceptor does not accept a lower-numbered proposal); a write takes from one to several milliseconds; the latency of an operation adds a write at the proposer, parallel messages, parallel writes at the acceptors and the replies; there is a variant in which the proposer's write occurs in parallel with those of the others when disk dominates the time. (SRE p.213-215)
- **Leader placement and quorum (p.219-222).** Concentrating leaders in the same place creates uneven bandwidth use (Fig. 23-11) and, if they fail together, drastically changes the network pattern (Fig. 23-12). Electing leaders only on the fastest machines concentrates traffic in one datacenter (and changes a lot if it goes down), so the algorithm must balance distribution and capacity; favoring older processes correlates with location when releases are per datacenter. For a system spanning North America and Europe it is impossible to have equidistant replicas: one example is to put five replicas (two in the central US, one on the east coast, two in Europe), in which the east coast is the link between two possible quorums; losing it leaves the RTT from Europe to the center about **50% higher** than to the east coast. The **hierarchical quorum** (nine replicas in three groups of three) tolerates losing one replica of the central group. Extra replicas have a cost; in heavily sharded, read-heavy systems fewer consensus groups can be used. (SRE p.220-222)
- **Consensus monitoring (p.222-223).** Number of members in each group and health of each process (a process can be up and unable to make progress); persistently lagging replicas (recovering state, lagging, caught up, leader); **existence of a leader** (without a leader the system is totally unavailable); **number of leader changes** (view/term; a rapid rise indicates a flapping leader, e.g. due to the network; a drop in the number may indicate a serious bug); **consensus transaction number** (should grow if the system is healthy); proposals seen and proposals accepted; throughput and latency; for diagnosis, distribution of acceptance latency, network latencies between locations, time of the acceptors in durable log and bytes accepted per second. (SRE p.222-223)
- **Declared scope of the chapter (p.223-224):** it deliberately avoids discussing specific algorithms, protocols and implementations because they change fast; what matters are the fundamentals and the final message (leader election, critical shared state, distributed lock: think consensus). Note 112 (p.224) points to Kyle Kingsbury's (Jepsen) articles on incorrect behaviors of distributed datastores. Note 120 (p.241): "model-view-controller" is an analogy borrowed from Smalltalk. (SRE p.223-224, 241)
