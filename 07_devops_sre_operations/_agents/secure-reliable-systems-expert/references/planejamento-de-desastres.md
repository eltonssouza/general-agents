## Ch. 16 — Disaster planning (BSRS p.399-421)

### Premise and definition of "disaster" (BSRS p.399-400)
- [from source] Security failures and incidents are inevitable; it is recommended to conduct disaster planning near the end of the development cycle, after implementation; planning ensures you continuously improve recovery capability instead of just hoping the system survives (BSRS p.399). A disaster is rarely perceived only when it is in full swing: first "smoke" appears (BSRS p.400).
- [from source] Forms of disaster: natural; infrastructure (component failures, misconfiguration); service or product outages observable by customers; degradations near a threshold; an external attacker with prolonged access before detection; unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data; urgent security vulnerabilities (treated as an imminent attack) (BSRS p.400). In this and the next chapter "disaster" and "crisis" are synonyms for any situation that warrants declaring an incident (BSRS p.400).

### Dynamic response strategies (BSRS p.400-401)
- [from source] Flexible plans allow adaptation; incident response is not like riding a bicycle: without practice the muscle is lost, leading to fragmented responses and longer recovery; well-practiced skills let specialists function naturally in the process (BSRS p.400-401). Segment the plan into phases (immediate response, short-term recovery, long-term recovery, resumption of operations, Figure 16-1); short-term recovery should define criteria for exiting the incident (which may require fully mitigating identified security threats); preparation includes: disaster analysis, response team, plans and playbooks, system configuration, testing and incorporating feedback (BSRS p.401).

### Disaster risk analysis (BSRS p.402)
- [from source] Determine the most critical operations (including dependencies such as network and application-layer components); identify systems that, if damaged or offline, incapacitate operations (mission-essential, mission-important or nonessential), technological and human resources needed to respond, and likely scenarios per system (grouped by probability, frequency and impact: low, medium, high, critical); a formal approach (standardized probability x impact matrix, Appendix A) avoids groupthink; review outliers (an unlikely event can be critical because of its impact); ask for expert review; risk varies with location (typhoons in Japan and Taiwan, hurricanes in the southeastern US) and matures with fault tolerance (redundant internet and power circuits); large organizations assess at the global level and per site, reviewing periodically (BSRS p.402).

### Building an incident response (IR) team (BSRS p.403-407)
- [from source] Staffing models: a dedicated full-time team; IR duties added to existing work; outsourcing (BSRS p.403). Identify members and roles: small organizations may have contributors from several teams or a single team; larger ones may have teams per area (security, privacy, operational); part can be outsourced (e.g., forensics), with the risk that third parties are not immediately available (BSRS p.403). Roles (can be combined; they are roles, not individuals): incident commander (leads the response to an incident), SREs (reconfigure systems or implement fixes), public relations, customer support, legal, privacy engineers, forensic specialists (reconstruction and attribution) and security engineers; shift rotation model to reduce fatigue (BSRS p.403-404).
- [from source] Avoid single points of failure: incidents do not respect schedules; executives must empower delegates who approve emergency fixes, configuration changes and messages, including across time zones; identify staff and a senior champion in advance who commits resources and removes blockers (BSRS p.404-405).
- [from source] Team charter: mission in one sentence (types of incidents), scope (environment: technologies, users, products, stakeholders; which incidents are handled internally and which by third parties), morale (adequate workload; see the morale topic in Ch.17), leadership and champion agreement on scope (customer firewall inquiries belong to support), definition of success (when the work is done) (BSRS p.405).
- [from source] Severity and priority models (used together): severity categorizes impact (five-point scale 0-4, 0 = most serious; example: unauthorized individuals on the network = severity 0; temporary unavailability of security logs = severity 2; use previous risk analyses so as not to classify everything as critical); priority defines the speed and time of response (0 = high, immediate response before any other work; 4 = routine operational work); a shared priority model keeps teams in sync; normally severity stays fixed over the incident cycle and priority can change (for example, from 0 to 1 or 2 after a critical fix) (BSRS p.405-406).
- [from source] Operational parameters: expected initial response time (5 minutes, 30 minutes, one hour, next business day), initial triage time and a response plan with an operational schedule, SLOs that indicate when IR interrupts day-to-day work; balanced on-call rotations (refers to the SRE book/workbook chapters; hand off to sre-practices-expert) (BSRS p.406-407).

### Response plans and playbooks (BSRS p.407-409)
- [from source] Well-made plans guide responders who decide quickly with limited information; they should cover: incident reporting, triage (who responds to the initial report), SLOs, roles and responsibilities, outreach (how to reach engineering teams), communications (informing leadership, intra-organization communication with chat, videoconference, email, secure IRC and bug tracker, communication with externals such as regulators and police in partnership with legal, contact index, communication with support, and communication with responders and leadership without alerting the adversary if the communication system is unavailable or compromised) (BSRS p.407-408).
- [from source] Security and reliability risk: depending on a single email or messaging system can harm the team: the adversary who compromised the email server or the domain controller can join the distribution list, follow the response, avoid future detection and migrate to systems declared clean; if the system goes down, there is no way to contact stakeholders; include backup communication methods (BSRS p.408). The plan describes high-level procedures and references detailed playbooks (e.g., for a connectivity problem, a summary of the areas to analyze and a playbook with instructions for accessing routers and firewalls); criteria for notifying senior leadership and engaging local engineering (BSRS p.408-409).
- [from source] Playbooks: specific end-to-end instructions for tasks (granting temporary emergency administrative access, extracting and analyzing logs, performing failover and when to apply graceful degradation); they are procedural, frequently reviewed and team-specific (the response may involve several teams with their own playbooks) (BSRS p.409). Store the documentation where it is accessible during the disaster and kept current; review and update periodically; define a system to track incident information (security and privacy: need-to-know; service outages: widely accessible) (BSRS p.409).

### Prestaging systems and people (BSRS p.409-412)
- [from source] Prestaging involves configuring systems with defined log retention, automatic responses and clear human procedures, eliminating coverage gaps; define access levels and escalation procedures for emergency access in advance, with read access to logs and analysis, reporting and forensic tools (BSRS p.409-410).
- [from source] Configuring systems: fault tolerance and failovers (Chs. 8 and 9); forensic agents (GRR, EnCase Remote Agents) with logs enabled; long security log retention (industry average of ~200 days to detect intrusions; rules such as the EU's limit retention; consult legal); keep hardware and software identical to restore backups on tape or other media and run periodic restore drills; multiple access and recovery paths, including air-gapped systems known to be safe for when the extent of corporate workstation compromise is uncertain (BSRS p.410-411).
- [from source] Training: IR members trained in severity and priority models, the operating model, response times and the location of plans and playbooks; engineers who help must also know the roles (at Google, IMAG based on the Incident Command System with incident commander, operational leads and communications lead); employees trained to recognize, report and escalate incidents through clear channels; a finite time limit for an engineer to handle something alone before escalating (for example, an initial window of 15 minutes, adjustable); decision criteria established beforehand (taking a compromised system offline, containment method); train for competing priorities (uptime vs preservation of forensic artifacts) and note activities to differentiate them from the attacker's artifacts (BSRS p.411).
- [from source] Processes and procedures: rapid acquisition of hardware and software (including fuel for generators), approval processes for outsourcing contracts (e.g., forensics), policies to preserve evidence and logs and prevent overwriting (BSRS p.412).

### Testing systems and response plans (BSRS p.412-418)
- [from source] Test from three perspectives: automated systems, processes and trained people; periodically (annually, at minimum); audit controls (quarterly or annually); consider disaster recovery and business continuity certifications for inspiration (BSRS p.412-413).
- [from source] Auditing automated systems: backups (created correctly, in a secure location, for the right duration, with correct permissions; recovery exercises and validation), event logs (stored correctly for a period proportional to risk), critical patches applied on time (audit automatic and manual processes), alerts generated correctly (validate each rule and dependencies, such as SMTP offline during a network failure) and communication tools (chat, email, bridges, secure IRC) with failover and message retention for the postmortem (BSRS p.413).
- [from source] Non-intrusive tabletops: test documented procedures and team performance without taking systems down; similar to Wheel of Misfortune; participants: line engineers with playbooks, senior leadership, public relations, lawyers; characteristics: believability (credible scenario, e.g., phishing that allows exploiting a vulnerability on the workstation, realistic pivot points), details (prior research, artifacts such as logs, reports and alerts), decision points (a 60-minute tabletop has about 10 to 20 decision points; decisions affect the plot, such as taking the email server offline preventing notifications), participants and facilitators (interactive: demonstrate the response, not just discuss, to build muscle memory), outcomes (actionable feedback, concrete recommendations, action items with owners) (BSRS p.414-415).
- [from source] Response tests in production: single system testing and fault injection (e.g., malicious USB: do the local logs track the USB port and are they aggregated and escalated in time?; fault injection frameworks allow testing a system without involving dependencies; the open source Envoy proxy has an HTTP fault injection filter that returns arbitrary errors for a percentage of traffic or delays requests; authentication migration case with timeouts treated as permanent failures in the authentication library's cache, reproduced with latency injection); human resource testing (what happens if key people are unavailable); multicomponent testing (simultaneous failures; do the failovers respect ACLs? does the dependent service fail closed?); system-wide failures/failovers (failover is only trusted after operating from the secondary site; Google power-cycles entire datacenter buildings; in the cloud, consider zone or region failure) (BSRS p.415-417).
- [from source] Red Team testing: offensive exercises by Google's Information Security Assurance organization, generally without notice to responders (except senior leadership), more productive than standard pentests because they know the infrastructure, balance external and internal attack and test human behavior (phishing, social engineering); see Ch.20 (BSRS p.417).
- [from source] Evaluating responses: postmortems with action items also for tests; "running an exercise without implementing the lessons is mere entertainment"; best practices: measure the time of each stage, blameless postmortems focused on systems, procedures and processes, feedback loops, collect artifacts and feed back into signal detection, keep logs for forensics, evaluate even "failed" tests, and color teams (a hybrid Purple Team to ensure the Blue Team fixes the vulnerabilities exploited by the Red Team, as vulnerability regression) (BSRS p.418).

### Google examples (BSRS p.419-421)
- [from source] Test with global impact: a 2019 simulation of a major Bay Area earthquake (physical plant, transportation, network, utilities, communication, business operations and executive decisions); questions tested include first aid, helping the public, escalating information with cellular networks and LAN/MAN/WAN unavailable, employee conflicts of interest, roads, employees and visitors stranded, damage assessment and how SREs and engineering teams from outside the region would take control of the systems if the affected ones could not hand over responsibility (BSRS p.419).
- [from source] DiRT with emergency access: SREs tested breakglass credentials for access to corporate and production networks when the ACL services were down; the detection team confirmed that the correct alert fired and that the access was legitimate (BSRS p.420).
- [from source] Industry-wide vulnerabilities (2018): SegmentSmack (CVE-2018-5390) and FragmentSmack (CVE-2018-5391) in the Linux kernel: forged IP fragments and TCP segments caused expensive operations (a service that withstood 1 Mpps would fall over at ~50 Kpps, a 20x reduction in resilience); Google assigned a team of full-time incident managers to identify affected systems (including vendor firmware images); technically, SREs already had defense in depth with ksplice (runtime patching via function redirection tables, without reboot) and kernel rollout discipline (target of under 30 days for the whole fleet and mechanisms to speed it up); ksplice covered the two functions tcp_collapse_ofo_queue and tcp_prune_ofo_queue; since the procedure was already tested and approved, VP approval was quickly obtained to apply it during a code freeze (BSRS p.420-421).
- [from source] Conclusion: start by identifying the most important system or data and how it would respond to various disasters; determine how long it can operate without the service and how many people or systems are affected; expand gradually (BSRS p.421).
