# SRE principles and role (Preface, Ch. 1, 2, 33, 34)

All pages are PDF pages (printed = N−12). Paraphrased text.

## Thesis
- SRE is Google's name for the discipline that covers a service's entire lifecycle: conception, deployment, operation, refinement and decommissioning. [from source] (SRE p.15)
- Cited estimate: 40-90% of a system's total cost occurs after deployment; therefore software engineering "only up to birth" is the wrong view. [from source] (SRE p.15)
- Reliability is the most fundamental feature; invest in it only up to the "reliable enough" point; after that the effort goes to features. [from source] (SRE p.15)
- Author's definition: SRE is what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team. [from source] (SRE p.23)
- SRE vs DevOps: the book says the principles are consistent; DevOps can be seen as a generalization of SRE principles, and SRE as a specific implementation of DevOps with its own extensions; Preface note 4 (p.20) says SRE is *distinct from the term DevOps* by its primary focus on reliability and its orientation toward removing the need for operations; that note supports the difference in focus, not a rivalry (the compatibility is in the box on p.24). [from source] (SRE p.24; note 4, p.20)

## The sysadmin model and its costs
- Direct cost: the team grows with the load because it depends on manual intervention. Indirect cost (larger): dev and ops have different vocabulary, incentives and stability goals, and the split degenerates into friction. [from source] (SRE p.22)
- "Trench warfare" anti-pattern: ops creates launch gates and checklists; dev reacts by fragmenting changes (flag flips, cherrypicks) to escape review. [from source] (SRE p.23)

## Composition and the 50% rule
- The book describes 50-60% Software Engineers and 40-50% candidates with 85-99% of the SWE profile plus rare knowledge (UNIX internals, layer 1-3 networking). [from source] (SRE p.23) [DATED] (Google's proportions in 2016)
- Ceiling of 50% of aggregate time on "ops" work (tickets, on-call, manual tasks); below 50% on development, return the load to dev or hire without assigning extra ops. [from source] (SRE p.23-24)
- Advantages: fast innovation, cheap teams, SREs grow sublinearly with the system. Challenges: hard hiring and the need for strong management support (e.g., stopping releases for the quarter when the error budget runs out). [from source] (SRE p.24)

## Listed responsibilities
availability, latency, performance, efficiency, change management, monitoring, emergency response, capacity planning. [from source] (SRE p.24)

## Tenets (Ch. 1)
- Durable focus on engineering: monitor the operational load and redirect the excess (bugs/tickets) to development until it is back to ≤50%. [from source] (SRE p.24)
- On-call: on average at most 2 events per 8-12 h shift; more causes fatigue, fewer wastes time. [from source] (SRE p.25)
- Postmortems for all significant incidents, whether or not they paged; blame-free. [from source] (SRE p.25)
- Error budget: 100% is the wrong target for almost everything; budget = 1 − availability target; set by the product. [from source] (SRE p.25)
- Monitoring: valid outputs are alert, ticket and logging; requiring a human to read email to decide whether there is an action is "fundamentally flawed" (software interprets, humans are only notified when they need to act). [from source] (SRE p.25-26; reinforced in App. B, p.330)
- Emergency: reliability depends on MTTF and MTTR; to assess the effectiveness of emergency response, the book says **the most relevant metric is MTTR** (how quickly the system is returned to health); "humans add latency": a system that avoids emergencies with human intervention has more availability; a playbook gives about 3x improvement in MTTR according to the book; "Wheel of Misfortune" is cited as training (p.26 only cites it; the postmortem re-enactment is on p.128, the description in Ch. 28, p.280, and the Wheel of Misfortune + DiRT pair appears on p.103). [from source] (SRE p.26)
- Change: about 70% of outages come from changes to a live system; mitigate with progressive rollouts, fast detection and safe rollback, automated. [from source] (SRE p.26)
- Capacity planning: organic and inorganic forecasting, regular load tests; SRE owns capacity planning and provisioning. [from source] (SRE p.26-27)

## Reliability hierarchy (Part III)
Monitoring → Incident Response → Postmortem/Root-Cause Analysis → Testing → Capacity Planning → Development → Product, analogous to Maslow's hierarchy (Fig. III-1): from the most basic need (the system working as a service) up to the highest (taking active control of the service's direction instead of firefighting). [from source] (SRE p.85-86)

**Summary of the Part III introduction (p.85-87).**
- Context: SREs operate services (related systems, used by internal or external customers) and are responsible for their health; Part III covers the day-to-day theory and practice: monitoring, capacity planning, responding to incidents, making sure root causes are addressed. [from source] (SRE p.85)
- **Origin of the hierarchy**: the book says it had not been explicitly developed until some Google SREs, among them former colleague Mikey Dickerson (note 41), temporarily joined the US government to help with the launch of healthcare.gov, in late 2013 and early 2014; they needed a way to explain how to increase the reliability of systems. The body text calls it Figure 3-1; the caption (p.86) is Figure III-1. [from source] (SRE p.85-86)
- **Monitoring** (base): without monitoring there is no way to know whether the service works ("flying blind"); you want to know about problems before the users do. [from source] (SRE p.85)
- **Incident Response**: on-call is a tool for the larger mission and for staying in touch with how distributed systems work and fail, and Google would get rid of the pager if it could. "Making the problem go away" is not necessarily fixing it for good: you can stop the bleeding by reducing the system's precision, temporarily turning off features (graceful degradation) or diverting traffic to another healthy instance; the details depend on the service, but responding well to incidents applies to every team. Finding out what is wrong is the first step (structured troubleshooting); during the incident it is tempting to give in to adrenaline and act ad hoc, and the book advises against that; managing incidents well reduces impact and anxiety. [from source] (SRE p.85)
- **Postmortem and RCA**: the goal is to be alerted and act manually only on new problems; "fixing" the same thing repeatedly is tedious and this way of thinking sets the SRE philosophy apart from more traditional operations environments. Blameless postmortem culture is the first step to understanding what went wrong (and what went right); the outage tracker records recent incidents, causes and actions (p.87). [from source] (SRE p.85, 87)
- **Testing**: prevent what usually goes wrong (prevention is better than cure); test suites give some assurance that the software does not make certain classes of errors before going to production. [from source] (SRE p.87)
- **Capacity Planning**: the Auxon case (Ch. 18) automates capacity planning; then come load balancing (frontend and datacenter), overload handling and cascading failures. [from source] (SRE p.87)
- **Development**: Google does large-scale systems design and software engineering within the organization; distributed consensus (Paxos) is at the core of many systems, including the global Cron; also distributed cron, data pipelines and data integrity (what you read is what you wrote). [from source] (SRE p.87)
- **Product** (top): reliable launches at scale, to give users the best experience from "Day Zero". [from source] (SRE p.87)
- "Further reading" (p.87): an ACM article on company-wide resilience testing (zombie apocalypse scenario), capacity planning "without a crystal ball" and a new approach to corporate network security, the **BeyondCorp** initiative (reference [War14], R. Ward and B. Beyer, "BeyondCorp: A New Approach to Enterprise Security", ;login:, Dec. 2014): replacing privileged intranets with device and user credentials, driven by SREs at the infrastructure level; the book merely points to it as reading, without detailing the architecture (the bibliographic reference also appears on p.346). [from source] (SRE p.87, 346)
- Limit: the book assigns no values or quantitative criteria to each rung of the hierarchy; the order and the mapping by chapter are those of the introduction. [inference] about the absence.

## Numeric details from Ch. 2 and Ch. 33 [from source]
**Regional sizing of Shakespeare (Ch. 2, p.35).**
- Load test: each backend task handles ~100 QPS; expected peak of 3,470 QPS ⇒ at least 35 tasks; **N+2** ⇒ 37 (during an update one task is unavailable, leaving 36; a machine failure during the update leaves 35, "just enough" for the peak; the probability of two simultaneous failures is assumed negligible, note 13).
- Peak distribution: 1,430 QPS in North America, 290 in South America, 1,400 in Europe/Africa and 350 in Asia/Australia (sum 3,470). With N+2 per region: **17 tasks in the US, 16 in Europe and 6 in Asia**; in South America, **4 tasks instead of 5** (reduces N+2 to N+1), accepting a small risk of higher latency if GSLB sends traffic to another continent, to **save 20% of the hardware** (in the larger regions the tasks are spread over two or three clusters). Bigtable is replicated in each region (resilience and lower latency; eventual consistency acceptable because the content changes little). (SRE p.35)
- Calculation [derived]: 17 + 4 + 16 + 6 = **43 tasks** in the regional version against 37 at a single site; the cost of redundancy and regional latency is a choice, not a rule of the book. Protocol buffers: "3 to 10 times smaller and 20 to 100 times faster" than XML (SRE p.33).

**Other industries (Ch. 33) with numbers.**
- Themes: disaster preparation and testing, postmortem culture, automation and overhead reduction, structured and rational decision-making. Strategies seen outside Google: organizational focus on safety, attention to detail, **swing capacity** (telecom uses the "switch on wheels", a mobile telco office, for predictable events such as the Olympics), live simulations and drills, training and certification, detailed requirements gathering and defense in depth. (SRE p.316-319)
- Examples: nuclear Navy with real drills "weekly, two to three days a week"; lifeguards with a "mystery shopper" (staged drowning); defense: **one year of design and three weeks of code**; refinery/manufacturing: the former CEO of Alcoa required notification **within 24 hours** of any injury that cost a day of work; near misses treated as a preventive postmortem (CHIRP in the United Kingdom). (SRE p.318-320)
- Automation (SRE p.320-321): the nuclear Navy (per the interviewee) avoided automation in favor of interlocks and procedures; trading became cautious (**Knight Capital: US$ 440 million in a few hours, 2012**; Flash Crash of 2010: trillions in 30 minutes); in the UK nuclear industry the **rule of thumb is that if the plant must respond to a situation in less than 30 minutes, the response must be automated**; in aviation the operational failover is automatic, but for some tasks automation is only trusted if verified by a human; in LASIK surgery automatic sanity checks on the entered data and automatic iris matching eliminated a class of medical errors.
- Decision (SRE p.321-322): decision basis agreed in advance, clear inputs, explicit assumptions, data beats the opinion of the most senior person (**HiPPO**); trading separates an enforcement team that can shut the system down ("if we're not trading, we're not losing money") and only it turns it back on; telecom still uses long-distance switches from the 1980s ("bulletproof and massively redundant"); nuclear: "if it works now, don't touch it"; several industries rely on playbooks and procedures in which every conceivable scenario is in a checklist or in **"the binder"**, the authoritative source of how to react, which suits slowly evolving industries or those with a limited-skill workforce (SRE p.322). Chapter conclusion: Google has a greater appetite for speed and, since lives are generally not at risk, uses error budgets to "fund" innovation (SRE p.322).
- Ch. 34 (p.324-325): SRE went from a few hundred (2006) to more than 1,000 people at a dozen sites; airplane analogy: the cockpit of a 747 still has two pilots because the interfaces are well thought out; compact SRE team, at a high level of abstraction, with backup systems and deep knowledge coming from daily operations.
- **Time split and evolution of activities (Ch. 34, p.324-325).** Teams are put together so that engineers split their time between **two equally important kinds of work**: (1) on-call, which is getting hands on the systems, seeing where and how they break and understanding scale challenges; (2) having time afterwards to reflect and decide what to build to make the systems easier to manage. The author says SRE plays the role of **pilot and engineer/designer** at the same time; experience becomes code packaged as a product, reusable by other SRE teams and even outside Google (Google Cloud). The core responsibilities are consistent over time (reliable, flexible, easy to manage in an emergency, well monitored, with planned capacity), but the typical activities evolve: the example is that "building a dashboard for 20 machines" becomes "automating discovery, dashboards and alerts across a fleet of tens of thousands of machines". Analogy: a hundred years ago the airplane had one engine (two, with luck), a pilot who was also mechanic and loader, and the failure of any system was catastrophic (p.324); today a 747 with hundreds of passengers, redundant systems and a 6,000-mile flight still has only two pilots (p.325). [from source] (SRE p.324-325) [DATED] for the 2016 numbers.
- Ch. 1 (p.23): Ben Treynor Sloss joined Google in 2003 and managed a "Production Team" of seven engineers; SREs are now seen as software engineers who design operations. For the limit of 2 events per 8-12 h shift and the playbook (~3x MTTR improvement), see the sections above. (SRE p.23, 25-26)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 3): Ch. 33 (p.316-322), "launch and iterate" and the preface note [from source]
- **Contrast with "launch and iterate" (p.319).** In detailed requirements gathering and design, the former defense contractor described a culture in which creating a new defense system usually meant **an entire year of design, followed by only three weeks writing the code**; the book says this is **very different from Google's "launch and iterate" culture**, which promotes a much faster pace of change with calculated risk, and that other industries (medicine, military) have **very different pressures, risk appetites and requirements**, which inform their processes. It is not "more reliable/recommended": it is fit to context. In the laser surgery machine example, requirements are gathered from surgeons and technicians because the designer neither operates nor maintains the equipment. (SRE p.319)
- **CAPA (p.319):** *corrective and preventative action* corresponds to blameless postmortem culture; see `postmortem-e-rastreamento-de-outages.md`.
- **Preparation strategies (p.317-319):** organizational focus on safety (at the synthetic diamond factory every management meeting began with safety; very detailed software standards and safety integrity levels SIL 1-4 in nuclear, military aircraft and railway signaling), attention to detail (submarine: a lubricating oil maintenance failure can become a major failure), **swing capacity** (telecom: "switch on wheels"; unpredictable event such as the 2005 leak of a celebrity's phone that generated DDoS symptoms), simulations and drills (aviation uses simulators with real feeds because it cannot test "in production"; telecom runs hurricane drills; the nuclear Navy runs drills "weekly, two to three days a week", breaking real things with control parameters; lifeguards use a "mystery shopper": staged drowning), **training and certification** (lifeguards: rigorous certification and recertification, site-specific training), requirements gathering and **defense in depth** (nuclear: redundancy in all systems and a final physical barrier). DiRT: Google forces real outages to make sure the systems react as thought, find weaknesses and avoid uncontrolled outages. (SRE p.317-319)
- **Regulation and near misses (p.320):** agencies cited: FCC, FAA, OSHA, FDA and competent EU authorities. At Alcoa, the CEO required being notified **within 24 hours** of any injury that cost a day of work and gave his personal phone number to the workers. *Near misses* are scrutinized as a "preventive postmortem" ("latent error + enabling condition"); the British **CHIRP** program collects confidential reports in aviation and maritime and publishes analyses in bulletins; lifeguards write a report after any incident (if feet enter the water, there will be paperwork) and, in traumatic cases, call a counselor. (SRE p.320)
- **Automation and decision (p.320-322):** nuclear Navy with a human decision chain (to operate a valve, an operator, a supervisor and a crew member on the phone with the engineering officer); trading more cautious (Knight Capital, Flash Crash); manufacturing in favor of automation for efficiency and repeatability; British nuclear **30-minute** rule; LASIK (data sanity check and iris matching eliminated a class of errors). Structured decision: basis agreed in advance, clear inputs, explicit assumptions, data beats the opinion of the most senior (HiPPO); other industries "if it ain't broke, don't fix it", follow "the binder" (checklists for every imaginable scenario, suited to slow industries or those with limited-skill labor), controlled experimentation (research and manufacturing) or separation of roles (trading: an *enforcement* team separate from the traders shuts the system down in the face of abnormalities and only it turns it back on). Conclusion: Google has a greater appetite for speed and uses error budgets to "fund" innovation because, in general, no lives are at risk. (SRE p.320-322)
- **Preface note 4 (p.20), what it actually says:** SRE says it is *distinct from the industry term DevOps*, because, although it treats infrastructure as code, it has **reliability as its primary focus** and is strongly oriented toward removing the need for operations. The note **does not assert rivalry**; the statement that the principles are consistent (DevOps as a generalization of SRE, SRE as a specific implementation of DevOps) is in the "DevOps or SRE?" box on **p.24**. For "it is not rivalry" cite p.24; the note on p.20 only supports the difference in focus. (SRE p.20, 24)

## Corrections after the blind test (round 6): Ch. 2 in detail (p.28-35), development environment and notes 9-13 [from source] [DATED]
- **Terminology and topology (p.28).** A *machine* is a piece of hardware (or a VM); a *server* is software that implements a service; any machine can run any server, and allocation is done by the cluster operating system, Borg. Dozens of machines form a rack; racks sit in rows; one or more rows form a cluster; a datacenter building normally houses several clusters; nearby buildings form a campus. Inside the datacenter, the Clos network called **Jupiter** connects hundreds of switches and supports up to **1.3 Pbps** of bisection bandwidth between servers in the largest configuration; between datacenters, the global backbone **B4** is a software-defined network (OpenFlow) with massive bandwidth for a modest number of sites and elastic bandwidth allocation. (SRE p.28)
- **Hardware failures and Borg (p.29-30).** In a cluster, in a typical year, thousands of machines and thousands of disks fail; the software abstracts this from users. Borg is a distributed cluster operating system, similar to Apache Mesos, that runs jobs (permanent servers or batch such as MapReduce) made up of one or thousands of identical tasks; if a task misbehaves, it is killed and restarted, possibly on another machine. Because tasks move, **BNS** (Borg Naming Service) is used, with paths such as `/bns/<cluster>/<user>/<job>/<task>` that resolve to IP:port. Borg bin-packs tasks according to the requested resources and considering **failure domains** (it does not put all of a job's tasks in the same rack, so the rack's top-of-rack switch is not a single point of failure) and kills a task that uses more resources than it requested (a slow crashloop is preferable to a task that was never restarted). (SRE p.29-30)
- **Storage (p.30).** Layers: **D** (fileserver on almost every machine, disk and flash), **Colossus** (cluster filesystem with replication and encryption, successor to GFS) and, on top of it, **Bigtable** (petabyte NoSQL, sparse sorted map indexed by row, column and timestamp, with eventually consistent replication across datacenters), **Spanner** (SQL-like interface for those who need real consistency worldwide) and others such as Blobstore, each with its trade-offs (see data integrity). (SRE p.30)
- **Network, GSLB, Chubby and Borgmon (p.32).** OpenFlow SDN network with simple switches and a duplicated central controller that precomputes the best paths; Bandwidth Enforcer (BwE) manages bandwidth. **GSLB** balances at three levels: geographic for DNS requests, at the user service level (YouTube, Maps) and at the RPC level; the service owner provides a symbolic name, the list of BNS addresses and the capacity at each location (in QPS). **Chubby** is the lock service with a filesystem-like API, uses Paxos, helps elect the master among replicas and stores the BNS mapping. **Borgmon** collects ("scrapes") metrics for alerts, behavior comparison and capacity planning. (SRE p.32)
- **Software infrastructure and RPC (p.32-33).** The code is heavily multithreaded; every server has an HTTP server with diagnostics and statistics. All services communicate through an RPC infrastructure called **Stubby**, whose open source version is **gRPC** (note 11); RPC is often used even for local calls, which makes it easier to refactor to another server. Data travels in **protocol buffers**, "similar to Apache's Thrift", and the book says that, compared with **XML**, they are simpler to use, **3 to 10 times smaller, 20 to 100 times faster** and less ambiguous (the comparison is with XML, not with JSON). (SRE p.32-33)
- **Development environment (p.33).** Except for some groups with their own open source repositories (Android and Chrome), Google engineers work from **a single shared repository** [Pot16]. Practical consequences: whoever finds a problem in a component outside their project can fix it, send the *changelist* (CL) to the owner for **review** and submit it to mainline; **changes to the project's own code require review** and **all software is reviewed before being submitted**. Build requests go to build servers in the datacenter, which compile in parallel; the same infrastructure runs continuous tests: for each submitted CL the tests of everything that depends on it, directly or indirectly, run, and the owner is notified if the change probably broke something; some projects use push-on-green. (SRE p.33)
- **Shakespeare example and life of a request (p.33-35).** Batch component (MapReduce in three phases map, shuffle and reduce, writing the index to a Bigtable) plus an always-on frontend; the user resolves the DNS, GSLB chooses the IP of the Google Frontend (GFE, reverse proxy that terminates TCP), the GFE finds the Shakespeare frontend via GSLB and sends the HTTP via RPC, the frontend calls the backend (BNS address via GSLB), which queries Bigtable; the chain takes a few hundred milliseconds, and a faulty GSLB would cause damage. The book attributes reliability to rigorous testing policies, careful rollout and proactive error recovery, such as graceful degradation. (SRE p.33-34)
- **Sizing (p.35).** Load test: a backend handles about 100 QPS; the predicted peak is 3,470 QPS, so at least 35 tasks, and N+2 gives 37; per region, 17 in the US, 16 in Europe, 6 in Asia and 4 (N+1, not 5) in South America, saving 20% of that region's hardware at the cost of a small latency risk (details in the Ch. 2 numbers section). (SRE p.35)
- **Notes 9 to 13 (p.35).** Note 9: datacenter hardware is homogeneous "more or less", since some datacenters accumulate generations of hardware and are expanded after they are ready. Note 11: gRPC is at grpc.io. Note 12: protocol buffers are an extensible, language- and platform-neutral mechanism for serializing structured data (note 40 of Ch. 9, p.84, repeats this definition; see `toil-automacao-e-simplicidade.md`). Note 13: the probability of two simultaneous task failures is assumed to be low enough to be negligible, but single points of failure, such as top-of-rack switches or power distribution, can invalidate this assumption in other environments. (SRE p.35)
- The book does not adopt Thrift: it only compares protocol buffers to it (p.33); the RPC infrastructure described is Stubby, with gRPC as the open source version (p.32-33).
- The book does not develop Kubernetes: note 10 (p.35) presents it as a descendant of Borg, an open source container orchestration framework started by Google in 2014 (kubernetes.io and [Bur16]), and refers to [Ver15] for the similarities between Borg and Apache Mesos; the bibliography cites it again (p.342). Nothing beyond that.

## What the book does NOT say
- That SRE "is" DevOps; a fixed number of SREs per service (it only gives on-call team minimums: 8 people, or 6 per site at two sites, SRE p.331); promotion rules.
