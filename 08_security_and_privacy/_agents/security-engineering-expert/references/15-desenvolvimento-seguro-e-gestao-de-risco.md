## 1. Premises
- `[from source]` There is no secret ("no silver bullet", Brooks); secure development management is fox knowledge, not hedgehog knowledge; security and safety are emergent properties built in from the start (SE3 p.997-998). Two questions: "are we building the right system?" and "are we building it right?" (SE3 p.998).

## 2. Risk management (SE3 p.998-1001)
- **Risk register:** list what can go wrong, score 1-5 for severity and 1-5 for probability, multiply (1 to 25), describe mitigation, rank and give each risk a senior owner (book example: 5×4=20 reduced to 16 by a hiring rule) (SE3 p.999). UK national register: pandemic = severity 5 × probability 4 (SE3 p.999). Failures: the resilience budget went to terrorism (SE3 p.998-999).
- **ALE (annual loss expectancy)** = expected loss × expected number of incidents per year; book table: SWIFT fraud US$50,000,000 × 0.005 = US$250,000; ATM fraud (small) US$20,000 × 0.5 = US$10,000; teller takes cash US$3,240 × 200 = US$648,000; the row "ATM fraud (large) US$250,000 × 0.2" appears as US$100,000 in the extracted text, although the product is US$50,000 (inconsistency in the printed page; `se3_calc.py ale` recomputes and warns) (SE3 p.1000). Data are accurate for common losses and "largely guesswork" for rare ones; consultancies adjust the total to justify the budget; understand what is data, guesswork and office politics (SE3 p.1000).
- Insurance: market cycle; insurers cannot assess cyber risk and correlated risks are excluded or expensive; insurance serves to protect executives (industry standard: "follow the herd"); security fads: hackers → viruses → firewall → PKI → blockchain (SE3 p.1000-1001).

## 3. Lessons from safety (SE3 p.1001-1009)
- **Safety method:** identify hazards and risks; strategy (avoid, restrict, redundancy); trace to critical components and operator procedures; functional and integrity requirements; test plan → *safety case* (ISO 61508, ISO 26262) (SE3 p.1002).
- **Hazard elimination** (motor circuit; SWIFT; minimize the TCB; contact-tracing app in an epidemic with the history kept on the phone) (SE3 p.1003). **Fault trees / threat trees** (top-down, DoD) and **FMEA** (bottom-up, NASA) (SE3 p.1003-1005); Challenger (SE3 p.1005).
- **Threat modelling (Microsoft):** "meet in the middle" approach: list the system's assets and the assets available to the attacker; trace attack paths between modules, trust levels and barriers; techniques: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege; use everything from architecture reviews to pentests (SE3 p.1006). Matrix of hazards × mechanisms (≥ two independent mechanisms per serious hazard); STPA (SE3 p.1006-1007).
- **Quantifying human risk:** a simple, frequent task with strong cues ≈ 1 error in 100,000; "it is when the red lights really come on that the worst errors are made"; default to a safe state (not always measurable); simplify what the operator sees; the worst security slips occur in important, rarely performed tasks; *fault masking* in redundancy; 737 Max (SE3 p.1007-1009). Safety deals with random failure; security assumes an adversary who forces the "one in a billion" combination ("programming Satan's computer") (SE3 p.1009).

## 4. Prioritizing protection goals (SE3 p.1010-1011)
Think about risk **and** reward (book example: turnover US$10m, profit US$1m, theft US$150,000; doubling turnover even while tripling the losses raises profit by 85% vs 15% from "ending theft"); fraud management subordinate to sales; SRE error budget (availability beyond what users perceive is waste); "don't trust anyone who only talks about 'tightening security'" (SE3 p.1010-1011). The value of life in safety varies from US$50,000 to >US$500m depending on the sector; with a hostile threat a risk of 1 in 10,000,000 becomes frightening (SE3 p.1011).

## 5. Methodology (SE3 p.1012-1026)
- Waterfall/V-model works when requirements are known; 70-80% of TCO comes after the product enters service — "maintenance is the product" (SE3 p.1013-1015). Iterative/spiral/agile: "solve your worst problem, repeat"; automated regression (IBM estimated 15% of fixes introducing bugs); Microsoft abolished the analyst/programmer/tester separation (SE3 p.1015-1017).
- **SDL (Microsoft):** pre-SDL training; requirements (risk assessment, bug bars, review ≤ 6 months), design (threat modeling, attack surface), implementation (approved tools, banning insecure functions, static analysis), verification (dynamic, fuzzing), release (incident response plan, final review); external SME and *security champion*; maturity model (SE3 p.1017-1019).
- **Gated development:** automated tests are not enough for security; patch Tuesday; when fixing a vulnerability, update the toolchain to find all similar ones; ~70% of apps have vulnerabilities in open-source libraries; cars and lab cars (SE3 p.1019-1020).
- **SaaS/CI-CD/DevOps:** staging, canary (~1% of traffic), *failure domains*, A/B, infrastructure as code; SRE with error budget (0.1% or 0.01%) and chaos monkey; **technical debt** — "DevOps tries to run without debt" (SE3 p.1020-1023).
- **DevSecOps:** Azure world (Big Four dictate; dozens of tools; "shift left"; pre-commit static analysis; continuous monitoring; bug bounty); Google world (reduce human touch in production; tool proxies; break-glass; microservices with limited blast radius; fail open vs fail secure; load shedding; single front end; Tink; BeyondProd) (SE3 p.1023-1026). Metrics: open security tickets, builds failing for security, time to compliance; "a badly managed firm manages to the metrics" (SE3 p.1026).

## 6. Vulnerability life cycle and incident response (SE3 p.1027-1032)
- Markets and bug bounties (Apple US$1m; ≥ 6 hackers with >US$1m on HackerOne in 2019); zero day; divergent interests among vendor, customer, researcher, agencies, security firms and large companies (SE3 p.1027-1028). **Responsible → coordinated disclosure** (VW sued academics; Shellshock/Heartbleed; Enox toy watch) (SE3 p.1028-1031). CVE, CVSS, NVD (SE3 p.1029).
- **Incident response plan in four components:** **monitoring** (threat intel, honeypots, reporting channels), **repair** (orchestrate the response; notify vendors and customers; alternative communication), **distribution** (fast patching; "in an emergency run the normal patch process as fast as possible"), **reassurance** (educate the CEO/board beforehand; press releases ready per severity; notification laws; "information security is a CEO issue") (SE3 p.1031-1032).

## 7. Organization and team (SE3 p.1032-1043)
- Bezos's law (two pizzas) (SE3 p.1032-1033); communication: log N vs N² vs 2^N; design failures come from misunderstood requirements (Curtis-Krasner-Iscoe; London Ambulance; Y2K as a counterexample) (SE3 p.1033-1034). Uncertainty: attack the hard problem first; checklists give false security (ISO 27001 in almost every firm with a large breach); CISO tenure ~2 years; moral hazard when pushing risk to third parties; decisions by people who will not be held accountable; "good enough" consulting; several specialists find more hazards (SE3 p.1034-1036).
- Team: technical career; diversity (≥ 3 women to change the culture); scrum; **declare errors openly** (air traffic controllers); agreed code style; "everyone responsible for their own code *and* specialists"; tools (Rust, static analysis from the start, GCM instead of ECB); secure evolution of APIs (SE3 p.1036-1042).
