## Ch. 13: linear factor models

## Ch. 14: autoencoders
- `[from source]` (DLB p.518) Autoencoder = encoder f (x → h) + decoder g (h → r); the goal is not copying, but useful properties of h. ***Undercomplete*** (dim h < dim x): loss L(x, g(f(x))) (14.1); with a linear decoder and MSE it learns the same subspace as PCA; with nonlinear f and g it is a nonlinear generalization of PCA. With excessive capacity it only copies (extreme example: an integer code i per example).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.519-520) **Regularized** (code of dimension equal to or larger than the input, *overcomplete*): they use a loss that encourages sparsity, small derivative and robustness to noise or to missing inputs; models with latent variables and inference (Helmholtz machine, VAE, GSN) can be seen as autoencoders.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.520-522) ***Sparse autoencoder***: L(x, g(f(x))) + Ω(h) (14.2); the penalty depends on the data, so it is not a formal *prior*; it is equivalent to approximate MLE of a model with latents; the Laplace *prior* p(hᵢ) = (λ/2)e^(−λ|hᵢ|) corresponds to Ω(h) = λΣ|hᵢ| (14.5-14.7); ReLU in the code layer gives true zeros (Glorot 2011b).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.522-523) ***Denoising autoencoder (DAE)***: minimizes L(x, g(f(x̃))) with x̃ a corrupted copy of x (14.9) and thus learns the structure of p_data (Alain and Bengio 2013). **Penalizing derivatives**: Ω(h,x) = λΣᵢ‖∇ₓhᵢ‖² (14.11) defines the *contractive autoencoder (CAE)*.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.523-524) Depth helps (universal approximation only guarantees a shallow encoder that does not impose arbitrary constraints on the code); deep autoencoders compress better (Hinton and Salakhutdinov 2006); it is common to pretrain with a stack of shallow autoencoders; stochastic encoders/decoders: −log p_decoder(x|h) (MSE if Gaussian, Bernoulli with sigmoid, softmax for discrete) (p.524).

## Ch. 15: representation learning
- `[from source]` (DLB p.543-544) **Unsupervised greedy layer-wise pretraining** (RBM, autoencoder, *sparse coding*) is what made it possible, in 2006, to train deep fully connected networks; "today we know it is **not necessary**" (p.543); it is "greedy" because it optimizes one layer at a time, keeping the previous ones fixed, and "pretraining" because it is followed by joint fine-tuning; it can be seen as a regularizer and as initialization; it also initializes DBNs and DBMs.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.545-546) Algorithm 15.1: f ← identity; X̃ ← X; for k = 1..m: f⁽ᵏ⁾ = L(X̃); f ← f⁽ᵏ⁾∘f; X̃ ← f⁽ᵏ⁾(X̃); optionally supervised fine-tuning. In many tasks pretraining does not help or **hurts** (in chemistry, on average slightly harmful, Ma 2015); today simultaneous training is preferred (*ladder network*, discriminative RBM) or freezing the extractor and training a classifier on top; local minima are no longer seen as a serious problem (p.546).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.551) **Supervised pretraining** as popular transfer learning with CNNs trained on ImageNet (Oquab 2014; Yosinski 2014), with published parameters, such as word vectors (Collobert 2011a; Mikolov 2013a) `[DATED]`. *Transfer learning/domain adaptation*: what is learned on P₁ helps on P₂ when many factors are shared (example cats/dogs → ants/wasps).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.561-563) **Distributed representation**: n *features* with k values describe kⁿ concepts (n binary → 2ⁿ configurations), against the symbolic/*one-hot* one (only n configurations); Fig. 15.7: n features divide ℝᵈ into O(nᵈ) regions, whereas nearest neighbor with n examples distinguishes n regions; examples of **non-distributed** representations: clustering/k-means, kNN, trees, Gaussian mixtures and mixtures of experts, local kernels, n-grams (p.562-563); generalization comes from shared attributes (cat and dog: "has fur", "number of legs"); the VC dimension of a *linear-threshold* network is O(w log w) (p.562).
