## 0. Ch. 1: "Background" (Stonebraker): XML, JSON, MapReduce, data lakes and DBMS architecture [from source] (REDBK p.1-2)
- **Readings and thesis** (REDBK p.1): the chapter introduces "What Goes Around Comes Around" and "Architecture of a Database System"; the thesis is that nobody learns from history (data models reinvented) and that the architecture of the second paper is still valid, but the details have changed a lot.
- **XML** (REDBK p.1): it was the "next big thing" ten years earlier and became a niche; the author attributes the defeat to excessive complexity, complex extensions of relational engines with poor performance, and the lack of a widely accepted use case; "relational won, XML lost" (the paper's prediction that someone would win the Turing award for simplifying XML was wrong).
- **JSON: three readings** (REDBK p.1): (1) general-purpose hierarchical format, which the author considers a **disaster in the making** (whoever thinks it is a good idea should read the section on IMS); (2) representation of **sparse data** (e.g., employees' hobbies), where relational modeling gives very wide, sparse tables, a disaster for on-disk row stores but acceptable in column stores, and where JSON is a reasonable encoding of a column; (3) a "schema on read" mechanism, which would be just a projection over JSON data. Conclusion: JSON is a good choice for sparse data and the author expects relational DBMSs to absorb it as one data type among many (REDBK p.1).
- **MapReduce, Impala and data lakes** (REDBK p.1-2): MapReduce was built by Google for the web crawl database; a few years later Google stopped using it for that application and moved to Big Table; the MapReduce market became an HDFS market and is moving toward SQL (Cloudera launched **Impala**, a SQL engine over HDFS without MapReduce). The **data lake** is described as a "junk drawer" of ingested files while the company decides which are worth curating (REDBK p.1-2).
- **DBMS architecture and legacy systems** (REDBK p.2): the architecture (parser/optimizer/executor, thread and process model) is still valid, but the legacy systems (Oracle, DB2) are described as "historical artifacts"; in data warehousing **column stores replaced row stores by being 1 to 2 orders of magnitude faster**; in OLTP, in-memory SQL engines with lightweight transaction management are becoming the norm (Red Book ch. 4); SQL Server 14 would be at least two engines (in-memory Hekaton and the legacy row store) under a common parser, the "elephants'" strategy in the face of the *innovator's dilemma*; IMS data remains in production, and NVRAM may open new architectural opportunities. `[DATED]` (2015).

## 1. Ch. 11: "Complex Analytics" (Stonebraker), the thesis [from source] (REDBK p.5-7)
- **Thesis and context** (REDBK p.5): in the last 5 to 10 years, analytic workloads more complex than typical business intelligence (BI) have emerged; the author says that many assume data science will replace BI in a decade or two, and therefore the chapter deals with the needs of the data scientist. The example is an advertiser's statistical question (how women buying an Apple computer over four different days differ from those buying a Ford pickup) and which ad to show.
- **The data scientist's cycle** (REDBK p.5): after cleaning the data (most of the time, covered in the integration chapter), they iterate "data management operations" and "analytic operations" until tired. What distinguishes data science from BI is that the analysis is **predictive modeling, machine learning, regressions**, and not analytic SQL.
- **Pipelines and incrementality** (REDBK p.5): analytics is a **pipeline** of computations (example: Tamr's entity consolidation module, with about six steps, avoids the N-squared cost through "feature" bands and "bins", and part is SQL, part is analytics over arrays). Most production applications are **incremental**: reprocessing everything on each delta wastes resources, so incremental algorithms must save intermediate state to persistent storage at each iteration; since saving state is data management, the effective analytic pipeline becomes **length one**. The "real-time" solution is to run incremental analytics continuously on a streaming platform.
- **Arrays, graphs, dense and sparse** (REDBK p.5): most complex analytics is **array-oriented** (linear algebra); some is graph-oriented. Arrays can be simulated in table systems, and graphs in tables or arrays. Dense problems (closing price of all NYSE stocks per day) differ from **sparse** ones (matrix of relationships among people); the analytics on each is quite different. For "small data" any solution will do.
- **The sad truth: files** (REDBK p.6): most data science platforms are **file-based** and have nothing to do with a DBMS (R, MatLab, SPSS, SAS code; many Spark users read files). The example is the NERSC system of the Berkeley lab, where it was not possible to run the Vertica DBMS due to configuration restrictions; they also cite the 1000 Genomes Project in SciDB as a positive sign. The author lists three drawbacks of files: metadata (calibration, time) missing or embedded in the name; lack of sophisticated data management; difficulty of sharing. **From here on, assume that data scientists will want DBMS technology.**
- **Classification of platforms, Table 1** (REDBK p.6-7): two dimensions. First, the representation: table/record (row, NoSQL or column) versus **array**, which is the natural structure of analytics (no conversion, multidimensional). Second, the coupling: **loose** (runs the query and copies the result to another address space, with a lot of data movement) versus **tight** (analytics as user-defined functions in the same address space as the DBMS). Four cells: array + tight = SciDB, TileDB, Rasdaman (array systems with embedded analytics, upper right corner; the array + loose cell is empty in the table); table + loose = Spark with HBase (Map-Reduce was the old exemplar; Spark has no persistence of its own and depends on RedShift, HBase etc.); table + tight = MADlib (Greenplum's UDF library) and Vertica with R.
- **Seven performance considerations** (REDBK p.6-7): (1) performance should improve from the lower left corner to the upper right; (2) most of it reduces to a few inner loops (matrix multiplication, SVD, QR decomposition), floating-point intensive, where hardware optimization gives almost **an order of magnitude** (BLAS, LAPACK, ScaLAPACK libraries over Intel MKL), important in dense arrays and less so in sparse ones; (3) **approximate** answers are much faster than exact ones; (4) high-performance computing (HPC) hardware is configured for large batch jobs, with a compute server connected to a storage server over the network, which **conflicts with a DBMS that expects to run continuously as a service**; (5) scalable codes run on several nodes and are usually network-bound, and TCP-IP may not be a good choice (MPI is a faster alternative); (6) most analysis codes tested **do not scale** to large data (out of memory or large temporaries), so test at production volume; (7) the structure of the pipeline is crucial: with a length-one pipeline, tight coupling is almost always good, and with length 10 loose coupling yields almost the same; in incremental operation, length one is expected.
- **Future research** (REDBK p.7): the author says the area is in the "wild west" phase and that the next edition should have seminal papers; asks for more end-to-end **benchmarks** (data management plus analytics); points to custom parallel algorithms that do not follow traditional dataflow (Hogwild!, which allows lock-free parallelism in shared memory; Google Downpour and Project Adam adapt the idea to distributed deep networks), **out-of-memory** algorithms (Spark requires the structures to fit in the combined memory of the cluster, which the author calls fragile), graph analytics (dedicated systems such as GraphX and GraphLab versus simulating them in arrays or tables) and the difference between the libraries' MPI and the DBMSs' TCP-IP, plus ScaLAPACK's **block-cyclic** partitioning, which no DBMS known to the author supports.

## 2. Ch. 11: Hellerstein's commentary, the opposing view [from source] (REDBK p.7-8)
- **Date of the note** (REDBK p.7): commentary dated December 6, 2015, with a "big tent" approach: database people have a lot to contribute, but will do better if they play well with others.
- **BI will not be replaced** (REDBK p.7-8): contrary to what Stonebraker assumes, advanced analytics **does not replace BI**, which is healthy and growing; a chart is often worth more than a complex statistical model (John Tukey, "respect the plot").
- **SAS and who uses it** (REDBK p.8): the advanced analytics and data science market is growing, but, unlike BI, **database technology currently plays no significant role** in it. The incumbent is **SAS**, a company with revenue of **several billion dollars a year** (the text gives no exact figure) and "decidedly not a database company"; users of SAS and of open alternatives such as R **are not database users**. Whoever assumes, like Stonebraker, that data scientists will want a monolithic "analytic DBMS" is "swimming against the tide".
- **Three threats to SAS** (REDBK p.8): (1) open-source statistical programming (R and the Python ecosystem: NumPy, SciKit-Learn, iPython Notebook), which scales poorly today but is being improved; (2) **tight coupling to big data platforms** ("DataFrame" interfaces for Spark/MLlib, PivotalR/MADlib, Vertica dplyr; the community is strongly inclined to open source, and the cloud is also a terrain where SAS has no advantage); (3) **analytic services** (recommendation, real-time fraud detection, predictive maintenance), with aggressive latency, scale, fault tolerance and evolution requirements; today done with custom code, and the opportunity is to commoditize the technology for most cases, still early.
- **Database research** (REDBK p.8): think outside the box and collaborate. Possibilities: new approaches to scalability (parallel dataflow like MADlib, MLlib or Ordonez's work at Teradata goes a long way; Hogwild! generated excitement by bringing databases and machine learning together); distributed infrastructure for analytic services (parameter servers solve only part; serving and evolving models online); and the **lifecycle and metadata** of analytics (here he agrees with Stonebraker): workflow management, data lineage, maintenance of materialized views; VisTrails is cited as an example used in practice.
- **Note on numbers** (REDBK p.8): the chapter text **does not** give an exact value for SAS revenue (only "multiple billions"); any specific figure (such as 3.2 billion) comes from outside the Red Book and must be labeled `[extrapolation]`.

## 4. Ch. 12: data integration (Stonebraker) [from source] (REDBK p.10-12)
- **History** (REDBK p.10): the topic was born with the large retailers of the 1990s consolidating sales data into a *data warehouse* (extract from the store systems, transform to a predefined global schema, load); a typical retail warehouse paid for itself in about a year. The ETL industry (*extract, transform, load*) emerged. The methodology: (a) up-front global schema; (b) one programmer per source to extract; (c) transformations and cleaning; (d) load script.
- **Three problems of ETL, scale of a dozen sources** (REDBK p.10): (1) an up-front global schema is very hard (one corporate schema took two years and was born outdated); (2) a lot of manual work; (3) integration and cleaning are hard (a typical 1990s project overran budget and schedule by a factor of two; rule of thumb: about **10% of the data is wrong**). A large company has about **5,000 operational databases** and only a few go into the warehouse; the brewery example shows that external data (weather) was not in the warehouse.
- **MDM (master data management)** (REDBK p.10-11): in the 2000s, standardizing key entities and curating a master set ("golden records"); the old ETL vendors started selling MDM. The author considers MDM **overhyped** based on two vignettes: Informix with operations in 58 countries and no way to count employees (business agility beats the standard) and a manufacturer with about 300 purchasing systems from acquisitions ("acquisitions wreck MDM").
- **Five steps of curation** (REDBK p.11): ingestion (parsing, expensive connectors), transformation (euros to dollars, airport code to city), cleaning (wrong data), schema integration ("my salary is your wages") and consolidation/deduplication (Mike Stonebraker and M.R. Stonebraker become one record). ETL and MDM do this at high cost and with little scalability: it is the unmet need, "the 800-pound gorilla in the corner".
- **Challenges per step** (REDBK p.11-12): semi-automatic connectors; transformations (Data Wrangler as the state of the art); cleaning with functional dependencies, outlier detection, crowdsourcing; schema matching (20+ years of work); entity consolidation is a clustering problem in a space of about 25 dimensions, **N squared**, so only approximate algorithms will do. The real problem is the **end-to-end system** (Data Tamer for enterprise scale; Data Wrangler for department level); research should use real data, not faults injected into clean data.
- **Hellerstein's commentary** (REDBK p.12): data transformation is increasingly a **user-centered** and context-dependent task; the "golden master" makes no sense for many analytic uses; tools should serve those who understand the data (closer to the business than to IT), with graphical and interactive interfaces; people say they spend **50 to 80% of their time** preparing data; he sees an opportunity in instant profiles and transformations suggested in real time.

## 5. Ch. 10: Web data (Bailis) [from source] (REDBK p.3-4)
- **Context** (REDBK p.3): services with billions of users (Google, Facebook) bring scale, heterogeneity and new modes of interaction; **the classic relational DBMS design did not anticipate this load and is not the technology of choice**: Web data management requires a mix of information retrieval, database internals, integration and distributed systems. Readings: Brin and Page (Google prototype), Brewer (search engine retrospective) and WebTables.
- **Search engines** (REDBK p.3): the first indexes (Yahoo!) were human-curated directories and did not scale; the design of a search engine is conceptually simple: crawlers download copies, build and maintain **read-only indexes** used for a relevance function, and a front end serves queries ranked by that score. The implementation is complex; the scoring is highly tuned and treated as an industrial secret (authors try to manipulate it); PageRank measures each page's "influence" through the link graph; anchor text and metadata come into play in practice. Keyword indexing dates back to the 1950s, and TFxIDF and inverted indexes to the 1960s; the systems innovation was scaling and handling dirty data. Brewer highlights fault tolerance and availability (*harvest* and *yield*, and the CAP theorem, ch. 7), and that Informix was about **10 times slower** than Inktomi's own solution, but that database design principles (top-down design, data independence, declarative engine) hold if adapted.
- **WebTables** (REDBK p.4): queries and understands relations in HTML tables (no fixed schema); only about 1% of the tables in the corpus were actual relations; builds a database of attribute correlation statistics (AcsDB) for metadata and schema auto-completion; the paper studies **154 million** relations, five orders of magnitude above the largest previous corpus. The argument of the "unreasonable effectiveness of data" (Halevy, Norvig, Pereira, 2009): with enough data, simple techniques beat sophisticated statistical models.

## 6. Ch. 5: large-scale dataflow engines (Bailis, with commentary by Stonebraker) [from source] (REDBK p.23-26)
- **Context** (REDBK p.23): cheap storage and growing volumes led Internet service providers to discard DBMSs and data warehouses and build their own engines (Google's series of publications: GFS, MapReduce, Chubby, BigTable). These systems implemented only a small subset of the features of conventional databases (high-level languages, optimizers, efficient strategies), but the open Hadoop ecosystem became popular and today resembles a traditional data warehouse, with modifications.
- **MapReduce** (REDBK p.23): library to simplify parallel and distributed computation at Google scale, especially the batch rebuilding of search indexes; it offers a low-level interface (two-stage dataflow) tied to a fault-tolerance strategy (intermediate materialization between the stages) and delegates storage to the Google File System. The database community called it simplistic and inefficient. In 2006 Yahoo! open-sourced Hadoop, followed by Dryad, Hive and Pig; the "post-MapReduce" systems came to expose SQL, general graph operator plans and use of indexes (Spark, F1, Impala, Tez, Naiad, Flink, AsterixDB, Drill).
- **DryadLINQ** (REDBK p.23-24): embedded language bindings that integrate with .NET's LINQ and run queries over Dryad (arbitrary dataflow graphs, fault tolerance by re-execution); it restricts the programmer to side-effect-free transformations, but offers a much higher-level interface than MapReduce, and influenced Spark and Naiad.
- **Three lasting impacts** (REDBK p.24): (1) **schema flexibility**: traditional warehouses are "walled gardens" (clean, structured data at load time), whereas MapReduce systems process data of arbitrary structure, **with no load step**, storing data before deciding what to do with it (ETL becomes a large workload; curating "data lakes" costs much more than storing, the topic of ch. 12; heterogeneity "is not free"); (2) **interface flexibility**: almost all use SQL-like languages; (3) **architecture flexibility**, with the mix of raw storage (HDFS), dataflow engines (Spark), analytic packages and SQL (Impala), at the cost of overhead. The dominant theme is **flexibility and heterogeneity** (storage formats, computing paradigms, implementations), with the format one having the greatest impact **by an order of magnitude or more**. **The why** (REDBK p.24): heterogeneity of **storage format** affects **novices, experts and architects** (all three audiences); that of **computing paradigm** mainly affects **experts and architects**; that of **systems implementation** mainly affects **architects**. All three are seen as relevant developments, with doubts about market impact and longevity. New formats (Avro, Parquet, RCFile) combine semi-structured data with advances such as columnar layout and are even more flexible than XML (REDBK p.24).
- **Looking ahead** (REDBK p.24-25; **text by Peter Bailis**, who signs the introduction to ch. 5, REDBK p.23; not by Hellerstein or Stonebraker): MapReduce was an extreme, ephemeral architecture that opened up the design space. Debates that **Bailis** cites (REDBK p.25): Rasmussen and colleagues argue that intermediate fault tolerance would only be necessary in large clusters (more than 100 nodes); McSherry and colleagues illustrate that many workloads run well on a single server (or thread), without distribution; domain-specific systems (GraphLab) versus a single engine (GraphX, Grail). Bailis perceives a movement toward consolidation. On Spark (Bailis, REDBK p.25): six years old, backed by Databricks, Cloudera and IBM, it **still has a way to go** to match the feature set of a traditional warehouse, but the feature set is growing fast and expectations are high; the gaps between traditional warehouses and post-MapReduce systems are **closing**. These are views of different authors: Bailis says Spark still needs to evolve and that the gaps are closing, and does not claim that Stonebraker's criticism is wrong (Stonebraker's view follows).
- **Stonebraker's commentary (October 26, 2015)** (REDBK p.25-26; it is a separate commentary, **after Bailis's text**, and the attribution "70% of accesses to SparkSQL" is Stonebraker's, citing Zaharia): BI was historically served by applications over a relational warehouse (Teradata, Vertica, Redshift, Greenplum); MapReduce, custom-built by Google for the Web crawl database, was promoted by marketing, and Google itself moved to BigTable. He considers it **inadequate** as a platform for data warehouse products and for distributed applications (message passing through the file system is too slow); the vendors renamed the platform HDFS and launched SQL engines (such as Cloudera's Impala), so "the MapReduce crowd became the SQL crowd". On Spark: according to the lead author, more than **70% of accesses** go through SparkSQL, which gives Spark "an identity problem": to compete as SQL it needs persistence, indexes, memory sharing and catalogs; the other 30% (mainly Scala) is distributed computation, but there is no coupling between data management and analytics, and Spark is in-memory only. **Lessons**: do not adopt something just because Google thinks it is a good idea; distrust marketing, especially performance claims; the community loves "the next shiny object", which generates turnover in the organization.
