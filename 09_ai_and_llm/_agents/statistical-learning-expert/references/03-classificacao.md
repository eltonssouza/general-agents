## 1. Why not linear regression for classes
- `[from source]` Coding a response with 3+ categories as 1, 2, 3 imposes an ordering and equal distances that do not exist; different codings give different models (ISLR p.144-145). With a binary 0/1 response, linear regression estimates Pr(class 1 | X), and the classifications coincide with those of LDA; however, the estimates can fall outside [0, 1] (ISLR p.145).
- `[from source]` ESL, the same point with more mechanics: in indicator-matrix regression ŷ = X(XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀY, one classifies by the largest component; the f̂_k sum to 1 if there is an intercept, but can be negative or > 1 (ESL p.122-123). For K ≥ 3 there is **masking**: in the example of three linearly separable classes the middle class never dominates (training error 0.33 with degree 1 and 0.04 with degree 2); loose rule: K aligned classes may require polynomial terms up to degree K − 1 (ESL p.124-125). In the vowel data (11 classes, p = 10) the test error is 0.67 for linear regression, against 0.56 for LDA and 0.51 for logistic: the book attributes the difference to masking (ESL p.125-126).

## 2. Logistic regression
- `[from source]` p(X) = e^{β₀+β₁X}/(1 + e^{β₀+β₁X}) (S curve); odds = p/(1 − p) = e^{β₀+β₁X}; the logit (log-odds) is linear in X; p = 0.2 corresponds to odds 1/4 and p = 0.9 to odds 9 (ISLR p.147). Increasing X by one unit adds β₁ to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by e^{β₁}; the effect on p depends on the value of X (ISLR p.147-148).
- `[from source]` Estimation by **maximum likelihood** (preferred over non-linear least squares); the likelihood is ℓ(β) = Π_{y=1} p(xᵢ) Π_{y=0}(1 − p(xᵢ′)) (ISLR p.148). The z-statistic plays the role of the t (ISLR p.149). Default: β̂₁ = 0.0055 and β̂₀ = −10.6513; p̂(1000) = 0.00576 (< 1%) and p̂(2000) = 0.586 (ISLR p.149).
- `[from source]` **Confounding**: the coefficient of *student* is positive in simple regression (0.0431 against 0.0292) and negative in the multiple regression with balance and income, because students have larger balances; that is, a student is riskier if the balance is unknown, but less risky than a non-student with the same balance (ISLR p.150-152). Predictions from the multiple regression: 0.058 (student) and 0.105 (non-student) with balance 1,500 and income 40 thousand (ISLR p.152).
- `[from source]` Multiclass logistic regression exists but is little used; discriminant analysis is popular in the multiclass case (ISLR p.152-153).
- `[from source]` ESL: K − 1 logits; ML by Newton-Raphson, which is **iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)** with adjusted response z = Xβ + W⁻¹(y − p); concave log-likelihood; β = 0 as the starting value, *step-size halving* if necessary (ESL p.138-140). Coefficient exp(0.081) = 1.084 → +8.4% in the odds per kg of tobacco, CI (1.03; 1.14) (ESL p.143); Wald tests (exclusion) and Rao tests (inclusion) (ESL p.144); logistic with L1 penalty (ESL p.144-146).

## 3. LDA and QDA
- `[from source]` Approach: model the distribution of the predictors in each class and invert by Bayes: Pr(Y = k | X = x) = π_k f_k(x)/Σ π_l f_l(x) (ISLR p.154). Reasons for LDA: with well-separated classes the logistic estimates are unstable; with small n and approximately normal X, LDA is more stable; it is popular for K > 2 (ISLR p.153).
- `[from source]` LDA with p = 1 (common variance σ²): δ_k(x) = x·μ_k/σ² − μ_k²/(2σ²) + log π_k; with π₁ = π₂ the Bayes boundary lies at (μ₁ + μ₂)/2 (ISLR p.155). Estimates: μ̂_k = class mean; σ̂² = (1/(n − K)) Σ_k Σ_{i∈k}(xᵢ − μ̂_k)²; π̂_k = n_k/n (ISLR p.156). In the example, Bayes error 10.6% and LDA 11.1% (ISLR p.156).
- `[from source]` LDA with p > 1: X ~ N(μ_k, Σ) with common Σ; δ_k(x) = xᵀΣ⁻¹μ_k − ½μ_kᵀΣ⁻¹μ_k + log π_k; "linear" because δ_k is linear in x (ISLR p.158-159). **QDA** gives each class its own Σ_k; δ_k is quadratic in x (ISLR p.164).
- `[from source]` LDA × QDA is bias-variance: estimating one covariance costs p(p + 1)/2 parameters; QDA estimates K p(p + 1)/2 (with 50 predictors, 1,275 per class); LDA has K·p linear coefficients. LDA is better with little data; QDA with a large training set or when the common Σ is clearly false (ISLR p.164-165). ESL: LDA has (K − 1)(p + 1) parameters and QDA (K − 1){p(p + 3)/2 + 1} (ESL p.129-130).
- `[from source]` ESL: LDA and QDA do well on a surprisingly large set of problems; the probable reason is **not** Gaussianity, but that the data only support simple boundaries, estimated stably (bias-variance) (ESL p.130). In the STATLOG project LDA was among the top three in 7 of 22 data sets (ESL p.130).
- `[from source]` RDA (Friedman): Σ_k(α) = αΣ_k + (1 − α)Σ, with α chosen by validation or CV; also Σ(γ) = γΣ + (1 − γ)σ²I (ESL p.131). Two classes: the coefficient vector of the regression with ±1 targets is proportional to the LDA direction, but the intercepts differ if N₁ ≠ N₂ (ESL p.128-129); with K > 2, LDA avoids masking (ESL p.129).

## 4. Confusion matrix, threshold and ROC (ISLR p.159-164)
- `[from source]` In Default, only 3.33% of individuals default, so a **null** classifier (always "no") errs 3.33% (LDA errs 2.75% on the training set) (ISLR p.159-160). Matrix: LDA predicted 104 defaulters (81 hits, 23 false), but missed 252 of the 333 defaulters (75.7%); sensitivity 24.3%, specificity 99.8% (ISLR p.160).
- `[from source]` The Bayes classifier uses threshold 0.5; lowering it to 0.2 (Pr(default) > 0.2) reduces the missed from 75.7% to 41.4% at the cost of 235 false positives and total error 3.73%; the threshold should come from domain knowledge and costs (ISLR p.161-162).
- `[from source]` ROC curve: true positive rate (sensitivity) against false positives (1 − specificity) for all thresholds; AUC is the summary (0.95 in Default; 0.5 equals chance, evaluated on an independent test set) (ISLR p.162-163). Rates: FPR = FP/N (type I error, 1 − specificity); TPR = TP/P (power, sensitivity, recall); PPV = TP/P* (precision); NPV = TN/N*; the denominators of the first two are the true counts, those of the last two are the predicted counts (ISLR p.163-164).

## 5. Comparison of the methods (ISLR p.166-169)
- `[from source]` Logistic and LDA have log-odds linear in x; they differ only in the estimation (conditional ML versus Gaussian estimates); LDA improves if normality holds approximately, logistic may be better if it does not (ISLR p.166). KNN makes no assumption about the boundary, dominates if it is highly non-linear, but does not indicate which predictors matter (ISLR p.166); QDA is a compromise between KNN and the linear methods (ISLR p.167).
- `[from source]` Six simulated scenarios with p = 2: (1) and (2) normals with linear boundary: LDA best, KNN-1 poor, QDA worse than LDA; (3) Student's *t*: logistic beats LDA and QDA deteriorates; (4) normals with correlations ±0.5: QDA best; (5) quadratic boundary: QDA and KNN-CV best; (6) more complex: KNN-CV best and KNN with K = 1 the worst (ISLR p.167-169). Conclusion: no method dominates; linear ones if the boundary is linear, QDA if moderately non-linear, KNN (with smoothness chosen by CV) if complex (ISLR p.169). Adding X², X³ to logistic or LDA adds flexibility; LDA with all quadratics and products has the form of QDA (ISLR p.169).
- `[from source]` ESL: logistic and LDA have the same form of logits; logistic leaves Pr(X) arbitrary and maximizes the conditional likelihood; LDA uses the joint (Gaussian mixture). If the f_k are Gaussian, ignoring the marginal part costs about 30% of asymptotic efficiency in the error rate ("with 30% more data logistic matches"); LDA is not robust to gross outliers; if the data are perfectly separable, the logistic estimates are infinite and those of LDA well defined; in general one feels that logistic is a safer and more robust bet (ESL p.146-147).

## Pitfalls
- Reporting only the overall error rate with imbalanced classes (ISLR p.159-160).
- Using threshold 0.5 by inertia when the costs of the errors are asymmetric (ISLR p.161-162).
- Using indicator regression with K ≥ 3 without checking masking (ESL p.124-125).
- Interpreting the coefficient of a simple logistic regression without checking confounding (ISLR p.150-152).
