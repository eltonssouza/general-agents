## 1. Why convolutions (UDL ch.10)

- `[from source]` Images: high dimension (224×224 RGB = 150,528 inputs; a hidden layer of the same size would have more than 150,528² ≈ 22 billion weights), neighboring pixels are related, interpretation is stable under geometric transformations (UDL p.175).
- `[from source]` **Invariance** `f[t[x]] = f[x]` (classification) and **equivariance** `f[t[x]] = t[f[x]]` (segmentation) (UDL p.176, eq. 10.1-10.2). Convolutional layers are equivariant to translation; pooling gives partial invariance (UDL p.177).
- `[from source]` 1D convolution with kernel 3: `z_i = ω1 x_{i−1} + ω2 x_i + ω3 x_{i+1}` (eq. 10.3); footnote: technically it is cross-correlation (the ML convention) (UDL p.177). Padding: zero, circular, reflection or "valid" (no padding, smaller output) (UDL p.178). Stride, kernel size (odd) and dilation (atrous) (UDL p.178-179).
- `[from source]` Convolutional layer `h_i = a[β + Σ ω_j x_{i+j−2}]` uses 3 weights and 1 bias, versus D² weights for the equivalent fully connected layer; the latter can reproduce the former with zeroed and repeated weights (UDL p.179-180, eq. 10.4-10.5, fig. 10.4).
- `[from source]` Channels: with Ci input channels, kernel K and Co output channels there are `Ci·Co·K` weights and `Co` biases (UDL p.180-181). The receptive field grows with depth: kernel 3 → 3, 5, 7 … (UDL p.181-182, fig. 10.6). 2D: `h_ij = a[β + Σ_m Σ_n ω_mn x_{i+m−2,j+n−2}]` and `Ci·Co·K·K` weights plus `Co` biases (UDL p.184, eq. 10.6).
- `[from source]` MNIST-1D experiment (UDL p.181-184): input 40; three convolutional layers with 15 channels, kernel 3, stride 2, "valid" → 19, 9 and 4 positions (4×15 = 60 values) → fully connected layer with 10 outputs; the convolutional network has **2,050 parameters** and the fully connected one with layers of 285, 135 and 60 units has **59,065**; test ~17% (conv) versus ~40% (FC), although both fit the training set (UDL p.181-183, fig. 10.8). Explanation: inductive bias; convolution is a regularizer with an "infinite penalty" on most FC solutions (UDL p.184). `[checked in dl_nets.py cnn-shapes --preset udl-mnist1d]`.
- `[from source]` Downsampling (subsampling/stride 2, max pooling, mean pooling) and upsampling (duplicate, max unpooling, bilinear, transposed convolution, whose matrix is the transpose of the stride-2 one) (UDL p.185-188, fig. 10.11-10.13); 1×1 convolution changes the number of channels without spatial pooling (UDL p.188, fig. 10.14).
- `[from source]` ImageNet: 1,281,167 training images, 50,000 validation, 100,000 test, 1,000 classes (UDL p.188). **AlexNet** (2012): 8 hidden layers (5 convolutional + 3 FC), first layer 11×11 with stride 4 and 96 channels, FC of 4,096, 4,096 and 1,000 units, ~60 million parameters (most in the FC layers), data augmentation by 2,048, momentum 0.9, batch 128, dropout, L2; top-5 with 16.4% error and top-1 with 38.1% (UDL p.189-190). **VGG**: 19 layers, 144 million parameters, 6.8% (top-5) and 23.7% (top-1) error (UDL p.190). Depth helps up to a point (UDL p.190, p.194). `[DATED]`.
- `[from source]` YOLO (detection): input 448×448, 24 convolutional layers, 7×7 grid with 1,024 channels, FC of 4,096, 2 boxes per cell with 5 parameters each (x, y, width, height and confidence); heuristic box suppression and transfer learning from ImageNet (UDL p.191-192). Semantic segmentation (Noh et al.): 224×224 → 224×224×21, encoder-decoder/hourglass with max unpooling and deconvolution (UDL p.192-193).
- Notes `[from source]`: LeNet (LeCun et al. 1998) already had convolutions + FC but with sigmoids and average pooling; GoogLeNet/inception; depthwise, grouped (AlexNet used them because of a GPU limit), separable convolutions; squeeze-and-excitation; PixelShuffle (UDL p.194-196). `[NOT READ]` p.197-199 (rest of the notes and problems).

## 2. Residual networks (UDL ch.11)

- `[from source]` Initial observation: going from 8 layers (AlexNet) to 18 (VGG) improved results, but adding more made them worse — **even on the training set**, which indicates a training failure, not a generalization failure (UDL p.200-202; CIFAR-10: 20 layers > 56, fig. 11.2). Hypothesis: "shattered gradients" — in deep sequential networks the gradient changes unpredictably with small changes (UDL p.201-203).
- `[from source]` Residual block: `h1 = x + f1[x, φ1]` etc. (eq. 11.4); unrolled, the output is the sum of the input with smaller networks (an "ensemble") and there are 2^K paths (16 for K=4) (UDL p.203-205, eq. 11.5-11.6). Order of operations: ReLU before the linear transformation (otherwise only non-negative values are added) and start with a linear layer (UDL p.205).
- `[from source]` With He init, the forward variance **doubles** with each block (exponential growth); dividing by √2 fixes it, but the usual remedy is BatchNorm (UDL p.206).
- `[from source]` **BatchNorm**: per unit, `m_h`, `s_h` over the batch; `h ← (h − m_h)/(s_h + ε)` and then `h ← γ h + δ` (UDL p.206-208, eq. 11.7-11.9). Parameters: K·D of each of γ and δ in networks with K layers of D units; in a CNN the statistics are over batch and position, K·C of each. At test time frozen statistics from the training dataset are used (UDL p.208). Cost: redundancy in the weights and extra parameters. Benefits: stable forward pass, larger learning rates, regularization from batch noise (UDL p.208).
- `[from source]` **ResNet**: block with BatchNorm-ReLU-conv repeated and added to the input; **bottleneck** block (1×1 reduces channels, 3×3, 1×1 restores) (UDL p.209-210, fig. 11.7); ResNet-200: top-5 4.8% and top-1 20.1% error, "one of the first to surpass human performance (5.1% top-5)"; state-of-the-art models at the time of the book were all transformers (9.0% top-1 error) (UDL p.209) `[DATED 2023]`. **DenseNet**: concatenate earlier outputs (Fig. 11.9: 3→32→35→67 channels); the chain is broken at downsamplings (UDL p.210-211). **U-Net**: encoder-decoder with skips by concatenation; the original used "valid" convolutions (cropping); fully convolutional, runs at any size; hourglass adds instead of concatenating and is used in pose estimation with heatmaps (UDL p.211-214).
- `[from source]` Why they work: it is not just depth — wide, shallow networks sometimes outperform deep ones and almost all of the gradient comes from short paths (Veit et al. 2016: in 54 blocks, paths of 5 to 17 blocks, 0.45% of the paths) (UDL p.213, p.216); the loss surface becomes smoother (UDL p.213-215, fig. 11.13).
- Notes `[from source]` (UDL p.215-219): ResNet v1 vs v2 (pre-activation); residuals alone double the trainable depth (Sankararaman 2020); BatchNorm causes gradient explosion of `√(π/(π−1)) ≈ 1.21` per layer in ReLU without skips (Yang 2019); variants GhostNorm, batch renormalization, **LayerNorm**, GroupNorm, InstanceNorm (fig. 11.14); "internal covariate shift" is disputed by Santurkar 2018; alternatives without BatchNorm: SkipInit, Stable ResNet, FixUp, `1/√2` (UDL p.216-219).

## 3. Convolutions in NNDL (ch.6)

- `[from source]` Three ideas: local receptive fields, shared weights/biases, pooling (NNDL p.244). A 5×5 field over 28×28 generates 24×24 neurons (28−5+1) (NNDL p.245). Neuron (j,k): `σ(b + Σ_{l=0}^4 Σ_{m=0}^4 w_{l,m} a_{j+l,k+m})` (NNDL p.246, eq. 125 — text; formula only from context). "Feature map", "kernel/filter" (NNDL p.246).
- `[from source]` Parameters: 20 maps of 5×5 = 20 × 26 = 520, versus 784×30 + 30 = 23,550 for an FC with 30 hidden units (>40 times more) (NNDL p.248-249). `[checked in dl_calc.py conv-params]`.
- `[from source]` 2×2 max-pooling (24×24 → 12×12) and L2 pooling (NNDL p.249-251).
- `[from source]` Ch.6 network (`network3.py`, Theano 0.6, GPU): shallow [FC 100 + softmax] 97.80%; +1 conv-pool (20 maps 5×5) 98.78%; +2nd conv-pool (40 maps) 99.06%; with ReLU (η=0.03, λ=0.1) 99.23%; with data expanded by 1-pixel shifts (50,000 → 250,000) 99.37%; with dropout 0.5 on the FC layers (1,000 neurons), 99.60%; ensemble of networks 99.67% (33 errors in 10,000) (NNDL p.254-264). An extra or larger FC layer gives 99.43-99.48% (NNDL p.261). Cireşan 2010 (FC only, 2,500-2,000-1,500-1,000-500, GPU, data expansion and η decreasing from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶) 99.65% (NNDL p.265). `[checked in dl_nets.py cnn-shapes]`: shapes 28→24→12→8→4 and FC with 640 inputs.
- `[from source]` Why dropout only on the FC layers: convolutions have built-in resistance to overfitting through shared weights (NNDL p.264-265).
- `[from source]` Why "we can train": convolutions reduce parameters, strong regularization, ReLU (3 to 5× faster according to the author), GPUs and long training (NNDL p.266).
- `[from source]` Large-scale imaging (NNDL p.281-290): LRMD 2012 (ImageNet 2011, 16 million images, 20 thousand categories, 15.8% vs 9.3%) and KSH (Krizhevsky-Sutskever-Hinton; the network UDL calls AlexNet, p.189-190 `[inference]`) (ILSVRC-2012: top-5 84.7% vs 73.8% for 2nd place, 63.3% top-1; ~60 million parameters; 2 GPUs) (NNDL p.282-285); GoogLeNet in 2014: 93.33% top-5 (2013: 88.3%) (NNDL p.286). Human (Karpathy): 5.1% vs GoogLeNet 6.8% error (NNDL p.287). Adversarial examples and noise images classified with high confidence (NNDL p.288-290).
- `[from source]` Documented error in the NNDL code: `FullyConnectedLayer` uses `scale=sqrt(1/n_out)` instead of `1/n_in` (NNDL p.268).

## 4. Difference in approach (UDL × NNDL)
- UDL starts from invariance/equivariance and MNIST-1D, and counts parameters; NNDL starts from receptive fields and counts parameters on 2D MNIST.
- For the same AlexNet, UDL cites top-5 error of 16.4% and top-1 of 38.1% (UDL p.190); NNDL cites top-5 accuracy of 84.7% and top-1 of 63.3% (NNDL p.283). 100 − 84.7 = 15.3 ≠ 16.4: the texts do not explain the difference (possibly different evaluation sizes/sets) — do not claim equivalence. `[inference]`
- UDL has residuals and normalization; NNDL does not (ch.6 predates ResNet as a focus).
