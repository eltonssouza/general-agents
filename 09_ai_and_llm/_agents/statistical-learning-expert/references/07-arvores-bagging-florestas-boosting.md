## 1. Decision trees (ISLR 8.1; ESL 9.2)
- `[from source]` They partition the predictor space into simple regions (boxes) and predict by the mean (regression) or mode (classification); they are simple and interpretable, but typically less accurate than the best supervised methods (ISLR p.318). Terms: internal nodes, terminal nodes (leaves), branches (ISLR p.320). ESL: the model is f̂(X) = Σ c_m I{X ∈ R_m}; the binary tree representation describes the partition and "mimics the way a doctor thinks" (ESL p.324-326).
- `[from source]` **Regression**: one seeks boxes that minimize RSS = Σ_j Σ_{i∈R_j}(yᵢ − ŷ_{R_j})² (8.1); since enumerating partitions is infeasible, one uses **recursive binary splitting**, top-down and **greedy**: at each step choose the predictor X_j and the cut s that reduce the RSS the most, without looking ahead (ISLR p.321-322). ESL: (9.12)-(9.14): the best constant on each side is the mean (ESL p.326).
- `[from source]` **Pruning**: a large tree overfits; an RSS-reduction threshold is shortsighted (a bad split may precede a great one); the strategy is to grow a large tree T₀ and prune it by **cost-complexity pruning** (*weakest link*): minimizes Σ_m Σ_{i∈R_m}(yᵢ − ŷ_{R_m})² + α|T| (8.4); α = 0 gives T₀ and large α gives smaller trees; α chosen by K-fold (Algorithm 8.1) (ISLR p.322-324). Hitters: 132 train and 131 test, 6-fold CV (132 is a multiple of 6), CV minimum at 3 leaves (ISLR p.325-326). ESL: minimum node ≈ 5, C_α(T) (9.16), α by 5 or 10 folds (ESL p.327).
- `[from source]` **Classification**: predicts the most common class; RSS does not work. The classification error E = 1 − max_k p̂_mk (8.5) is not very sensitive; one uses **Gini** G = Σ p̂_mk(1 − p̂_mk) (8.6, node purity measure) or **entropy** D = −Σ p̂_mk log p̂_mk (8.7), numerically similar; for pruning, either, but the classification error is preferable if the goal is the accuracy of the final tree (ISLR p.326-327). Splits that do not change the prediction can exist because they increase purity (RestECG<1: 9/9 against 7/11) (ISLR p.329).
- `[from source]` ESL: impurities in (9.17); with two classes they are 1 − max(p, 1 − p), 2p(1 − p) and −p log p − (1 − p) log(1 − p); Gini and entropy are differentiable; they are weighted by the sizes N_mL and N_mR of the children; example (400, 400): the splits (300,100)+(100,300) and (200,400)+(200,0) have the same error 0.25, but the second produces a pure node and has lower Gini and entropy, so use Gini or entropy to grow (ESL p.328-329). `[inference]` Package calculation (`slr_calc.py node`): weighted Gini 0.375 in the first split against 0.333 in the second. Gini is also the expected error rate of classifying by random draw according to p̂_mk (ESL p.329).
- `[from source]` Unordered categorical predictors with q levels: 2^{q−1} − 1 partitions; with 0-1 output (or quantitative) the levels are ordered by the proportion of class 1 (or by the mean) and treated as ordered, an optimal result; the algorithm favors predictors with many levels and can overfit badly (ESL p.329). Loss matrix L_kk′ and observation weighting (ESL p.329-330). Missing data: a "missing" category or **surrogate splits** (ESL p.330). Multiway splits fragment the data (ESL p.330). Others: C4.5/C5.0 (rule sets), splits on linear combinations (ESL p.331).
- `[from source]` **Trees × linear models**: f(X) = β₀ + ΣXⱼβⱼ (8.8) against Σ c_m·1(X ∈ R_m) (8.9); if the relationship is well approximated by a linear model, linear regression beats the tree; if it is highly non-linear and complex, the tree may win; assess by CV (ISLR p.329-330).
- `[from source]` **Advantages**: easy to explain (more so than linear regression), mirror human decision-making, great in plots, handle qualitative predictors without dummies (ISLR p.330). **Disadvantages**: lower accuracy and **not robust**: a small change in the data changes the tree a lot (ISLR p.331). ESL: the high variance comes from the hierarchical nature (the error at the top propagates), the surface is not smooth and it is hard to capture additive structure (ESL p.331-332). On spam, the 17-node tree has a test error of 9.3% (ESL p.332).

## 2. Bagging
- `[from source]` Trees have high variance; **bagging** (bootstrap aggregation) reduces the variance: average of B trees fitted to bootstrap samples, f̂_bag(x) = (1/B)Σ f̂*ᵇ(x); the trees grow deep and **unpruned**; in classification, majority vote; B is not critical (a very large one does not cause overfitting; B = 100 sufficed on Heart) (ISLR p.331-332). The variance of the mean of n independent variables is σ²/n (ISLR p.331).
- `[from source]` **Out-of-bag (OOB) error**: each tree uses about 2/3 of the observations; the rest (≈ B/3 trees per observation) allow a valid OOB prediction; with large B the OOB error is equivalent to LOOCV (ISLR p.333-334). Variable importance: total drop in RSS (regression) or Gini (classification), averaged over the B trees; bagging trades interpretability for accuracy (ISLR p.334-335).
- `[from source]` ESL: the bagging estimator differs from the original only if it is non-linear or adaptive; the vote proportions are **not** probability estimates (true probability 0.75, all vote 1 → 1); averaging the trees' probabilities reduces the variance, especially for small B (ESL p.301-302). The ideal aggregate never increases the MSE (8.52), but this does **not hold for 0-1 loss**: bagging a good classifier improves it, a bad one worsens it; *bagged* trees are not independent (the "wisdom of crowds" requires independence); bagging loses the simple structure (a *bagged* tree is no longer a tree); nearest neighbors, already stable, change little; with a single split bagging did not help (0.166 against 0.065 for boosting) (ESL p.304-307).

## 3. Random forests
- `[from source]` Like bagging, but at each split only a random sample of m of the p predictors is considered (typically m ≈ √p, such as 4 of 13); this **decorrelates** the trees (if there is one strong predictor, the *bagged* trees look alike and averaging correlated quantities reduces the variance little); m = p is equivalent to bagging; small m helps with many correlated predictors; there is no overfitting as B increases (ISLR p.334-336). Gene expression example: 349 patients, 500 highest-variance genes, 15 classes; single-tree error 45.7%, "null rate" 75.4%, 400 trees suffice (ISLR p.335-337).
- `[from source]` ESL: the variance of the mean of B identically distributed variables with correlation ρ is ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B (15.1); the correlation limits the gain; for *bootstrap* trees ρ is usually 0.05 or less (ESL p.607-608). Algorithm 15.1 (ESL p.607). Authors' defaults: classification m = ⌊√p⌋ and minimum node 1; regression m = ⌊p/3⌋ and minimum node 5, but treat them as tuning parameters (on California housing, m = 6 was much better than the default ⌊8/3⌋ = 2) (ESL p.611). On spam: forest 4.88%, boosting 4.5%, bagging 5.4% (ESL p.609); on the "nested spheres" problem and on California housing boosting beats the forest (ESL p.610-611). The forest stabilizes at ~200 trees; the OOB error is almost identical to that of N-fold CV, so the forest is fitted in a single sequence and training can stop when the OOB stabilizes (ESL p.610-612). OOB permutation importance; permutation is **not** equivalent to removing the variable (others serve as surrogates); the proximity plot (MDS) is of doubtful usefulness (ESL p.612-614). ESL states that "random forests" tends to match boosting with simpler tuning (ESL p.606).

## 4. Boosting (ISLR 8.2.3; ESL ch. 10)
- `[from source]` ISLR, Algorithm 8.2: start with f̂ = 0 and rᵢ = yᵢ; for b = 1..B, fit a tree f̂ᵇ with d splits to the residuals (X, r), update f̂ ← f̂ + λf̂ᵇ (8.10) and rᵢ ← rᵢ − λf̂ᵇ(xᵢ) (8.11); output f̂ = Σ λf̂ᵇ (8.12). Idea: **learn slowly**; no bootstrap (ISLR p.336-338). Three parameters: B (can overfit, slowly; CV), λ (0.01 or 0.001; small λ requires large B) and d (interaction depth; d = 1 = *stumps*, an additive model) (ISLR p.337-338). On 15 classes, stumps outperformed depth 2 and the forest; small trees suffice (ISLR p.338).
- `[from source]` ESL, **AdaBoost.M1** (Algorithm 10.1): weights wᵢ = 1/N; for m = 1..M fit G_m with weights; weighted err_m; α_m = log((1 − err_m)/err_m); wᵢ ← wᵢ·exp[α_m·I(yᵢ ≠ G_m(xᵢ))]; G(x) = sign[Σ α_m G_m(x)] (ESL p.357-358). Example: the *stump* alone errs 45.8%; after 400 iterations 5.8%; 244-node tree, 24.7% (ESL p.358-359). Breiman called it the "best off-the-shelf classifier in the world" (ESL p.359).
- `[from source]` The success "is not mysterious": boosting fits an **additive model** Σ β_m b(x; γ_m) by *forward stagewise* (Algorithm 10.2) (ESL p.360-361); with squared loss the residual is fitted (ESL p.361-362); AdaBoost = *forward stagewise* with exponential loss exp(−yf), with β_m = ½ log((1 − err_m)/err_m) and α_m = 2β_m (ESL p.362-363); the training error reaches zero in ~250 iterations and the test error keeps falling (ESL p.363). The population minimizer is half the log-odds (ESL p.364-365).
- `[from source]` **Robustness**: exponential loss concentrates influence exponentially on negative margins; the binomial *deviance* log(1 + e^{−2yf}) grows linearly and is much more robust to noise and mislabeled data, where AdaBoost degrades; squared loss is not a good substitute for 0-1; in regression, absolute loss is more robust than squared and Huber's combines the two (ESL p.366-368).
- `[from source]` **GBM** (gradient boosting): fit the tree to the negative gradient (pseudo-residuals) by least squares; Table 10.2 (squared → residual; absolute → sign; Huber; multinomial deviance → I(y = G_k) − p_k); Algorithm 10.3 (initialize at the optimal constant, compute r_im, fit tree, compute γ_jm per region, update) (ESL p.377-380). Implementations: MART, gbm, mboost (ESL p.380) `[DATED]`.
- `[from source]` **GBM parameters**: J (tree size, the same for all): the maximum interaction level is J − 1; J = 2 (stump) gives only main effects; "4 ≤ J ≤ 8 works well" and J > 10 is unlikely to be needed (ESL p.381-382). M by validation with *early stopping* (ESL p.383). **Shrinkage** ν: f_m = f_{m−1} + ν·Σγ_jm I(x ∈ R_jm); the best is small ν (< 0.1) and M by *early stopping*, at greater computational cost (ESL p.383-384). **Subsampling** (*stochastic gradient boosting*, fraction η ≈ ½ without replacement) reduces cost and often improves (ESL p.384, 386); four parameters J, M, ν, η (ESL p.386). Relative importance averaged over the trees (largest = 100) and *partial dependence* f_S(X_S) = E_{X_C} f(X_S, X_C) for 1 to 3 variables (ESL p.386-389). On spam the GBM errs 4.5% against 5.5% for additive logistic, 8.7% for pruned CART and 5.5% for MARS (ESL p.371-372).
- `[from source]` What boosting sacrifices from trees: speed, interpretability and (in AdaBoost) robustness to mislabeling; GBM tries to mitigate this (ESL p.371).

## Pitfalls
- Pruning by Gini instead of by classification error when the goal is accuracy (ISLR p.327).
- Using the bagging vote proportion as a calibrated probability (ESL p.302).
- Thinking that more trees overfit bagging/RF (they do not, ISLR p.332, 336) but they can overfit boosting (ISLR p.337).
- Interpreting permutation importance as the "effect of removing the variable" (ESL p.612).
- Using λ or d without validating (ISLR p.337-338).
