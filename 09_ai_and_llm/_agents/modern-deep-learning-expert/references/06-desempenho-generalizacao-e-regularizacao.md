# 06 — Measuring performance, generalization and regularization

Sources: UDL ch.8-9 (PDF p.132-174) and NNDL ch.3 (overfitting, regularization, other techniques, hyperparameters: PDF p.106-173). Read in full (UDL: body and notes; NNDL: body; problems skimmed).

## 1. Test error: noise, bias, variance (UDL ch.8)

- `[from source]` MNIST-1D: 10 classes, 4,000 training examples, 40 dimensions; network with 2 layers of 100 units, SGD with batch 100, η=0.1, 6,000 steps (150 epochs); training reaches zero error and the test error stays at ~40% (chance = 90%); the test loss rises after ~1,500 steps, because the softmax makes the network ever more confident in the same errors (UDL p.132-134). Test: 1,000 extra examples (UDL p.134).
- `[from source]` Three sources of error: noise (irreducible), bias (model unable to fit the true function) and variance (limited training data) (UDL p.136-137). Decomposition for least squares in regression: `E_D[E_y[L[x]]] = variance + bias² + noise σ²` (UDL p.137-138, eq. 8.7). With other losses the terms interact in more complex ways (UDL p.138).
- `[from source]` Reduce variance: more data. Reduce bias: more capacity. But, with fixed data, more capacity increases variance (trade-off); in the book's example the minimum total error occurs at capacity 4 (UDL p.139, fig. 8.9 p.142).
- `[from source]` **Double descent**: with 10,000 training examples and 5,000 test examples (Adam, step 0.005, full-batch, 4,000 steps), the test error does not rise as predicted by the classical trade-off, but keeps falling with more capacity; when 15% of the labels are shuffled, the peak appears near #parameters ≈ #examples and then a second descent, below the best of the classical part (UDL p.141-143, fig. 8.10 p.144). Regimes: classical (under-parameterized), critical and modern (over-parameterized) (UDL p.143).
- `[from source]` Explanation (uncertain): inductive bias; with extra capacity the model interpolates between the points more smoothly; in 40 dimensions and 10^5 examples the coverage is minuscule (10^40 boxes with 10 divisions per dimension: "curse of dimensionality") (UDL p.143-146). What makes the solution smooth? Initialization and/or the training algorithm act as an implicit regularizer (UDL p.146).
- `[from source]` Choice of hyperparameters: training, validation (for choosing) and test (for measuring the final result) sets; search is expensive because many hyperparameters are discrete and conditional (UDL p.146-147). In the notes, the book calls the training/validation/test split "cross-validation" and describes k-fold (UDL p.148).
- Notes `[from source]`: representational vs effective capacity, VC-dimension, Rademacher; epoch-wise double descent; adding data can worsen performance (UDL p.148-149); high-dimensional properties (nearly orthogonal points, volume in the "shell") (UDL p.149); data drift: covariate shift, prior shift, concept shift (UDL p.149); random search, Bayesian optimization, SMAC, Tree-Parzen, Hyperband, BOHB (UDL p.149-150).

## 2. Regularization in UDL (ch.9)

Definitions: strictly, a term in the loss; in practice, any strategy that improves generalization (UDL p.152).
- Explicit: `Σℓi + λ g[φ]`; probabilistic view: `λ g[φ] = −log Pr(φ)` (MAP) (UDL p.153-154, eq. 9.2-9.4). **L2**: `λ Σ φj²` (Tikhonov, ridge, Frobenius); in networks it is applied to the weights and not to the biases, and smooths the function (reduces variance, increases bias) (UDL p.154-155, eq. 9.5).
- Implicit: with a finite step, GD follows the modified loss `L + (α/4)||∂L/∂φ||²` (eq. 9.8); SGD adds `(α/4B) Σ_b ||∂Lb/∂φ − ∂L/∂φ||²` (variance of the batch gradients) (UDL p.155-158, eq. 9.9). Trends: full-batch GD generalizes better with larger steps; SGD generalizes better than GD and smaller batches tend to be better (UDL p.156, p.158-159, fig. 9.5).
- Heuristics (UDL p.159-168): early stopping (keep the best point on validation); ensembling (average of the outputs or of the softmax pre-activations; bagging); **dropout** (zero typically 50% of the hidden units at each step; at test time use the weight scaling rule `×(1−p)` or Monte Carlo dropout); noise on inputs, weights or labels; **label smoothing** (probability `1−ρ` on the label and the rest split among the other classes); Bayesian inference (infinite weighted set; only approximations); transfer learning (remove the last layer, fine-tune everything or only the new ones), multi-task, generative self-supervised learning (masking) and contrastive; data augmentation. Four principles: make the function smoother, increase the effective data, combine models and seek wider minima (UDL p.168, fig. 9.14).
- Notes `[from source]` (UDL p.169-173): Hanson-Pratt "weight decay" is `φ ← (1−λ')φ − α ∂L/∂φ` (eq. 9.13) and is only equivalent to L2 in plain SGD, with `λ = λ'/(2α)`; in Adam they are different, hence AdamW. The book says it is a "misconception" to call L2 weight decay (UDL p.169) — although in the main text (p.154) it uses "weight decay" for L2 on the weights (inconsistent usage in the book itself). L1/LASSO (sparsity), L0, elastic net (UDL p.170). Early stopping ≈ L2 with `λ ≈ 1/(τα)` under a quadratic approximation (UDL p.171). Dropout by Hinton 2012 / Srivastava 2014 (UDL p.172). AlexNet increased the dataset by a factor of 2,048 with augmentation (UDL p.173).

## 3. Regularization in NNDL (ch.3)

- `[from source]` Visible overfitting: [784,30,10] trained with only 1,000 images and cross-entropy, η=0.5, m=10, 400 epochs: the training cost always falls; test accuracy stabilizes at ~82% around epoch 280 and the test cost worsens from epoch ~15; training accuracy reaches 100% and test accuracy stays at 82.27% (NNDL p.107-111). With 50,000 images: training 97.86% vs test 95.33% (gap of 2.53 pp versus 17.73 pp) (NNDL p.113-114). More data reduces overfitting (NNDL p.114).
- `[from source]` Validation and early stopping: use `validation_data` (10,000 images) to decide when to stop and for hyperparameters, keeping `test_data` for the final measurement (hold-out method) (NNDL p.111-113).
- `[from source]` **L2 ("weight decay")**: `C = C0 + (λ/2n) Σ w²` (eq. 85-87, NNDL p.114-115); gradient `∂C/∂w = ∂C0/∂w + (λ/n) w` and rule `w → (1 − ηλ/n) w − η ∂C0/∂w` (eq. 88-92, NNDL p.116, checked by render); biases do not decay; in the mini-batch `w → (1−ηλ/n) w − (η/m) Σ ∂C_x/∂w` (NNDL p.117, eq. 93). The code implements this in one line (NNDL p.151, p.154).
- `[from source]` Results: 1,000 images, λ=0.1: peak 87.1% (vs 82.27%) (NNDL p.117-119); 50,000 images and λ=5.0 (λ scales with n to keep the decay factor): 96.49% vs 95.49% and training-test gap <1 pp (NNDL p.119-120); 100 hidden, λ=5.0: 97.92% on validation; η=0.1 and 60 epochs: 98.04% (NNDL p.120).
- `[from source]` Why does it help? The author discusses a degree-9 polynomial vs a line, Occam with caution (stories of Bethe and Mercury) and concludes that the razor is only a guide; "regularization is somewhat of a kludge" with no convincing theory; conjecture of self-regularization of gradient descent in networks (LeCun et al. 1998) (NNDL p.121-127).
- `[from source]` L1: `C = C0 + (λ/n) Σ |w|`, `w → w − (ηλ/n) sgn(w) − η ∂C0/∂w`; shrinks by a constant amount (L2: proportional to w), concentrating weights in a few connections (NNDL p.128-130, eq. 95-98, checked by render).
- `[from source]` Dropout: randomly and temporarily delete half of the hidden units (input and output intact), train on the mini-batch, restore; at test time use the whole network and **halve the output weights of the hidden units** (NNDL p.130-131). Heuristic: like training many networks and averaging; avoids co-adaptation (NNDL p.131-132). MNIST feedforward: 98.4% → 98.7% with dropout and modified L2 (NNDL p.132-133).
- `[from source]` Artificial data expansion: 15° rotations, translations, skew and elastic distortions raise an 800-hidden-unit network from 98.4% to 98.9% and 99.3% (Simard et al. 2003) (NNDL p.134-135). SVM vs network comparison: an SVM with 50,000 images (94.48%) beats the network with 5,000 (93.24%); "is algorithm A better than B?" depends on the data (NNDL p.136-138).

## 4. Hyperparameters: the NNDL workflow (ch.3 p.156-173)
`[from source]`
1. First obtain any signal above chance: simplify the problem (only 0s and 1s, reduces data by 80% and speeds up ×5), the network ([784,10]), monitor more often and on 100 validation images (NNDL p.158-159). Adjust λ proportionally to n (λ=1000 → 20 with 1,000 images) (NNDL p.160).
2. Learning rate: test η = 0.025, 0.25, 2.5 (λ=5.0); find the threshold where the training cost stops falling, starting at 0.01 and varying by factors of 10; use a value ~2× below the threshold (NNDL p.162-164); on MNIST the threshold is ~0.5 and η=0.25 is used (NNDL p.164). Choosing η by the training cost is the author's personal preference (NNDL p.164-165).
3. Early stopping: "no improvement in 10 epochs", then 20, 50 (NNDL p.165-166). Schedule: keep η until validation worsens and divide by 2 or 10, ending at 1/128 (NNDL p.166-167).
4. λ: start at 0, find η, then λ=1.0 as a starting point (no principled justification, NNDL p.167), vary by factors of 10 and re-optimize η (NNDL p.167).
5. Mini-batch: online (m=1) works, but matrix optimization favors larger m with scaled η; choose m from the validation × real time plot (NNDL p.168-170).
6. Automatic: grid search; Bergstra-Bengio 2012; Bayesian (Snoek 2012) (NNDL p.171). "A hyperparameter is never solved, only abandoned" (NNDL p.172).
`[DATED]` 2015 suggestions; see UDL p.149-150 for more recent search methods.

## 5. Regularization equivalence between the books
`[inference]` UDL sums the loss and uses `λΣφ²`; NNDL takes the mean and uses `(λ/2n)Σw²`; an SGD step is identical with `α = η/n` and `λ_UDL = λ_NNDL/2` (verified in `dl_calc.py l2-equiv`). See `comparacao-udl-nndl.md`.
