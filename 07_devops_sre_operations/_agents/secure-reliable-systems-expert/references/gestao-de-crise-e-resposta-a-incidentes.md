## Ch. 17 — Crisis management (BSRS p.423-451)

### Premise (BSRS p.423-424)
- [from source] Keeping systems up even under attack; reliability measures how well the organization withstands a security crisis; IMAG (Incident Management at Google) is the same methodology used for outages and for security incidents; security incidents are inevitable (industry maxim: there are companies that know they were compromised and those that do not) and the outcome depends on preparation and response (BSRS p.423). Current challenges: transparency with users, regulations such as the GDPR, and contracts that pressure deadlines (it is not unusual for a customer to ask to be notified of a possible security problem within 24 hours of detection); cloud, BYOD, and IoT reduce visibility and control over assets (BSRS p.424).

### Is it a crisis or not? (BSRS p.424-426)
- [from source] Not every incident is a crisis; the first step when escalating is triage (analogy with the EMT who assesses a bus and car accident) (BSRS p.424). In triage, find out whether it is an error (false positive), an easily fixable problem (opportunistic compromise), or a complex and potentially damaging problem (targeted compromise); predictable problems follow predetermined processes; targeted attacks require an organized and managed response; have pre-planned criteria for what is an incident and which risks are severe or acceptable (BSRS p.425).
- [from source] Example of three organizations facing ransomware: no. 1 (mature process and layered defenses, only runs signed and approved software) resolves it with one engineer and a standard process; no. 2 (sales demos in the cloud) automatically wipes and recreates compromised instances; no. 3 (few layers and little visibility) faces serious risk and a possible crisis with rebuilding of networks and systems; the same threat has different severities depending on maturity (BSRS p.425-426). Questions to decide between a playbook and crisis management: what stored data could be accessed and what is its value; trust relationships of the compromised system with others; compensating controls the attacker would need to cross; opportunistic malware (adware) or advanced/targeted (tailored phishing); consider the highest likely level of risk (BSRS p.426).
- [from source] Compromises vs bugs: with defense in depth, vulnerabilities do not need to be treated as incidents, but can be managed with IR processes when complex or high-impact; at Google, extreme-risk vulnerabilities are treated as incidents, even without active exploitation; in coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD) a response reinforced by OpSec and confidentiality may be needed; after public disclosure, fixing systems with complex interdependencies is urgent and slow; examples: Spectre and Meltdown, glibc (CVE-2015-0235), Stagefright, Shellshock, Heartbleed; CVD according to Google: balance between the vendor's time, the needs of the reporter, and those of users and customers (emerging ISO 29147:2018 standard) (BSRS p.426-427).

### Taking command (BSRS p.427-430)
- [from source] First step: do not panic; first responders are taught not to run; the seconds gained by running are outweighed by the consequences of not planning (BSRS p.428). Difference between outage and compromise: the system does not resist being fixed, but the attacker can observe and react; fixing without a complete investigation can be catastrophic; the SRE normally fixes first and documents later, whereas a security response requires completing the investigation before attempting to fix; devote the first five minutes to breathing and planning; postmortems rarely say that responding five minutes sooner would have been more effective (BSRS p.428).
- [from source] IMAG is based on the Incident Command System (ICS) used by firefighters, rescue, and police; flexible, lightweight for small incidents, and scalable; it formalizes command, control, and communications; take command by an explicit declaration ("Our team declares an incident involving X and I am the incident commander (IC)"), aligning expectations; warn executives that teams may ignore or bypass normal processes until the incident is contained; the IC directs the response and maintains control with constant communication; the same framework for SRE and security allows interoperating under stress (BSRS p.429).
- [from source] Assembling the team: whoever declares becomes the IC or chooses an IC, explicitly and with the designee's explicit acceptance; the IC finds those who know the affected systems well and "deputizes" them into the IR team; almost every incident needs an operations lead (OL), the tactical counterpart of the IC (the IC sets strategic objectives; the OL takes care of achieving them; the technical team reports to the OL); other roles: management liaison (decides, for example, to shut down a revenue-generating service, send people home, or revoke credentials), legal lead (employee expectation of privacy; permissions to examine browser history, including a personal profile), and communications lead (BSRS p.429-430).

### Operational security (OpSec) (BSRS p.430-433)
- [from source] OpSec in a crisis = keeping the response activity secret (suspected compromise, internal abuse, dangerous vulnerability); establish an OpSec plan before any incident; the IC is responsible for defining, communicating, and enforcing confidentiality rules; brief each member; example: if the email server is in scope, forbid email about the intrusion; document specific guidance per member and ask for acknowledgment before starting; without clear communication there is leakage or premature disclosure (BSRS p.430-431). A good plan also avoids exposing the organization further: an administrator who logs in with administrative credentials on the affected machine gives the attacker leverage; use remote forensic agents deployed in advance to collect artifacts without risking accounts (BSRS p.431). Consequences of alerting the attacker: a persistent one may go quiet (you lose visibility and leads) and one who has already achieved the objective may destroy as much as possible on the way out (BSRS p.431).
- [from source] Common OpSec mistakes: communicating or documenting the incident in a medium the attacker can monitor (email); logging into compromised servers (exposes useful credentials); connecting to the attacker's command and control servers (downloading their malware from the investigation machine, port scanning, domain lookups) and appearing in their logs; locking accounts or changing passwords before completing the investigation; taking systems offline before understanding the scope; allowing access to analysis workstations with credentials the attacker may have stolen (BSRS p.431-432). Good practices: in-person meetings when possible; if using chat or email, use new machines and infrastructure (temporary cloud environment, different machines such as Chromebooks); remote agents or key-based access; be specific about confidentiality when asking for help; consider the conclusions a clever attacker would draw from each action (sudden group policy tightening) (BSRS p.432).
- [from source] When your tools try to help: chat clients, email, and collaborative editors automatically create links and fetch content, which can contact the attacker's infrastructure (example of a log with a link expander User-Agent and a GET for malware_c2.gif); write domains and indicators so the tools do not match them (example[dot]com), as a habit (BSRS p.432-433).
- [from source] Trading good OpSec for the greater good: an exception when there is imminent, clearly identifiable risk (a critical system where vital data, systems, or lives are at risk; a vulnerability so easy and well known, like Shellshock, that shutting the system down may be best); the shutdown signals that something is wrong (BSRS p.433). Imminent-risk tradeoff: in 2019 Apple shut off access to the FaceTime servers for about a day until fixing an easily exploitable privacy bug, seen as correct; big decisions are typically made by executives, and the IC is the expert in the room; often the best of suboptimal options is chosen (BSRS p.433).

### The investigative process (BSRS p.434-437)
- [from source] Investigating a compromise is working back through each phase of the attack to reconstruct the attacker's steps, keeping a tight loop of effort between tasks; digital forensics = discovering all of the attacker's actions on a device; techniques: forensic imaging (secure read-only copy with checksum of disks, useful in court), memory imaging (process trees, executables, passwords of encrypted files), file carving (recovering deleted files, since some OSs only unlink the name), log analysis, and malware analysis (feeds forensics and detection); build a forensic timeline (chronological list of events) to determine correlation and causality (BSRS p.434-435).
- [from source] Malicious email example: an attachment installs a malicious browser extension on a developer's machine, the attacker steals credentials, accesses a file server, and copies confidential files; the instinct to lock the account immediately must be restrained by OpSec; start with a hands-off approach until you know enough; declare an incident and bring in an OL; initial questions (how the backdoor was installed; capabilities; in which other browsers it exists; what the attacker did on the machine) are pivot points: each answer generates new questions (for example, the file share also becomes subject to investigation) (BSRS p.435-436).
- [from source] Sharding the investigation into three tracks: Forensics (systems touched by the attacker), Reversing (suspicious binaries and IOCs), and Hunting (searching for the fingerprints across all systems and notifying Forensics); the OL maintains a short feedback loop (BSRS p.436). When the pace of new leads slows, the IC decides to move to remediation (like knowing when to stop popping popcorn); a lot of planning is needed beforehand (Ch.18); forensics at scale or in the cloud is a broader topic (BSRS p.437).

### Keeping control of the incident (BSRS p.437-441)
- [from source] The IC must devote all their time to controlling and managing the incident, anticipating needs; if you catch yourself checking logs or doing forensics, step back (BSRS p.437). Parallelize: assign future tasks (e.g., a redacted, shareable list of indicators for police or third parties); custom roles at any time (remediation lead, RL, can study compromised areas and prepare the cleanup plan; someone can start the postmortem) (BSRS p.437-438). The IC's role as a loop: check in with each lead (status, new information, blockers, need for staff or resources, overload, upcoming problems, fatigue); update status documents; stakeholders informed; help needed; parallelizable tasks waiting; enough information to remediate; time until the next updates; is the incident over? (BSRS p.438). The OODA loop (observe, orient, decide, act) as a decision mnemonic (BSRS p.438).
- [from source] Handovers: nobody works nonstop for long periods; example of the California fire department (ICs per area, shifts, briefing, and handing off leads); law of diminishing returns: fatigue leads to more mistakes than hits; it is recommended to limit shifts (including IC) to at most 12 continuous hours; split the team into two groups to cover 24 hours if there is staff; without staff, accept a slower response; "hero mode" is unsustainable and should be used rarely; follow-the-sun across the Americas, Asia-Pacific, and Europe (BSRS p.439). Prepare the handover in advance: update documents, evidence notes, and logs; logistics of time and method; the meeting starts with a summary of the state and direction of the investigation, and a formal handover between the leads; a useful question from the outgoing IC: "if I weren't handing the investigation to you, what would I do in the next 12 hours?"; handover agenda: designate a note-taker (preferably from the incoming team), summarize status, outline tasks for the next 12 hours, discuss, the incoming IC outlines the tasks they will take over and schedules the next meetings (BSRS p.441).
- [from source] Morale: the IC is responsible for maintaining morale; tips: eat (plan breaks and food), sleep (diminishing returns; leaders may need to enforce rest), de-stress (a team that broke a disk with a hammer and liquid nitrogen for an hour), watch for burnout (cynicism, defeatism; talk frankly; offer relief with a replacement), and lead by example (visible self-care) (BSRS p.441-442).

### Communications (BSRS p.442-446)
- [from source] Communication is the hardest technical challenge of a response (BSRS p.442). Misunderstandings: jargon, acronyms, implicit assumptions ("we can turn the service back on once the attack is mitigated" means different things to product and security); rule of thumb: be explicit and overcommunicate; the responsibility lies with the communicator (BSRS p.443). Hedging: qualifiers ("we are pretty sure we found all the malware") muddy the decision; prefer specific answers ("we are sure about servers, NAS, email, and file shares, but not about hosted systems with less log visibility") (BSRS p.443).
- [from source] Meetings: quick, regular syncs with the key people (IC, OL, lawyer, executives) every two or four hours; aggressively limit attendees (if few speak and the others listen or check email, the list is too large); the IC opens with a pre-planned agenda; kickoff agenda for a security incident: designate a note-taker; introductions (name, team, role); rules of engagement (confidentiality, OpSec, who decides, tell the lead when switching tasks); describe status; discuss; summarize actions and owners; ask about resources, other groups, and blockers; schedule the next sync and who attends (required and optional) (BSRS p.444-445). Ongoing sync agenda: note-taker; status summary (IC or OL); updates per thread; next steps; the OL assigns tasks and asks each person to repeat what they will do; schedule meetings; the OL updates the action tracker; the note-taker is essential (the notes serve the postmortem; consult legal if there are legal implications) (BSRS p.445).
- [from source] The right level of detail for the right people: those who know little fill in gaps (gap-filling), generating rumors; executives receive short updates (progress, blockers, consequences); the IR team needs up-to-date information; other employees (decide what to tell: help vs leak); customers (may have a legal right to notification within a deadline or receive it voluntarily); participants in the justice system (police may ask for information that was not meant to be shared) (BSRS p.445-446). Communications lead (CL): stays informed and prepares communications; works with sales and support, prepares briefs for executives, legal, and regulators, works with press and PR to avoid conflicting statements, watches the dissemination of information to respect need-to-know; may hire crisis communication consultants (BSRS p.446).

### Putting it together: a compromise example (BSRS p.446-450)
- [from source] Scenario: an engineer finds an unknown service account in an unknown cloud project; at noon escalates to security; triage assumes the worst case (compromised account) and declares the incident; the IC notifies the team: an incident has occurred, takes on the IC role, and needs support (BSRS p.446-447). Communications and OpSec: create a cloud environment not associated with the organization using a corporate card, freshly rebuilt laptops unconnected to management, and guide executives and legal to obtain a secure laptop and account; discuss in person in a nearby room (BSRS p.447). Start: a security engineer with forensic experience as OL; collect cloud logs for the period when the credentials were added; the account was used when the engineer was away, concluding compromise; investigation expanded to all activity around that time; without enough forensic analysts, sysadmins are brought in (briefing by phone, not email) to collect logs; at 5 p.m. it is realized the team will tire: four hours are allocated and a handover is prepared; at 9 p.m. the entry point is found: well-made phishing that led the engineer to run a command that downloads the backdoor (BSRS p.447-448).
- [from source] Handover to London: document findings within an hour, brief a senior IC in London, transfer ownership of the documentation, and confirm the team understood the next step; the London IC takes over until 9 a.m. PST; overnight they analyze the backdoor and the attacker's actions (BSRS p.448-449). Handback at 9 a.m.: the simple backdoor allowed remote login; the attacker used credentials saved in the shell history and added their own service account key to the list of administrative tokens; started using the service APIs directly, uploaded a machine image and launched dozens of VMs in a new project; an audit of all credentials confirmed that only the malicious service account and the known tokens were not verifiable; concise update to executives and legal; new high-priority task: the attacker's other actions on production machines (BSRS p.449).
- [from source] Communications and remediation in parallel: CL (a good technical writer) and RL (a sysadmin); the CL drafts with the lawyer a blog post with blanks to fill in later, ready and approved before the details are in; the RL lists each affected resource and cleanup proposals (change credentials even without confirmed password exposure; copy the engineer's important data and erase the home directory, recreating it on a reinstalled workstation); discovery: the VMs were a coin-mining fleet; options to erase or snapshot and archive for a possible report to authorities, and delete the project (BSRS p.449-450). Closing: with no more leads, remediation is carried out after checking with legal and leadership, notes for the postmortem, outbrief with the team, clear communication that the incident is closed, and a briefing of the London team (BSRS p.450).
- [from source] Conclusion: incident management at scale is an art distinct from project management; focus on processes, tools, and organizational structures; parallelizing response and forensics and managing with ICS/IMAG scales and is reliable (BSRS p.451).
