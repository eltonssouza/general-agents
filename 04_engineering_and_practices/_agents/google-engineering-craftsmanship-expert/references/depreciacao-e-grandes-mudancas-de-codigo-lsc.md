# Deprecation and Large-Scale Code Changes (SWE-G Ch. 15 and Ch. 22)

> Convention: pages = PDF (see `INDEX.md`). Source: `Software-Engineering-at-Google-completo.pdf`. Ch. 15 "Deprecation" (PDF p.222-230) and Ch. 22 "Large-Scale Changes" (PDF p.316-329); both by Hyrum Wright.

## 1. Ch. 15: deprecation
- [from source] (SWE-G p.222) Deprecation is the orderly migration away from obsolete systems and their final removal. Premise: "code is a liability, not an asset". Google admits it is still learning how to deprecate.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.223) Age does not justify deprecation; deprecation is for systems proven obsolete for which an equivalent replacement exists. The value is in the functionality, not in the code; having two systems for the same function generates costs and hinders the evolution of the new one. There is a limit to how much deprecation the organization can bear at the same time ("don't close all the streets to pave them").
- [from source] (SWE-G p.223-224) Why it is hard: Hyrum's Law (more users, more unexpected uses); the replacement is never identical; emotional attachment; political cost of funding the removal (the costs of doing nothing are hardly visible). Evolving a system in place is usually cheaper than replacing it (p.224, p.230).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.224-225) Deprecation in design: ask how easy it will be to migrate to a replacement and how to replace parts incrementally; "don't start projects that the organization is not committed to maintaining".
- [from source] (SWE-G p.225-226) Two kinds: advisory (no deadline, low priority; "hope is not a strategy") and compulsory (with a removal deadline and an enforcement mechanism). The book says that a deprecation warning tends to reduce new uses, but rarely leads to active migration; it recommends that compulsory deprecations be driven by a specialized team until the end (p.226), with the power to break non-compliant users after sufficient warnings. Discovery of hidden dependencies through temporary shutdowns of increasing duration (p.226).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.227) Deprecation warnings must be actionable and relevant; excess generates "alert fatigue". At Google, tools such as Error Prone and clang-tidy limit warnings to newly changed lines.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.227-229) Management: explicit owners of the process; incremental milestones; tools for discovery (Code Search, Kythe), migration (LSC) and prevention of "backsliding" (Tricorder with `@deprecated`; visibility lists in the build).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.229-230) TL;DR: ongoing maintenance costs must be weighed against the cost of removing; removing is usually harder than building; evolving in place is usually cheaper when the cost of shutting down is included; ecosystem costs are diffuse.
- Fidelity note: the v1 package attributed to p.226 the sentence that "the team that introduces the new library is responsible for funding and executing the migration of all clients in the monorepo" as a quote from the chapter. The text of p.226 deals with compulsory deprecation driven by a specialized team; the idea that infrastructure does the migration appears as the "Churn Rule" (SWE-G p.19) and as a reason for centralizing LSCs (SWE-G p.317). "Advisory deprecation usually fails" is a correct paraphrase of p.225-226, but the book does not mention a "30-day deadline".

## 2. Ch. 22: LSC
- [from source] (SWE-G p.316) An LSC is a set of logically related changes that cannot practically be submitted as a single atomic unit; at Google almost always generated by tools. The book notes that the largest possible atomic commit shrinks as the codebase and the number of engineers grow.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.316-317) LSC categories: cleaning up anti-patterns, replacing uses of deprecated features, infrastructure improvements (compiler upgrade) and migrating users to a new system. Most LSCs have almost zero functional impact; cases of hundreds of thousands and even millions of references.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.317) Who does it: infrastructure teams, because they have the domain knowledge, centralize the expertise and avoid "unfunded mandates"; organic migrations rarely succeed completely.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.318-320) Barriers to atomic changes: technical limits of the VCS, merge conflicts, "haunted graveyards" (countermeasure: tests), heterogeneity (LSCs only work when most of the effort is done by computers) and tests (CI also runs tests that transitively depend on the files; small changes make diagnosis easier).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.320) Note: it is common for 10% to 20% of a project's changes to be the result of LSCs. The TAP train (p.320-321): every three hours, a sample of 1,000 random tests per change, then an "uber-change", individual rerun of the failing tests, report; it can take more than six hours.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.321-322) Case `scoped_ptr` to `std::unique_ptr`: more than 500,000 references, a peak of more than 700 independent changes and more than 15,000 files per day; completed with an alias and textual replacement.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.322-323) Culture and policies: a lightweight approval process supervised by a group of experienced engineers; local owners have no veto over the LSC. Tool input: Kythe, ClangMR, JavacFlume and Refaster; rule of thumb that, above about 500 edits, it is worth learning the change-generation tool instead of editing manually (p.323).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.323) "Rosie" is the tool that splits ("shards") a master change into smaller pieces that can be tested, sent for review and submitted independently.
- [from source] (SWE-G p.324) Language support: type aliasing and forwarding functions help; statically typed languages make it easier than dynamic ones; automatic formatters are a crucial part (changes should look like they were written by humans). Operation RoseHub case (p.324).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.324-328) Four-phase process: authorization, change creation, shard management and cleanup. Authorization (p.325): a document with the reason and estimated impact, domain review, a supervision list of about a dozen people, generally liberal, typically with a "global approver". Rosie splits by project boundaries and ownership rules and runs an independent test-send-commit pipeline (p.326), limits pending shards and runs at low priority; "cattle versus pets" analogy (p.326). Review by pattern (p.327).
- [from source] (SWE-G p.328) Cleanup: prevent reintroduction (Tricorder flags new uses in review). TL;DR: an LSC process lets you rethink the immutability of certain decisions; traditional refactoring models break at large scale; "doing LSCs is building the habit of doing LSCs".

## 3. What is NOT in these chapters
- Rosie as "sending atomic CLs per team and merging asynchronously" is an approximate description; the book says it splits by project/ownership, tests, sends for review and submits each shard independently (p.326).
- A "central approval committee" exists (p.322, p.325), but the book says it usually approves liberally.
- The 5-step diagram of the v1 package (Proposal, Automation, Partitioning, Review, Merge) was a reinterpretation; the book's phases are the four listed above (p.324-325).
- "Phases 1-3" of a deprecation procedure (blocking new uses, communication, physical removal) is an [extrapolation] consistent with the backsliding tools (p.229), but is not a sequence declared in the book.
