## Ch. 19: approximate inference
- `[from source]` (DLB p.648) Exact inference can be seen as **optimization**; the **ELBO** (*evidence lower bound*, also "negative variational free energy") is L(v,θ,q) = log p(v;θ) − D_KL(q(h|v)‖p(h|v;θ)) (eq. 19.1); since the KL is non-negative, L ≤ log p(v), with equality iff q = p(h|v). `[extrapolation]` The following derivations (EM, mean field) were **not read**.

## Ch. 20: deep generative models
- `[from source]` (DLB p.671-672) **RBM** (invented under the name *harmonium*, Smolensky 1986): bipartite graph with one visible layer and one latent layer, no connections within each layer; the Boltzmann learning rule is "local" (Hebb: "fire together, wire together", p.671); the negative phase is harder to explain biologically.
- `[from source]` (DLB p.711-713) **Variational autoencoder (VAE)** (Kingma 2013; Rezende 2014): directed model with learned approximate inference, trainable by gradient alone. It maximizes the variational bound L(q) = E_{z∼q(z|x)} log p_model(z,x) + H(q(z|x)) = E log p_model(x|z) − D_KL(q(z|x)‖p_model(z)) ≤ log p_model(x) (eq. 20.76-20.78): a reconstruction term and a term that pulls the posterior toward the *prior*; the encoder network produces the parameters of q and backpropagation goes through the samples of continuous z (p.712). Limitations pointed out: image samples tend to be **blurry** (cause unknown; possibly an effect of MLE and a Gaussian p_model); they use only a small subset of the dimensions of z (p.713). Extensions: DRAW (RNN with attention), recurrent VAEs and the *importance weighted autoencoder* L_k (eq. 20.79, p.713). Fig. 20.6 shows learned 2-D manifolds (rotation and expression of faces, p.715).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.714-716) **GAN** (Goodfellow 2014c): a generator x = g(z;θ⁽ᵍ⁾) and a discriminator d(x;θ⁽ᵈ⁾) in a **zero-sum game**: g* = argmin_g max_d v(g,d) (eq. 20.80), with v = E_{x∼p_data} log d(x) + E_{x∼p_model} log(1 − d(x)) (20.81); at equilibrium d = ½ everywhere; it requires neither approximate inference nor the gradient of Z (p.714-715); convergence is only guaranteed when max_d v(g,d) is convex in θ⁽ᵍ⁾, and is difficult in practice with neural networks (p.715).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.716-718) Simultaneous gradient descent on two costs **does not guarantee equilibrium** (example v(a,b) = ab: a stable circular orbit instead of converging to the origin; the equilibria of the minimax are saddles of v); it is not known how much this affects GANs (p.716). The "non-zero-sum" formulation most effective in practice is motivated by a heuristic (the generator tries to increase the probability that the discriminator is wrong); **stabilizing** GANs is "an open problem" (p.716). **DCGAN** (Radford 2015) is cited as a well-performing deep convolutional network for image synthesis (p.716); **LAPGAN** (Denton 2015) uses conditional GANs in a Laplacian pyramid and fooled human subjects on up to 40% of the outputs (p.717). A GAN can fit distributions with zero probability at the training points (−∞ likelihood at test but a plausible manifold) (p.717); dropout seems important in the discriminator (p.718).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.718-719) **Generative moment matching networks** (Li 2015; Dziugaite 2015) train the generator by *moment matching* with the **MMD** (*maximum mean discrepancy*) cost, without an auxiliary network; GMMN samples are visually disappointing, improved by generating codes of an autoencoder; the cost requires batches of examples (p.719). **Convolutional generators** use the transpose of the convolution and obtain more realistic images with fewer parameters (p.719).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.731-732) "Diffusion inversion" (Sohl-Dickstein 2015) trains a transition operator that undoes one noise step and removes spurious modes `[DATED]`: it is **not** the "diffusion" of current image models, which the book does not know. ABC (Rubin 1984).
- `[from source]` (DLB p.732-734) **Evaluating generative models** is subtle: a stochastic log-likelihood estimate vs a deterministic lower bound are not comparable without knowing how loose the bound is; a cheap AIS can underestimate Z and **overestimate** log p(x); preprocessing must be identical (multiplying the input by 0.1 artificially increases the likelihood by a factor of 10); on MNIST do not mix real-valued and binary models or different binarization schemes (p.733); practitioners also inspect samples visually (p.734).
