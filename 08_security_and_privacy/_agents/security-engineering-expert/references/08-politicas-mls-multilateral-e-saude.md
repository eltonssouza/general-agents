# 08. Security policy models: MLS, multilateral, health and Chinese Wall (Chs. 9 and 10)

Convention: "SE3 p.N" = PDF page; printed = PDF − 44 (ch. 9) and − 43 (ch. 10). Reading: ch. 9 in full; ch. 10 p.384-409 and 412-416 in full (p.409-411 only excerpts). Health cases (NHS, HES, DeepMind, TPP) are from 2020: `[DATED]`.

## 1. What a "security policy model" is
- `[from source]` Top-down approach: **threat model → security policy → mechanisms**; the policy (often neglected) is the document that says clearly and concisely what the mechanisms must achieve (SE3 p.360).
- `[from source]` Empty corporate "policy" ("approved by management", "everyone will comply", "need-to-know") is useless: it does not say who determines need-to-know, mixes levels, leaves the mechanism implicit and does not say who detects/reports violations (SE3 p.360-361).
- `[from source]` *Security policy model* = succinct statement of protection properties (one page or less), agreed with the community or the client's management and the basis for formal analysis; *security target* = detailed description of the mechanisms; *protection profile* = implementation-independent target (SE3 p.361).

## 2. MLS and Bell-LaPadula (SE3 p.362-367)
- Labels Unclassified < Confidential < Secret < Top Secret; clearance ≥ classification to read; information only flows upward, except by deliberate declassification; codewords/compartments; TS/SCI (SE3 p.362-363).
- **Anderson report (1972):** a secure system should do one or two things well; mechanisms simple enough to verify and that change rarely; *reference monitor*; **TCB** = components (hardware, software, humans) whose failure can cause a policy violation (SE3 p.364).
- **Bell-LaPadula:** *simple security property* (no read up) and *\*-property* (no write down); motivated by Trojan horses; MAC protects independently of the actions of the user and their programs (SE3 p.364-365).
- **Criticisms:** System Z and *tranquility* (strong/weak); *high water mark* (a process accumulates labels; labels explode); applications need to be rewritten; revocation in the middle of transactions; "trusted subjects" end up in the TCB and are not verifiable by BLP alone; BLP does not deal with creation/destruction of subjects/objects (SE3 p.365-367).
- **Evolution:** noninterference, nondeducibility (too weak), *type enforcement/DTE* (used in SELinux; "trusted pipelines": guards and firewalls only break if ≥ 2 stages are compromised; drawback: state explosion), RBAC (1992) and ABAC (SE3 p.367-369). **Biba** (integrity as the dual: no read down, no write up; *low water mark*); Windows Vista+ uses Low/Medium/High/System levels with NoWriteUp by default (SE3 p.369-370).
- **What goes wrong:** composition of secure systems fails with feedback; *cascade problem* (span limits); *covert channels* (DoD target ≈ 1 bit/s; dangerous for keys → crypto in dedicated hardware); malware (an 8-hour virus penetrated "MLS" in 1983); polyinstantiation (US: cover story; UK: "classified"); practical costs: label explosion, TCB bloat, overclassification, obsessive secrecy (SE3 p.375-381). Today most operate *system high* + guards/data diodes (MILS) (SE3 p.381).
- Lesson of method: "be a *fox*, not a *hedgehog*" — MLS casts every problem as one big problem (SE3 p.381-382).

## 3. Multilateral security (Ch. 10)
- **Lateral flows**, not only hierarchical ones: organizational, by relationship, mixed or volumetric; centralizing sensitive systems creates a more valuable asset and gives access to more people ("the benefits of networks scale more than linearly; so do the harms") (SE3 p.384-386).
- **Compartmentation and lattice:** 2^N compartments with N codewords; BLP works practically the same; a lattice isolates but does not solve combination and downgrade (SE3 p.387-388). Vault 7 (CIA): cyberweapons without compartmentation, shared sysadmin passwords, no monitoring; JWICS without 2FA (SE3 p.389).
- **Wildlife (Wildbook):** abstracted data can be *more* sensitive; four access levels; insider control through "two-dimensional transparency" and local auditability (SE3 p.389-392).
- **Health (BMA, 1995):** a single lifelong EPR is infeasible (60+ rules); MLS proposal (DST Secret...) infeasible because "sensitivity is the patient's decision" (SE3 p.397). **BMA's nine principles** (SE3 p.398): (1) ACL per record; (2) record opening; (3) control (one responsible clinician alters the ACL); (4) consent and notification; (5) persistence; (6) attribution (every access marked, audit trail of deletions); (7) information flow (information derived from A only goes to B if B's ACL is contained in A's); (8) aggregation control; (9) TCB assessed by independent experts. It is strictly more expressive than BLP (has state; more compartments than patients) (SE3 p.399).
- **Hospital practice:** rules such as "ward nurse sees patients of the last 90 days"; BMA = lossless compression; RBAC = lossy but works (SE3 p.399); do not make a critical system depend on a flaky system (smartcards) (SE3 p.399). NPfIT (~£10bn): "RBAC + legitimate relationship" gave the receptionist access to psychiatric notes; "a doctrine of one record with one security context per patient was a bad idea" (SE3 p.400-401). Fictitious opt-outs, widely accessible emergency systems (SE3 p.401-402). Secondary-use scandals (HES sold to >1,000 organizations; DeepMind/Royal Free; TPP) (SE3 p.402-405) `[DATED]`.
- **Consent, security by default and transparency** (medConfidential): single opt-out; secure by default (it is not the patient's job to defend their data); real transparency (SE3 p.406-407). Positive consent gets 70-80% (Biobank); take queries to the live data and take out only statistics (OpenSAFELY) (SE3 p.407-408).
- **Chinese Wall (Brewer-Nash; SE3 p.412):** separation of duties + stateful MAC; simple security: s accesses c iff, for every c' that s can read, y(c) ∉ x(c') or y(c) = y(c'); *\*-property*: s can write to c only if it cannot read c' with x(c') ≠ ∅ and y(c) ≠ y(c'); in practice manual (sanitized CVs), with a *cooling-off* period (SE3 p.413-415).
- **Chapter lesson:** multilateral security requires the "fox" approach: understand the application in detail, know what has gone wrong and think like an adversary (SE3 p.415).
