# 07. Security economics and incentives (Ch. 8)

Convention: "SE3 p.N" = PDF page; printed = PDF − 44 (ch. 8). Reading: p.319-355 in full; p.356-358 only quickly. Market data and 2020 cases are `[DATED]`.

## 1. The central thesis
- `[from source]` Around 2000 it was realized that many security failures come from wrong incentives: "if the party who guards the system is not the party who suffers when it fails, expect trouble"; security mechanisms are often designed to **shift liability** (SE3 p.319). Security is often **a power relationship** (SE3 p.338). Network insecurity is like pollution: a negative externality (SE3 p.320).
- `[from source]` The "perversely motivated guards": police who discourage reporting of cybercrime; banks and money laundering (formalized due diligence); payment providers who hope to learn from third parties because finding a fraud pattern would make them bear the loss; click fraud (the more they fight it, the less revenue); finding bugs in your own code delays the product; security policy captured by the Big Four, with a focus on "change your password every month" (SE3 p.348-349).

## 2. Information markets and lock-in (SE3 p.321-328)
- Marginal cost ≈ 0 → free/ad-funded; network externalities (value grows more than linearly); two-sided markets; supply-side economies of scale; lock-in through switching costs (dominated by training people); the four together lead to dominant firms (SE3 p.325-326).
- **Value of lock-in** (Shapiro-Varian): the value of a software company = total lock-in of its customers; example: 100 employees × US$150 = US$15,000; if the switching cost is lower, customers switch; if higher, the vendor raises the price (SE3 p.326-327).
- Security mechanisms that control compatibility have as "attackers" the owners of the equipment or competitors, not malicious outsiders; locking down too much hurts the business (open IBM PC vs Mac) (SE3 p.327).
- **Asymmetric information:** *market for lemons* (50 good cars at US$2000 and 50 lemons at US$1000 → price tends to ~US$1000); bad security products dominate when the buyer cannot tell the difference; hidden information (*adverse selection*) vs hidden action (*moral hazard*); cyber insurance is expensive because those who cut corners buy more; stakeholders exaggerate or downplay threats (SE3 p.328-329). **Public goods**: security has public-good aspects; vulnerabilities sold to cyberweapons makers → governments stockpile → industry pays the collateral damage (SE3 p.329-330).

## 3. Game theory (SE3 p.330-334)
- Dominant strategy and Nash equilibrium (SE3 p.330-331). **Prisoner's dilemma:** (deny,deny) is Pareto-efficient and the dominant-strategy equilibrium is not; it only changes by changing the game (laws, treaties, omertà) (SE3 p.331-332). Repeated games and *tit-for-tat*; with noise, forgive (SE3 p.333). *Hawk-dove*: proportion of hawks p = v/c when c > v (SE3 p.333). Online reputation turns interactions into iterated games (SE3 p.334). Tacit collusion without an illegal agreement (ISPs, airlines) (SE3 p.334).
- **Auctions:** English, Dutch, first-price, Vickrey (honest bid is optimal), all-pay; failures: bidding rings, entry detection/deterrence, risk aversion, signaling, budget constraints (SE3 p.335-336). Ad auction: ad rank = bid × quality; optimizing revenue favors extreme content (SE3 p.336-337).

## 4. Economics of security and dependability (SE3 p.337-354)
- **"Why is Windows so insecure?"** In a platform race, "ship it Tuesday and get it right by version 3" is rational; security comes later and serves lock-in; SSL/TLS left it to the user to judge the certificate (SET would have cost the banks); "bargains then rip-offs" = "poor security, then security for someone else" (SE3 p.338-340). Network effects for bads: malware targets the dominant platform (SE3 p.340).
- **Patch cycle:** simple models (i.i.d. bugs) show that dependability is a function of initial quality and total testing time; software can be "like wine" (OpenBSD) (SE3 p.340-341). *Responsible/coordinated disclosure*, markets (iDefense, Zerodium; US$1m+ for iOS), bug bounties; deliberate contribution of vulnerable code to open source; open source turns into an exploit faster (SE3 p.341-342).
- **Structural models** (Hirshleifer, Varian): *weakest-link* (most careless programmer), *best-shot* (champion architect), *sum-of-efforts* (testers): sum-of-efforts > best-shot > weakest-link in efficiency; large companies hire more testers and fewer better programmers; defenses with only a private benefit → "low-hanging fruit" (SE3 p.342-343). An agency with both an offensive and a defensive mission tends to stockpile vulnerabilities (SE3 p.344).
- **Lock-in, tying and DRM:** format wars; adversarial interoperability; IRM/Trusted Computing increased switching cost; "fighting a nonexistent threat, the music industry let the computer industry eat its lunch" (SE3 p.344-345). Antitrust, moats, Sales of Goods Directive 2019/771 (SE3 p.346-348) `[DATED]`.
- **Privacy:** privacy paradox; experiment by John et al. (privacy assurances led to disclosing *less*; the "gamer" disclosed 2x) (SE3 p.349-350); industry makes privacy risks less salient; Pew 2015: 93% think it is important to control who accesses their information, 6% "very confident" in the agencies (SE3 p.350-351) `[DATED]`.
- **Organizations:** complacency → crisis → panic → "the bad guys move on to the next target" (SE3 p.351); competition matters more than public/private ownership (SE3 p.352).
- **Economics of cybercrime:** Becker (rewards/punishments); situational prevention; minimization; aim at the *choke point*; trajectory gamer → cheats → malware; NCA ads; 2019: ~1 million UK households with burglary/car theft vs >2 million with online fraud; breach disclosure: one breach causes a small share-price drop for ~1 week, two breaches shake confidence for longer (SE3 p.352-354). Measurement is hard because those who publish have an incentive to exaggerate losses (SE3 p.354).

## How to use in design reasoning
`[inference]` Before proposing a control, ask: who pays the cost of failure? who decides? who benefits from not seeing the problem? (SE3 p.319, 338, 348). For prioritization, see also `references/15-desenvolvimento-seguro-e-gestao-de-risco.md`.
