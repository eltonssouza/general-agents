## Ch. 18 — Recovery and aftermath (BSRS p.453-478; Part V p.479)

### Premise (BSRS p.453-454)
- [from source] What differs in recovering from security incidents is the attacker: persistent, can keep access or return at any time, even during recovery; those who recover are usually those who build and operate the systems, not security professionals; the chapter shows how to keep the attacker out during recovery and discusses tradeoffs such as stopping the attacker's activity vs leaving them in place to learn more (BSRS p.453). Reference cited: NIST SP 800-184 (guide for cybersecurity event recovery) emphasizes checklists and testing, but applying its rigor in the middle of a crisis can be difficult (BSRS p.454, note).
- [from source] Well-designed systems (Chs. 8 and 9) are resilient and recoverable; recovery requires integration with crisis management tactics, balancing continuing to hold off the attacker with restoring assets to a known good (potentially improved) state; security specialists are called in for forensics, vulnerability triage and subtle decisions, but restoring requires the expertise of those who operate the system; recovering from attacks involves a more ambiguous environment than pre-planned playbooks; the attacker may change behavior mid-attack; recovery also serves to drive posture improvements (BSRS p.454).

### Recovery logistics (BSRS p.454-456)
- [from source] Whoever does the recovery should be different from whoever investigates: the investigation is long and detailed, recovery can begin with the investigation still ongoing, the skills differ; formal team (from one individual to the whole organization), with coordination mechanisms (formal teams, frequent meetings, shared documentation repositories, peer reviews); many organizations use sprints, Scrum and short feedback loops (BSRS p.454-455). Roles apply at any size (internal team or outsourcing, such as hiring a contractor to rebuild a system); well-organized recovery looks like a choreographed ballet: clearly define the roles of preparing, reviewing and executing and communicate frequently face to face (BSRS p.455).
- [from source] The IC and OL name a remediation lead (RL) to plan the recovery; the RL coordinates the recovery checklist with the IC, assembles the team with the relevant expertise; at Google, those who recover are the teams that build and operate the systems (SREs, developers, sysadmins, helpdesk, security specialists for routine processes) (BSRS p.456). Information management: raw incident trails, notes, checklists, new operational documentation and attack information must be available to the team and inaccessible to the attacker (e.g., air-gapped computer); tools outside the maximum scope of the compromise; start with cards and add an independent provider when the team's machines are safe; resources everyone updates in real time; keep reliable recovery notes for audit (note takers or documentation specialists; Google uses technical writers) (BSRS p.456).
- [from source] Timeline: the best time to start varies (critical infrastructure can recover almost immediately, as in DoS; attacker with full control: plan now, execute only upon understanding what they did); the processes apply during and after the investigation; when recovery starts, the investigation has already started a postmortem document (raw notes) that the recovery team updates; do not start without some plan and create checklists beforehand; post-recovery actions should begin soon after the end so as not to forget details or postpone medium and long-term fixes (BSRS p.456-457).

### Planning the recovery (BSRS p.457-464)
- [from source] Goal: mitigate the attack and return systems to normal routine by applying improvements; complex incidents require parallelizing and structuring teams (BSRS p.457). Scope: varies with the attack (ransomware on one machine: reinstall; a state actor with presence across the network and exfiltrated data requires multiple strategies and skills); effort may not be proportional to severity or sophistication (an organization unprepared for simple ransomware may have many compromised machines) (BSRS p.457-458). Needs a complete list of affected systems, networks and data and of the attacker's TTPs to identify related resources (if the configuration distribution system was compromised, those that received configurations from it may be in scope); the IC assigns someone to maintain action items in a mitigation doc for the formal postmortem; classify actions into short-term mitigations (e.g., applying patches) or long-term strategic changes (e.g., changing build processes to prevent vulnerable libraries); examine each affected asset against the attacker's behavior; list of attacker behaviors and possible defenses as in Ch.2 (Table 2-3) as operational documentation; short communication loop between postmortem notes, investigation and IC; without efficient information exchange, attackers bypass mitigations; consider that the attacker is still present and watching (BSRS p.458).
- [from source] Considerations: (1) how will the attacker respond? Ejecting requires near-perfect knowledge of the attack and a solid plan; example of six compromised systems rebuilt without knowing how the attack began: the attacker still active on other systems sees the removal and can destroy the rest; attackers also spy on email, bug trackers, code changes and calendars; use systems invisible to the attacker (new messaging system and new machines, for example cheap Chromebooks); there is a human on the other side reacting; consensus: wait for complete understanding before ejecting, but if the attacker is already doing something dangerous (taking sensitive data or destroying systems) you can act earlier ("chess game": know the steps to checkmate); integrate tightly with the investigation to avoid re-entry (BSRS p.459-460).
- [from source] (2) Are the recovery infrastructure or tools compromised? Ask the investigation; example: laptops and configuration server compromised: remediate the server before rebuilding the laptops; backup restoration tool with malicious code: restore the code before recovering data; recovering an asset in infrastructure under the attacker's control leads to recompromise; pattern: a "clean" or "safe" version of the asset, isolated (clean network or new system, possibly replicating the whole infrastructure or key parts; e.g., quarantined network and configuration server with a fresh OS, manually configured to bootstrap new machines without attacker-controlled configurations) (BSRS p.460-461).
- [from source] (3) What variants of the attack exist? Example: buffer overflow exploited on a web server with 20 other servers running the same software: investigate whether the others were compromised and how to mitigate the effect of the vulnerability for all; repeated risks (ship anchor severing a submarine cable: analyze the risk category and plan capacity); look for related vulnerabilities (class or the same flaw in other software), especially in custom code or shared libraries (fuzzing, Ch.13); in open source or commercial software check available patches and include broad upgrades in the recovery (BSRS p.461).
- [from source] (4) Will the recovery reintroduce attack vectors? Restoring images, code repositories or configurations can reintroduce vulnerable software ("golden images" from full snapshots: update them and delete compromised snapshots before systems return); if the attacker modified the recovery infrastructure (configurations in a repository), restoring them persists their changes, requiring going far back in time; think about the attack timeline; if it is not possible to determine when they modified, it may be necessary to rebuild large parts from scratch in parallel; traditional backups (tape) may contain the attacker's modifications: destroy or quarantine those that hold evidence for analysis (BSRS p.462).
- [from source] (5) What are the mitigation options? Distributed systems allow fixing modules in place; in the cloud, tear down and replace compromised containers or VMs; depending on the assets (machines, printers, cameras, data, accounts) less-than-ideal options remain with short-term technical debt to eject the attacker for good (e.g., a manual deny rule on the router, without peer review or version control, so the attacker does not see the change; disable automatic rule pushes until the new rule enters the canonical configuration and set a reminder to re-enable); questions when accepting technical debt: when and how fast can it be removed; does the organization commit to maintain and pay for it; will it affect uptime and error budget; how will people recognize the mitigation as short-term (mark as technical debt, comments and commit messages); how will a future engineer with no knowledge of the incident prove it can be removed; how effective will it be if it stays too long (example of the compromised database isolated on its own network during a migration that takes six months instead of two weeks, with risk of accidental reconnection); did a domain specialist identify gaps in the responses? (BSRS p.462-464).

### Recovery checklists (BSRS p.464-465)
- [from source] With the scope defined, list options and tradeoffs; they form the basis of one or more checklists; every recovery should use routine and tested practices; documenting and sharing steps eases collaboration, shows what to parallelize and coordinates the work; each item maps to a task and the skills needed, and people claim tasks by skill, giving the IC confidence that the items marked were done; the checklist should contain details (tools, commands) and provide for cleanup and rollback procedures in case the plan fails (BSRS p.464). Template (Figure 18-1, read visually from the image on p.465): instructions block with color coding (green = completed, orange = to do, red = task that cannot be completed and must be replaced with an equivalent action, blue = task that will not be completed because it is operationally risky and needs adjustments); incident commander section (give the "hands off keyboards" briefing, present an overview of tasks and constraints, tell everyone the exact time remediation will begin); ordered list in which the remediation lead starts execution: [team name] task description, with detailed commands or steps (BSRS p.465). The "hands off keyboard" briefing requires the audience's full attention (BSRS p.464, note).

### Starting the recovery (BSRS p.465-471)
- [from source] Goal of mitigation and recovery: eject the attacker, ensure they do not return and make the system more secure (BSRS p.465). Isolation (quarantine): common technique (antivirus that moves a binary to a quarantine folder; isolate a host by switch port or on the machine itself; entire networks by segmentation, as in DoS responses) (BSRS p.465). BeyondCorp (zero trust) amplifies isolation: assets are trusted only after validating security posture, little trust between assets, natural isolation against lateral movement, access via proxy and strong credentials revocable at any time (BSRS p.466). Isolating also serves to keep compromised infrastructure running (critical database whose rebuild takes weeks: own network with traffic restrictions); alert: leaving compromised assets online is an insidious form of technical debt (only copy of the data, difficulty replacing, forgetting; in the worst case someone new removes the quarantine); mark compromised assets (visible stickers; updated list of quarantined MAC addresses with network monitoring); the checklist and the postmortem should cover whether quarantined assets were remediated safely and permanently (BSRS p.466).
- [from source] System rebuilds and software upgrades: wipe the malware and keep running, or reinstall? If the attacker installed several types of malware and you do not know all of them, the chance of a complete cleanup is lost; typically reinstalling from scratch with images and software known to be good is the best solution (may require reinstalling the BIOS, reformatting the disk or replacing hardware, as with UEFI/LoJax rootkits); with reliable design principles, rebuild is simple (Ch.9); examples: hardware-verified boot in a chain of trust (Chromebooks): just reboot; automated release systems such as Rapid; containers and instant releases in the cloud; if there is no version control or standard images, introduce them in short-term recovery (open source options such as Bazel, Chef, Helm), accepting an approximate initial configuration and technical debt to pay down (BSRS p.466-467).
- [from source] Data sanitization: confirm the attacker did not tamper with source code, binaries, images and configurations nor the systems that build, manage and release them; obtain known-good copies from the origin (vendors), from backups or from uncompromised VCS and compare checksums; if the copies are on compromised infrastructure, have high confidence about when the tampering began; binary provenance (Ch.14) eases this (example of glibc with malicious code in the build system: identify binaries built in the risk window, where they were deployed and dependencies; clearly mark the compromised components; create tests that prevent reintroducing vulnerable code); verify application data (database records); backups with strong cryptographic integrity (Ch.9) give confidence; reconciling changes may require special tools (partial restore with simultaneous integrity), ideally built and tested beforehand (BSRS p.467-468).
- [from source] Recovery data: rollback, restore, backup, hermetic rebuild and transaction replay tools have parameters that trade speed for data safety; do not change the defaults unless the tools are regularly tested on realistic production loads (tests, staging and mocks do not realistically simulate cache warming or the stabilization of balancing estimators); tune between incidents; review data-loss monitoring logs: they may reveal an inflection point that is a lower bound regardless of how many backups are skipped; restoring in place a backup that is not old enough can reactivate compromises; if the oldest safe backup has already been overwritten, only data remediation remains; restored backups may have desirable modified data mixed with corrupted data (malicious or random); reconstruction tools usually focus on random or malicious damage, not both; use routine integrity checks (known cryptographic signatures); redundancy and testing against random events (BSRS p.468-469).
- [from source] Rotation of credentials and secrets: attackers hijack existing accounts (password phishing, pass-the-hash to reuse NTLM/LanMan hashes and gain admin access); rotate system, user, service and application credentials (e.g., SSH keys, compromised authorized_keys in Ch.9); network devices, out-of-band management and SaaS may be in scope (BSRS p.469). Other secrets: data-at-rest encryption keys, SSL keys (if the frontend was compromised, rotate to avoid MITM); record encryption key on the compromised database server: rotate and re-encrypt; application communication keys; cloud service API keys stored in source code or local files are a common vulnerability (study of secret leaks in public GitHub repositories): determine whether they were accessible and rotate conservatively and frequently (BSRS p.470). Reset order in the checklist: administrator credentials, known-compromised accounts and accounts with access to sensitive resources; if there are many users, it can be a single disruptive event for everyone; SSO and centralized authentication reduce the impact, but introducing them during recovery can be time-consuming and expensive (BSRS p.470). 2FA via cloud identity services is a "quick win", mainly on email; it reveals the attacker's login attempts as login failures; long-term roadmap: FIDO keys (Ch.7) (BSRS p.471). Trap: centralized systems (LDAP, Active Directory) keep password history (salted hashes) to prevent reuse; an attacker with the hashes who manages to crack them infers patterns (password2019, password2020...), dangerous when password change is part of remediation; the safest way to store passwords is a salted key derivation function made for the purpose (scrypt, Argon2) and hashes encrypted at rest (note); consult security specialists, threat modeling and consider 2FA (BSRS p.471).

### After the recovery (BSRS p.471-473)
- [from source] Transitioning out of the incident and considering long-term impacts; smaller incidents have simple recovery; in the aftermath of Operation Aurora (2009) Google made systematic changes (some overnight; others, like BeyondCorp and work with the FIDO alliance on 2FA with security keys, took longer); the postmortem should distinguish short and long term (BSRS p.471-472).
- [from source] Postmortems: teams keep notes to integrate into the official postmortem; every postmortem should have a list of action items for the underlying problems; a good postmortem covers both the technological issues exploited by the attacker and opportunities to improve incident handling; document deadlines and efforts and decide what is short or long term; blameless postmortems in the SRE book; security questions: main contributing factors and variants elsewhere; what tests or audits should have detected it earlier and can be created; was the incident detected by an expected technological control (such as IDS) and, if not, how to improve; how fast was it detected and responded to and is that within an acceptable range; was important data protected enough to deter; did the team use the tools well (versioning, deploy, testing, backup, restore); which normal procedures were bypassed and what remediation to apply now; temporary infrastructure changes to refactor; bugs opened during the incident and recovery; industry and peer best practices that would have helped (BSRS p.472-473).
- [from source] Action items with explicit owners, ordered into short and long term; short term: "low-hanging fruit" (adding 2FA, reducing the time to apply patches, creating a vulnerability discovery program); long term: more fundamental (creating a dedicated security team, backbone encryption, changing operating system choices) integrated into the security posture strategy; the routine period after an incident is also the one that precedes the next: an opportunity to learn and prepare (BSRS p.473).

### Examples (BSRS p.473-478)
- [from source] Compromised cloud instances: a web package with a common vulnerability on cloud VMs exploited by an opportunistic attacker with scanners, who uses the VMs to launch new attacks; the RL, with the investigation notes, decides to fix the software, redeploy VMs and shut down the compromised instances, and that short-term mitigation must discover and address related vulnerabilities; checklist (Figure 18-2, read visually on p.474): (1) security specialists (or security-focused SREs/developers): set up vulnerability scanning, with details about other parts of the software on the instances; (2) web development team: fix the vulnerable software (source, versions and commands); (3) SRE team: deploy new cloud instances in parallel with the compromised ones, with the software updated by default, tested and validated before serving traffic; (4) SRE team: disable the compromised instances, archiving them permanently or erasing them so they are not reused; the postmortem identifies the need for a formal vulnerability management program, which goes into the long-term strategy (BSRS p.474-475).
- [from source] Large-scale phishing: a seven-day password campaign against an organization without 2FA, 70% of employees fell for it, the attacker reads emails and leaks information to the press; complexities: the attacker has only used email so far; VPN and cloud have independent authentications but there is password reuse; the attacker threatens new actions; it is necessary to balance ejecting quickly (removing email access) and preventing access to other systems; checklist (Figure 18-3, read visually on p.476): (1) sysadmins/SREs: implement 2FA infrastructure for email, with details on how to enroll all employees; (2) email SREs: lock email accounts until they are reset and use 2FA, possibly locking all at once instead of a rolling window given the ongoing activity; (3) IT team (e.g., Helpdesk): adopt 2FA and password resets, helping users change email, VPN and cloud passwords, possibly in an all-hands meeting; (4) sysadmins/SREs: adopt 2FA for VPN and cloud systems; the postmortem points to: broader 2FA on critical systems, SSO, phishing education and an IT security specialist, in the long-term strategy (BSRS p.475-476).
- [from source] Targeted attack with complex recovery: the attacker has had access to the systems for over a month, stole the SSL keys, inserted a backdoor in the code to divert US$ 0.02 from each transaction and monitors executives' emails; complexities: the investigation does not know how it started nor how access continues; the attacker sees executives and the response team, can alter and deploy code, has access to the SSL key infrastructure, customer communications potentially compromised, diverted money; focus: parallelize the recovery with one checklist per problem: email team (cut access and prevent re-entry, password resets, 2FA), source code team (cut access, sanitize files, redeploy clean code, know when the attacker changed the code and whether there are safe versions, ensure the VCS history was not altered), SSL keys team (deploy new keys securely, know how they were obtained), customer data team (what information the attacker may have accessed, password and session resets, connection with support and legal), reconciliation team (the US$ 0.02 per transaction: database records, finance and legal); less-than-ideal choices (cutting email: shut down the system, lock all accounts or create a new system in parallel; each alerts the attacker and may not resolve how they got in); investigation and recovery work together (BSRS p.477-478).
- [from source] Conclusion: recovery is a distinct and crucial step that requires a dedicated team of domain experts; consider how the attacker will react, who does what and when, what to tell users; parallelized, planned and synchronized with the IC and the investigation; the goal is to eject the attacker, remediate the compromise and improve security and reliability; every compromise should generate long-term lessons (BSRS p.478).

### What the book does NOT cover in this chapter
- It provides no formal postmortem model in document format (only questions and action items); it does not detail negotiation with extortionists, insurance nor Active Directory restoration; it does not address regulatory notification (it only cites communication to users).
