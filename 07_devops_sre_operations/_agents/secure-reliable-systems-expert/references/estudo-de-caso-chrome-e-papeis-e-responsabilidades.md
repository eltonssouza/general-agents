## Ch. 19 — Case study: the Chrome security team (BSRS p.481-489)

### Context and evolution of the team (BSRS p.481-484)
- [from source] Chrome was one of the first Google products to have its own security organization; challenges: modern browsers have the complexity of an operating system and a large share of the functionality is security-critical; client software and Windows were different from Google's portfolio, with little expertise transferable from the central team; the project would be predominantly open source, unable to depend on corporate security practices (BSRS p.481). The browser launched in 2008 and the project started in 2006 (BSRS p.481-482).
- [from source] Four stages: Team v0.1 (no formal security team at launch; expertise distributed across engineering plus consulting from the central team and from vendors; several critical buffer overflows discovered in the first two weeks, a pattern of developers under time pressure with optimized C++); Team v1.0 (about a year later, a dedicated team with engineers from the central team and new hires); Team v2.0 (2010: Vulnerability Reward Program, huge response; WebKit had not had much security scrutiny; the security team started diving into the code and fixing many bugs, becoming a hybrid engineering team of security specialists rather than isolated consultants); Team v3.0 (2012: security principles published; 2013: engineering manager and more dedicated engineers, an offsite that defined the mission: provide Chrome users with the safest possible platform and advance web security) (BSRS p.482-483).
- [from source] Perennial focus areas (from a brainstorm with Post-its): security reviews (consulting and review of sensitive designs and changes; shared responsibility; scaling through documentation, training, and owners of critical parts), bug finding and fixing (millions of critical lines, hundreds of developers), architecture and exploit mitigation (minimize the impact of any bug; OS-specific investments), usable security (user decisions), and web platform security (security for web app developers) (BSRS p.483-484). Leads responsible per area, dedicated managers, and agility, information sharing, and swarming to avoid silos (BSRS p.484).
- [from source] People: finding and retaining people who care about the mission and collaborate well is critical; everyone contributes to hiring, interviews, and growth feedback; personal networks, intern conversion, cold contact with conference speakers; working openly lets you point candidates to the Chromium wiki; hiring people who have an interest in security with expertise in other areas (an engineer with an SRE background); diversity of experience is a key factor (BSRS p.484). The 2012 principles remain relevant in 2020 (BSRS p.484).

### Security is a team responsibility (BSRS p.484-485)
- [from source] Security as a core product principle and everyone's responsibility; the security team will never own all of Chrome's security, so it embeds awareness and good practices in daily habits; design conventions make the easy, fast, well-lit path also the secure path; all engineers, including security ones, fix bugs and write code (if they only find and report, they lose touch with how hard it is to write bug-free code and create an "us versus them" mentality); fuzzing infrastructure (started on a machine under an engineer's desk; in 2019 it supports fuzzing at Google and in the world, with ClusterFuzz opened in early 2019), annual fuzzing contests with prizes and tutorials; safe base libraries (e.g., a safe numerics library) so that the default is the secure one; peer bonuses and positive feedback for those who model good practices; if the organization does not have security as a core value and shared responsibility, a more fundamental reflection is needed (BSRS p.485).

### Helping users browse safely (BSRS p.486)
- [from source] Effective security must not depend on user expertise; Chrome seeks nearly invisible security: transparent updates, secure defaults, making the safe decision the easy one; usable security problems (social engineering, phishing, effectiveness of warnings); strategic hiring of a usable security specialist (research scientist; justified by academic expertise and diverse perspectives), partnership with UX (previously at times in conflict) and later UX designers and researchers; security experts, because they understand systems too well, become blind to users' challenges (BSRS p.486).

### Speed matters (BSRS p.486-487)
- [from source] User security depends on detecting flaws and delivering fixes before they are exploited; fast, automatic updates are one of the main security features; partnership with release technical program managers (TPMs) (they measure crashes, ensure high-priority bug fixes, release incrementally, brake when it is going too fast, speed up security and reliability releases); the Pwn2Own and Pwnium contests served as forcing functions to prove the ability to ship critical fixes in under 24 hours (rarely needed thanks to defense in depth) (BSRS p.486-487).

### Design for defense in depth (BSRS p.487-488)
- [from source] Bugs are inevitable (C++ and browser complexity); continuous investment in exploit mitigation and an architecture without single points of failure; a living component diagram colored by risk for reasoning about the layers of defense; sandboxing: launch with a multi-process architecture and sandboxed renderers (prevent a malicious site from taking over the computer); changing landscape (sensitive data in the cloud): cross-site data theft as important as compromising the machine; in 2012 Site Isolation (isolating individual sites) was started, estimated at one year and took more than five times longer; the team continuously communicated the defense-in-depth motivation, progress, and reasons for the delay, and showed positive impact on code health; Site Isolation partially mitigates speculative execution vulnerabilities (discovered in 2018) (BSRS p.487). Defense-in-depth work rarely has user-visible change: leadership must manage, recognize, and invest proactively (BSRS p.488).

### Transparency and community engagement (BSRS p.488)
- [from source] Transparency as a core value: do not minimize the impact or bury vulnerabilities in silent fixes; it publishes how it handles security issues; discloses all vulnerabilities fixed in Chrome and dependencies (internal or external) and, whenever possible, lists each fixed security issue in the release notes; public quarterly summaries and an external mailing list; VRP and conference sponsorship; attribution and rewards; an annual offsite that connects the Chrome team with the central and embedded teams (e.g., Android); 20% time of Google security enthusiasts on Chrome and vice versa; collaboration with academics; a multi-year HTTPS adoption effort (Ch.7) as an example of ecosystem change (BSRS p.488).

### Conclusion (BSRS p.488-489)
- [from source] Security as a core principle from early on, investment scaled with the team and user base; the formal team came a year after launch; mission and principles; focus; security as a team responsibility, transparency, and external engagement created a security-centered culture; innovating at speed enabled agility; defense in depth protects against isolated bugs and new attacks; human aspects (from user experience to hiring) broadened understanding; willingness to learn from mistakes to make the default path the secure one (BSRS p.488-489).

### DATED
- [DATED] Description of the state of Chrome in 2020 (ClusterFuzz opened in 2019, VRP, Site Isolation, Pwn2Own/Pwnium); VRP and disclosure practices have evolved; "ca. 2020".

### What the book does NOT cover in this chapter
- It does not generalize into a formal organizational model (RACI) or product security metrics; the chapter is a narrative case.

## Ch. 20 — Understanding roles and responsibilities (BSRS p.491-506)

### Premise (BSRS p.491-492)
- [from source] The chapter challenges the myth that security is only for specialists: everyone is responsible, although sometimes a specialist is needed; security and reliability depend on people and processes; two questions: who is responsible and how the efforts integrate into the organization (the answer depends on goals and culture) (BSRS p.491). Reliability roles (SRE) are in other sources (SRE book and workbook); this chapter focuses on security (BSRS p.492) [route to sre-practices-expert].

### Who is responsible for security and reliability? (BSRS p.492-494)
- [from source] They must be integrated into the systems lifecycle: everyone's responsibility (developers, SREs, security engineers, test engineers, tech leads, managers, project managers, tech writers, executives); if delegated to an isolated team that cannot demand changes from other teams, the same flaws repeat and the task becomes "Sisyphean"; non-functional requirements (Ch.4) become the organization's focus throughout the lifecycle; car analogy: almost every component incorporates safety and reliability and everyone who builds it does that work (BSRS p.492).
- [from source] The role of specialists: in one view, engineers focus on the core functionality; the security engineer looks at the attacker's perspective and the reliability engineer understands dependency chains and metrics that lead to satisfied customers and meeting the SLA; the roles must work together, not in isolation; specialists provide nuanced judgment and must be integrated into the lifecycle: security design review at the start, continuous audits against the specifications, and testing by an independent person (BSRS p.492-493). Risk assessment: they help decide when there was not time for protections or graceful degradation before a launch (will adversaries be able to attack? does the global system go down if traffic from one country is larger than expected? probability? are there temporary mitigations?) (BSRS p.493).
- [from source] Security specialists should implement specific technologies that require specialized knowledge (cryptography: "don't roll your own crypto"; custom AAA; new secure frameworks); reliability engineers are best positioned for centralized infrastructure and organizational automation (platforms); both should create tailored practices, policies, and training, a brain trust, and awareness (Ch.21), such as SRE and security educational programs for new hires at Google and self-study courses (BSRS p.494).

### Understanding security expertise (BSRS p.494-496)
- [from source] Hiring security professionals is hard: medical analogy (generalist and specialists); know the skills your organization needs; small organizations (startup, open source project) can make do with a generalist; as it grows, specialization increases (BSRS p.494-495). Table 20-1: Google milestones and the expertise needed (Google Search 1998: protection of query logs, DoS, network and systems security; AdWords 2000: data, network, systems, application, compliance and audit, anti-fraud, privacy, DoS, insider risk, financial data, regulation, complex web apps, identity, account abuse; Blogger 2003: content abuse, DoS, platform abuse; Gmail 2004: privacy, cryptography, anti-spam, anti-abuse, incident response, insider risk, enterprise security, protection of highly sensitive content, threats from highly capable attackers, identity systems, account abuse) (BSRS p.495).
- [from source] Certifications and academia: they indicate interest and the ability to learn key concepts, but may not attest to aptitude for your context; balanced approach (practical experience, certifications, and interest); credentialed professionals struggling to apply the knowledge; beginner candidates or those from other specialties can add value quickly; general academic programs, domain-specific (PhD), and mixed (public policy, law, privacy); consider the context (first hire experienced, then beginners with mentoring; a niche problem such as self-driving car security may justify a recent PhD) (BSRS p.495-496).

### Integrating security into the organization (BSRS p.496-498)
- [from source] When to start is more art than science; the sooner the better; observed triggers: handling personal data (sensitive logs, financial, health data, email); need for very secure environments or custom technologies; regulations that require adherence to a standard or audit (Sarbanes-Oxley, PCI DSS, GDPR); contractual requirements (breach notification and minimum standards); during or after a compromise (ideally before); reaction to a compromise at an industry peer; start before all these conditions (example: new product with online payments: consider a specialized vendor, whose vetting takes time; a vendor that does not integrate securely leads to fines, loss of trust, and rework; several organizations cease to exist after incidents, such as Code Spaces and American Medical Collection Agency; contract with additional requirements: implement before signing) (BSRS p.496-497).
- [from source] Cost: (1) security must be balanced against the other requirements (shutting down datacenters would leave Google nearly 100% secure and with no customers; availability is a core security principle); understand what the business requires to operate and profit; (2) security is everyone's responsibility: distribute the cost (example: six products with 20 firewalls each: instead of the central team maintaining 120 firewalls, operate an automated configuration system that accepts, validates, approves, and pushes changes proposed by the product teams, scaling and reducing errors) (BSRS p.497-498). Non-technical areas too: boards of directors examine security and privacy; lawsuits such as the shareholders' suit against Yahoo! in 2017; treat security as a continuous risk assessment process, embedded in the development cycle (BSRS p.498).

### Embedding specialists and security teams (BSRS p.498-501)
- [from source] Configurations range from specialists fully embedded in product teams (Ch.19) to fully centralized teams; Google's central one is a hybrid; CISOs and security leaders report to various executives (CEO, CFO, CIO, COO, legal, VP of engineering, CSO); there is no right or wrong configuration; it may not suit financial firms and utilities (BSRS p.498-499).
- [from source] Example at Google: a central security organization as a peer of product engineering, led by a VP of engineering: an ally of engineering with enough independence to raise problems and resolve disputes without conflict of interest (similar to SRE with separate reporting chains); without that: a team that does not raise serious issues because launches are prioritized, seen as a blocking gate to be bypassed by silent launches, or slow for lack of documentation and code access; standard processes (tickets) for requests (BSRS p.499). Security champion: a member of each product engineering group who facilitates collaboration with the central team; initially senior engineers in good standing with an interest in security, who become technical leads of initiatives; with more complex teams, a senior decision maker (director or VP) who decides (launches vs. fixes), obtains resources, and resolves conflicts; combine process and responsibilities (the central team does design reviews and audits, sets policies and standards, builds secure frameworks and common infrastructure such as least privilege; champions decide how the controls work in the team and drive the implementation) with a short communication loop (charters, meetings, lists, chat) (BSRS p.499-500). Decentralized teams for complex products (Android, Chrome) with end-to-end responsibility, their own standards and review process (e.g., hardening the Android media stack); the security team must be open and accessible, with timely and consistent feedback (BSRS p.500).
- [from source] Google's Smart System for Intake: generic ticket queues generate back-and-forth; it uses a dynamic questionnaire that asks common security questions, warns and educates about risky decisions (memory-safe language, validated template framework, sensitive data, critical system), creates a structured ticket routed to the right team; allows skipping and using "other" (explicitly for one-off and urgent requests); if many skip, the system needs adjustments; benefits: self-service and self-education, time for productive collaboration, speed, more tickets closed per week, and better quality of information (BSRS p.500-501).

### Special teams: Blue and Red (BSRS p.501-504)
- [from source] Colors (scheme derived from the US military): Blue Teams assess and harden software and infrastructure, and handle detection, containment, and recovery; anyone who works on defense, including those who build secure and reliable systems; Red Teams run end-to-end offensive exercises simulating realistic adversaries, focusing on: a specific objective (e.g., exfiltrate customer account data), surveillance (test detection of reconnaissance), and targeted attacks (demonstrate the feasibility of exploiting supposedly theoretical problems to decide which deserve defense) (BSRS p.501-502). Before starting: get buy-in (legal and executives), define limits (do not access customer data or disrupt production; use approximations such as test accounts), balancing realism and the comfort of partner teams; adversaries will not respect limits, so watch for unprotected areas (BSRS p.502). Some Red Teams share plans with Blue; a formal Purple Team bridges them, useful for many exercises, to move fast or to distribute Red activity among product engineers, and inspires looking where you otherwise would not; engineers who maintain systems know the weaknesses (BSRS p.502-503).
- [from source] Detecting Red Teams: if they do not share information, establish a de-escalation protocol for when Blue detects Red (otherwise there are improper notifications to executives, legal, and regulators); Red Teams are not vulnerability scanning teams (predictable, automatable weaknesses) nor penetration testing teams (a large set of vulnerabilities in a narrower scope, usually days); Red engagements are goal-oriented, with broad scope, last weeks, and cross product and infrastructure boundaries; good Red Teams often reach objectives without being detected; use the result in a blameless way to understand complex systems and trust boundaries; Red Teams reinforce threat models and defenses (BSRS p.503). Limits: they do not perfectly mirror external attackers (especially insiders, who know the systems); cannot be done often enough for statistical metrics or a real-time view; they serve to find rare edge cases; useful for teaching the adversarial mindset to those who design and maintain systems (embedding them in the attack team on a small project) and for a roadmap of risk reductions (BSRS p.504).

### External researchers and VRP (BSRS p.504-506)
- [from source] VRPs (bug bounty) pay for responsible disclosure (not always in cash; Google's first VRP, in 2006, gave a T-shirt and thanks); before starting, cover the basics with reviews and scanning, otherwise you will pay for bugs your team would find (and several researchers may report the same one) (BSRS p.504-505). Steps: (1) assess readiness (scope, payment tiers, and funds); (2) decide between an in-house program or a specialized company; (3) process for intake, triage, investigation, validation, follow-up, and fixes (about 40 hours per serious issue, excluding the fix); (4) payment process with legal and finance (researchers worldwide); (5) launch, learn, and iterate (BSRS p.505). Challenges: the "firehose" of reports (understand the level of response needed), poor report quality (misconfigured browsers), language barriers (translation), and disclosure guidelines without consensus (when to disclose, deadline for fixing, what to reward; readings: Google blog, Project Zero, Oulu group, ISO); address issues in a timely manner and be aware they may have been exploited before (a breach to handle) (BSRS p.505-506).
- [from source] Conclusion: security and reliability are born from the quality or absence of processes and practices; people are the main driver; collaboration across roles, departments, and cultures; making everyone responsible is the best defense (BSRS p.506).
